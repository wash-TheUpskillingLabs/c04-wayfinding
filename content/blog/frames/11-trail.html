The second frame is a whole place: a marked trail, the people who walk it, the people who help them along it, and the people who keep it. It holds the people and the open-source work too, because it starts from where the quilt differs.

The Appalachian Trail

If we treat this as a problem of the Appalachian Trail, then one marked path runs the whole way and everyone hikes their own hike, for a day, a section or the whole thing, at any pace and over any number of years; people take a trail name and fall in with a trail family; trail angels and trail towns help whoever comes through; local clubs keep every stretch, with a named overseer for each; and people who finished come back to keep it, to help on it, and to gather again.

The walkers. More than 3 million people walk some part of the trail each year, for a day, a week or a season.52, 53 A few thousand set out to walk all of it in a year, and about one in three finish.54 Anyone who finishes, in one go or over many years, can report it and be counted a “2,000-miler.” The Conservancy takes their word for it, recognizes section hikers equally with thru-hikers, and doesn’t ask about the order, the direction, the speed, or whether they carried a pack.55 More than 20,000 completions have been reported since 1936.56 Hikers usually take a trail name, chosen by themselves or given by others on the trail, and anyone may read or write in the logbooks at shelters and visitor centers.57 “Hike your own hike” is the trail’s reminder that there’s no one right way, and the trail family people fall in with often keeps in touch long after.58

The help along the way. Trail angels give hikers direct kindness and generosity, which hikers call trail magic.59 The Conservancy and the long-distance hikers’ association add that the most essential service anyone can give is to volunteer to maintain the trail.60 Fifty-six towns are designated A.T. Communities, and local businesses that help hikers can sign on as supporters.61, 62 Ridgerunners look after the busiest stretches and advise hikers, and most of them are former thru-hikers.53 Some who were helped come back to help: one couple now camps each year at a gap in Georgia to be trail angels themselves.63 Near the middle, in Harpers Ferry, West Virginia, hikers stop at the Conservancy’s headquarters, the trail’s “psychological halfway point.” They have their photo taken, get a number and log their travels, and visitors can look through albums of these photos going back to the 1970s.64, 65

The keepers. The trail is kept open mostly by volunteers: in 2023, 4,730 of them gave 170,147 hours.66 About 30 local clubs each maintain an assigned section, and the Appalachian Trail Conservancy, whose small paid staff works mainly as their support, holds them together.67, 68 This region has its own. The Potomac Appalachian Trail Club formed in 1927 to build a key section, and today it cares for about 1,200 miles of trail.69 Its overseers each take ownership of a stretch, and district managers recruit, train and support them. Crews take on the larger projects.70, 71 One small standard holds the whole trail together: a white blaze two inches wide and six inches tall, about 165,000 of them, kept up by the clubs. Clubs may choose between two ways of marking a turn and no others, and an individual maintainer can’t change the marking alone.72, 73 Crews like the Konnarock Crew run sessions of three to eight days. No experience is needed, experienced leaders teach, and crew work has led some volunteers to jobs with the Conservancy.74, 75 Problems come back through the logbook in every shelter, where hikers write about the state of the trail, and through trail-condition reports the Conservancy gathers from the ground and shares.76, 77 One club’s newsletter logs who did what work, where and when, and clubs report each year’s volunteers, work and hours to the Conservancy.78, 79

After the hike. Every May, Damascus, Virginia, a town of about 700, swells to an estimated 25,000 people for Trail Days, a reunion of hikers past and present.80, 81 Each fall the Appalachian Long Distance Hikers Association, begun in 1983 as an off-trail family of hikers and friends of the trail, holds a Gathering. It’s free for people who finished that year, a way to ease back in and see trail friends again, and it ends with a work trip to give back.82, 83

Where it came from. Benton MacKaye proposed the trail in 1921 as “a project in regional planning,” not only a path. Along it he wanted shelter camps, community groups and food and farm camps, with room for study, summer schools and field courses.84, 85 The community camps never came, but the trail built a civil society of its own: the clubs, the trail towns and the hikers’ customs.85

It answers where the quilt differs. A trail is never finished: blazes fade, storms bring trees down, and the overseer keeps it after the season. Most hikers never meet the people who keep their trail open, which is open source’s problem too, and the clubs’ answer is a work log and a yearly count. Sections depend on each other, so one closure reroutes everyone. The work is spread out, each overseer on their own schedule, while work trips bring people together. Changes to the standard go through the clubs. A small core keeps the trail, and millions come for a day. The first threshold is designed: “no experience needed.” And it’s held the way this region holds OLOS: local clubs keep sections of one trail to one standard, and a national body keeps the standard and carries the whole.

It holds all four paradoxes. Seeing the route, making your own: the blazes are the same everywhere, everyone hikes their own hike, and the 2,000-miler counts every way of finishing the same. Seen as yourself, part of a team: a trail name given by the people around you, a trail family, and a stretch that’s yours on a trail that’s everyone’s. A person at every crossing: angels, trail towns, ridgerunners and crew leaders, many of them former hikers, so help doesn’t all come out of organizers’ hours. When the Cycle ends: a hike ends at Katahdin or Springer Mountain, and Trail Days, the Gathering and the clubs go on, with people coming back as angels and keepers.

It holds the other frames, too. The Camino is the walk without the keepers: the marked way and the record. The ski resort is the trail map. The CrossFit box is everyone on the same trail at their own pace. The film credits and the relay are the work log, the roster and the sections handed on. The train station is Harpers Ferry. The doula is the trail angel and the ridgerunner, and the tailor’s shop is the crew leader beside a newcomer. The harbor is the club and the Gathering, and parkrun is Trail Days every May. From the quilt we’d keep two things the trail lacks. One is the signature: put each keeper’s name where users can see it, not only in the club newsletter. The other is the gift: the quilt goes to one person at a threshold in their life.

Where it differs from our work. Walking the whole trail takes months of full-time walking, and most who try don’t finish. The Labs happens in evenings, and it can’t lose two in three. For decades, long-distance hiking here was mostly white and mostly people with time to spare, and The Labs is for people who don’t have that.86 A trail can’t be copied and sent back the way code can. It changes slowly, and a relocation can take years; software changes weekly. And the trail is rooted in one place, while The Labs moves between libraries.

What it opens. One blaze: a small shared mark for the way on at every threshold in OLOS, the same in every region, with a short list of allowed local variations. Hike your own hike, counted the same: a record that counts every way through The Labs, a whole Cycle in a season or one evening at a time, in any order, on the member’s word, like the 2,000-miler. An album at the halfway point: at a threshold everyone passes, anyone who wants to adds a photo and a line, and anyone can look back through it. Trail angels and trail towns: past members who show up at the hard stretches, and libraries and local businesses that sign on as places that welcome Upskillers. Sections and overseers: every stretch of the route, whether a threshold, a page or a notice, has a named keeper who walks it a few times a season, which answers “who keeps it in January?” by design. Work trips and crews: open labs as Saturday work trips, with small, real fixes and newcomers beside overseers, and each Cycle as a crew that takes on one big project and disbands while the club stays. A register at every threshold, and a work log that credits the unseen work. And a Trail Days: one weekend a year when everyone who has been through a Cycle comes back.

To try it by Nov 13, give three thresholds on the journey map each a named overseer and a simple register, and at one open lab keep an album where anyone who has crossed a threshold can add a photo and a line, if they want to. Then ask three people whether they could tell who keeps the way, whether they’d add to the album, and whether they’d take a stretch themselves.

Who would have to adopt it: regional organizers, as district managers; alumni, as overseers and angels; libraries and local businesses, as trail towns; national OLOS, as the keeper of the standard; and the people using it, who write in the register.

Watch for a record that only counts finishing. Most people never walk the whole trail, so a Labs record has to count every stretch. Watch for help that does harm, too: the Conservancy publishes guidance for trail angels because trail feeds can crowd and litter the trail.60 Watch for overseers burning out alone on a stretch nobody visits, an orphaned section with no keeper, a standard that hardens until nothing can change, and unseen work staying unseen. And it can read as outdoorsy, or as a thing for people with months to spare: the frame is the whole place, not the hike.

To a member it would feel like this: I can find my way because someone marked it, I go at my own pace, people along the way know my name, and one day I might be the one who keeps a stretch.
