For each need: how a person might say it, what the research says, what it feels like to go without it, how the field answers it, and how it shows up inside The Labs.

Mattering

“I want someone to see what I can do, and for it to count.”

Psychologists have studied mattering since 1981: the sense that other people notice you, think you’re important and rely on you. In its first studies, with four samples of teenagers, it predicted less depression even after self-esteem was taken into account. It has two halves, feeling valued and adding value, and people need both.14, 15

Without it, you’re overlooked: screened out by a degree you don’t have, or summed up by a number that can’t show what you added. The field answers this need more than any other; 27 of the 50 organizations reach for it, almost always with something countable: a credential, a wage, a placement. Organizations feel it too. Libraries want federal law to name them as workforce institutions, and every program needs a funder to see its results.3 Inside The Labs, only the problem owner raised it: employers judge us against multi-million-dollar institutions, and writing things down doesn’t count as a contribution.6

Coherence

“I need this to make sense: where I am, what’s next, and that I can handle it.”

The medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky called this a sense of coherence. He built the idea from interviews with people who had come through severe hardship, and found three parts: the world is understandable, you have what you need to manage it, and it’s worth the effort. Across hundreds of studies since, a stronger sense of coherence goes with better mental health and quality of life.16

Without it, you’re guessing, and the guessing wears you out. Fourteen of the 50 reach for it, nearly all from above: a state inventory of resources, a regional platform with AI agents, a resource guide.3 Inside, all three voices feel it, and from the ground: what’s next, what’s expected of me right now, how my own work becomes part of a team.4–6

Not Alone

“I don’t want to face the hard part by myself.”

Support from other people protects us most when things are hardest; researchers call this buffering. Brain studies make it concrete: under threat, holding a familiar person’s hand quiets the brain’s alarm. James Coan’s social baseline theory reads that the other way around. The brain expects company, and being alone is what adds the extra work.17

Without it, the hard part is the part you face alone. Seventeen of the 50 reach for it. In public systems the answer is a paid guide, like a case manager, a coach or a librarian trained to help, and everyone is trying to stretch that one person further: AI to free up advisors, some of whom carry more than 400 students; about 50 paid counseling slots for about 500 federal workers. A few make the group the answer instead, like Climb Hire’s pods and Civic Tech DC’s recurring room.3 Inside, all three voices feel it. Alumni said the most valuable part of The Labs was the people.4 The problem owner said nobody’s job is to help them find their way.6

Belonging

“I want a place, and people, that are still mine when this ends.”

In 1995 Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed the evidence and called the need to belong a fundamental human motivation. It takes two things together: frequent, good time with the same people, and a bond that lasts. A changing cast of strangers doesn’t satisfy it, and people form bonds readily and resist letting them end.18

Without it, the end of a program feels like being dropped. Eighteen of the 50 reach for it, and they know endings well. Byte Back closed after 27 years despite good results. The Clark Foundation spent itself out. Code for America ended its volunteer network. Money for digital navigators was cut in 2025. What lasted kept a recurring room and a name, like Civic Tech DC, or a way back in for alumni, like Per Scholas.3 Inside, all three voices feel it: leaving the Cycle means leaving The Labs, yet 7 of 11 alumni are still in touch with their team, just as Baumeister and Leary would predict.4, 6

Believing I Can

“I want to believe I can become someone new, and that I’m allowed to.”

Albert Bandura called the belief that you can do a particular thing self-efficacy. It grows most from doing the thing and succeeding, next from watching someone like you do it, and then from being told, by someone you trust, that you can.20 A review of 47 job-search programs found they helped people find work only when they built motivation, self-belief included, as well as skills.21 And Herminia Ibarra, studying people who changed careers, found they didn’t think their way into a new self. They tried on possible selves, often by finding someone already living the life they wanted.22

Without it, you hold back, even when you have the skills. This is the quietest need on the map: 9 of the 50. The field mostly treats a change of career as paperwork, with a certificate, a résumé translated out of federal format or a nudge toward entrepreneurship. Its own evidence says it’s about belief. Byte Back found its participants lacked confidence, not computers. Of the workers Opportunity@Work calls STARs, skilled through alternative routes, only 15 in 100 call themselves that.3 Inside, the problem owner sees it too: people aren’t sure they’re allowed to go off and do it on their own.6
