This is desk research, and it shows. Dorst finds themes in what people live through. We read what organizations publish, and named needs for people we haven’t met. Every mark in the field comes from a website, a report or testimony, not from a person telling us what they need. The words in quotation marks above are ours, written the way we think a person would say them. They’re a guess until someone says them back.

That tilts the map. Public pages are written to be seen by funders and councils, so Mattering is probably louder here than it is among the people those organizations serve. Four of the files are thin, and two organizations carry no theme at all in what we found.

Inside, we have three voices: a survey, a record and a conversation. Twelve alumni answered the survey, and 11 of them had joined a team; the people who came once and left aren’t in it. Nobody inside has yet been asked what they need.

The research we used to name the needs mostly studied individuals, often students or couples. Reading it into what organizations publish is our inference. It tells us what to look for, not what people here feel.

And one reader made the marks. Someone else would mark some rows differently, which is why every station shows its evidence.

The fastest way to make this better is the interview our README already asks for: “What would a good year here look like for you? Why does that matter? And why does that matter?” Keep asking why, and you reach the need. Then use their words, not ours, for the names on this map.
