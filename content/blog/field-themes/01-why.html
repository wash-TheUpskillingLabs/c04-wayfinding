On Nov 7 the pod wrote its paradox, and each builder left with a frame. This week every frame gets a quick prototype and three real people to try it. On Nov 14 the room votes on proposals.

Before that, we went back to a step we hurried past. Kees Dorst’s method has nine steps, and the fifth is themes: “investigating the themes that emerge in the broader field.”1

A theme is what people need underneath what they ask for. Ask an organization what it wants and you get a solution: a platform, a credential, a job fair. Ask how, and you get a scenario. Ask why, and you get a goal. Keep asking, and you reach something a person lives through, personal and universal at once, like belonging, trust or feeling in control. Dorst’s colleagues find themes by looking for what keeps coming back across people who otherwise have little in common.2

That’s where a frame gets its strength. A frame built on a real human need has ground to stand on; a frame built on a clever comparison doesn’t. As our README puts it, frames come from the themes, not from staring at the paradox.1

Behind every organization on our field map are people: a federal worker starting over after the cuts, a librarian asked to help someone find work, an advisor with too many people to see, an adult who walked into a program and didn’t know where to go next. So we read the 50 organizations for the person underneath.3 What is each one trying to give someone, and what does that person need? Then we set three voices from inside The Labs beside them: the alumni survey, the organizers’ account and the problem owner.4–6 Last, we named each need with the term psychologists use for it, so the names mean something precise and the pod can build on what’s already known.

This is Wayfinding’s work because the field is the country around the route. It’s where people come from on the way into The Labs, and where they go on the way out. If The Labs meets people somewhere the field can’t see them, they get lost at the border.
