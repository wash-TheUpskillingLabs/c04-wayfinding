The frames the pod wrote on Nov 7 aren’t in the README yet, so we can’t test them here. Instead, here are four frames these needs suggest, each built at an interchange on the map and written in the pod’s form, with what it would feel like to the person on the other end. Take one, or hold yours up against them.

Vouching

If we treat this as a problem of vouching, then recognition comes from the people who built beside you, written where the field can read it.

It’s built where Mattering meets Support, at seven organizations on the map. A vouch gives both halves of mattering at once: someone noticed, and what you added is on the record. Elsewhere there are signs it works. Workers skilled through alternative routes moved up more often inside employer networks committed to reading their evidence, 52 in 100 against 39 in the market at large, though those employers may have joined because they already hired such workers. And employers read contributions to other people’s open-source projects as endorsements.7, 8 It moves the paradox by making team-made work visible without freezing it into a certificate. To prototype it, after a group day each teammate vouches for one specific thing another did, on that person’s record. Show it to three alumni and one person who hires.

To a member it would feel like this: someone I built with said what I did, somewhere it counts.

The Station Agent

If we treat this as a problem of a station with an agent at the gate, then every step on the route shows where you are and names the person to ask about the next one.

It’s built where Coherence meets Support, which all three voices inside feel. It offers what Antonovsky found people need, a world that makes sense, along with the company Coan found the brain expects. The field’s version is the digital navigator, a trusted person at the first threshold; our alumni asked for moderators to step in when teams got stuck.3, 4 It moves the paradox by pairing a map that holds still with a person who can adapt. To prototype it, put a named alum or moderator on each step of the journey map, and ask three people at three different steps to find their next one.

To a member it would feel like this: I always know where I am, and who to ask.

Home Port

If we treat this as a problem of a home port, then a Cycle is one voyage, and a crew keeps its berth between voyages.

It’s built where Support meets Belonging, at seven organizations and in all three voices inside. Civic Tech DC kept meeting after its national hub let it go. Recurse Center’s alumni “never graduate.” The Indivisible groups that lasted met monthly and replaced leaders as they left.3, 11, 12 It meets Baumeister and Leary’s two conditions directly: the standing night is the frequent contact, and the berth is the bond that lasts. Attachment research adds a reason it helps people move on, not just stay: adults explore more when they have a secure base that is there when needed, doesn’t interfere and encourages them.23 It moves the paradox by giving people something stable to come back to, so the Cycle can stay adaptive. To prototype it, make a standing night and a page where past teams keep their place and newcomers can visit, and ask three alumni whether they’d come.

To a member it would feel like this: when the Cycle ends, I still have somewhere to go, and people who expect me.

Rite of Passage

If we treat this as a problem of a rite of passage, then each threshold is a crossing that is named, witnessed and remembered.

It’s built on Becoming, the quietest line on the map, and maybe the need that’s been missing. A rite uses three of Bandura’s sources of self-belief at once: the person’s own success, seen by others like them, and named out loud by people they trust. The anthropologist Arnold van Gennep found the same three stages in rites of passage across many societies: leaving an old role, a time in between, and being welcomed into the new one.13 The middle stage is called liminal, from the Latin for threshold. To prototype it, set aside a short, opt-in moment at a group day when a team names a crossing one of its members made, like a first build shipped or a first training taught, and ask three people what it changed. A rite marks a crossing. It never ranks the people who haven’t crossed yet.

To a member it would feel like this: someone marked the moment I became a builder.

A Test for Any Frame

  • Which human need is it built from? Name it, say it the way a person would, and say what research stands behind it. If you can’t, it’s a comparison, not a frame.
  • Do unlike organizations reach for that need, and does someone inside The Labs feel it? Check the map.
  • Which side of the paradox gives? Say what moves.
  • Does it open many things to build, or only one? Dorst’s colleagues call this fruitfulness.
  • Can three real people try it by Nov 13, and tell you how it felt?
