# Case: Cooperative Extension, 4-H and Master Gardener volunteer leader systems

**Kind:** paradigmatic (century-old national → state → county → club volunteer structure) with documented attrition
**Bears on:** facilitators, pre-cycle, post-cycle, activation-mobilization
**Logged:** 2026-11-05 (desk research, sources below)

## 1. What it is
U.S. land-grant universities run Cooperative Extension through county offices. Paid county agents recruit, train and support volunteers who run local 4-H clubs (youth) and Master Gardener programs (adults trained in horticulture who then owe volunteer hours).

## 2. Where it came from
Public universities with USDA partnership. This file draws on peer-reviewed *Journal of Extension* articles (1999–2018), USDA's National Framework for 4-H Volunteerism (2008), a Purdue Extension publication on Master Gardener retention (c. 2019), and an Oregon State study in the *International Journal of Volunteer Administration* (2012).

## 3. When
Programs date to the early 20th century; sources here run 1999–2019.

## 4. What it says

**Structure (hub and spoke)**
- USDA's 2008 framework describes leadership at national, state and county/local levels and calls for clarifying roles among them; it also calls for training and using "middle management" volunteers to widen reach ([USDA, 2008](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20120913082407/http://www.national4-hheadquarters.gov/comm/Framework_4-H_Volunteerism082508.pdf)).
- In one Oregon county (Deschutes, c. 2003): about 1,100 4-H youth, 300 volunteers and 130+ project clubs, coordinated by a county 4-H agent described as central to recruiting and retaining volunteers ([Journal of Extension, 2003](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2003august/rb5.php)).

**Pre (why volunteers start)**
- Among former Deschutes 4-H volunteers, top reasons for starting were making a difference for youth, satisfaction of helping, and having their own children in 4-H ([Journal of Extension, 2003](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2003august/rb5.php)).
- Mississippi Master Gardeners (n=233) rated learning more about horticulture as their top reason; the authors advised county directors to set clear expectations before training ([Journal of Extension, 2011](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2011october/rb1.php)).

**During (training and the first year)**
- Florida (Pinellas County) Master Gardener trainee drop-out was 26%, 17% and 27% in 1995–1997; after veteran volunteers were each assigned 2–4 trainees as mentors in 1998, drop-out was 2%. The 1998 class was about a third smaller than earlier ones ([Journal of Extension, 1999](https://archives.joe.org/joe/1999august/rb3.php)).
- In a sample of Indiana counties, an average of 70% of Master Gardener interns completed the 35 volunteer hours needed to certify, ranging from 50% to 90% by county ([Purdue Extension, c. 2019](https://ag.purdue.edu/department/extension/ppp/resources/ppp-publications/publications-archive/mobile/ppp-123/retaining-extension-master-gardener-volunteers-improving-communities-and-expanding-opportunities.html)).

**Post (why volunteers leave or stay)**
- Former Deschutes 4-H volunteers' top reasons for leaving were that their children were no longer in 4-H and that time demands rose; ratings of county Extension support were not a main reason ([Journal of Extension, 2003](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2003august/rb5.php)).
- Purdue authors list feeling unappreciated, losing interest to another cause, and wanting a new challenge as reasons Master Gardeners leave; five of eight county coordinators named local Master Gardener Association membership as a main reason people stayed ([Purdue Extension, c. 2019](https://ag.purdue.edu/department/extension/ppp/resources/ppp-publications/publications-archive/mobile/ppp-123/retaining-extension-master-gardener-volunteers-improving-communities-and-expanding-opportunities.html)).
- In Oregon (2008 survey, 781 responses), volunteers logging 25 or fewer hours a year reported less flexibility, responsibility and appreciation than higher-hour volunteers ([Langellotto-Rhodaback and Miller, 2012](https://www.ijova.org/docs/FE20128_Langellotto.pdf)).

**Youth-side attrition (context for the volunteer load)**
- National 4-H participation rose from 6.3 million (1996) to 7.0 million (2003), but club membership stayed nearly flat (1.54 to 1.57 million). Older studies found 40–50% of new members leaving after the first year (West Virginia 1983; southeast Kansas mid-1980s) ([Journal of Extension, 2005](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2005october/rb4.php)).

**Facilitators / activation-mobilization**
- USDA's framework notes a shift from long-term volunteering toward shorter, episodic engagement and says program design must adapt ([USDA, 2008](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20120913082407/http://www.national4-hheadquarters.gov/comm/Framework_4-H_Volunteerism082508.pdf)).
- University of Idaho 4-H created "facilitation coach" volunteers (a middle-management role with a signed position description) and six-week project sessions for short-term volunteers; one or two volunteers then served 10–15 youth at one county site instead of 2–3 per club. Coaches also mentored new families through their first year ([Journal of Extension, 2018](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2018october/iw7.php)).

## Failure modes seen
- **Life-stage exit:** Volunteers who joined because their child was in 4-H left when the child did ([Journal of Extension, 2003](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2003august/rb5.php)).
- **Training-to-service gap:** About 30% of Indiana interns, and up to half in some counties, did not complete the hours to certify after training ([Purdue Extension, c. 2019](https://ag.purdue.edu/department/extension/ppp/resources/ppp-publications/publications-archive/mobile/ppp-123/retaining-extension-master-gardener-volunteers-improving-communities-and-expanding-opportunities.html)).
- **Unmentored first year:** Florida trainee drop-out ran 17–27% before mentors were assigned ([Journal of Extension, 1999](https://archives.joe.org/joe/1999august/rb3.php)).
- **First-year cliff (youth):** 40–50% of new 4-H members left after year one in older state studies ([Journal of Extension, 2005](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2005october/rb4.php)).
- **Low-dose volunteers feel peripheral:** Low-hour Oregon volunteers reported less appreciation and responsibility ([Langellotto-Rhodaback and Miller, 2012](https://www.ijova.org/docs/FE20128_Langellotto.pdf)).

## 6. Hearsay and limits
- The Deschutes study surveyed only those it could reach (51 of 331 former volunteers), so reasons for leaving may be skewed. The Florida mentor result is one county, one year, with a smaller class; no control.
- National volunteer counts and trends were not found in sources opened here; USDA ES-237 data exist but were not reviewed.
- **Rival explanation:** Falling first-year drop-out in Florida may reflect the smaller 1998 class (more staff attention per trainee) rather than mentoring itself.
- Could confirm: state 4-H volunteer specialists; county Extension agents; USDA NIFA enrollment (ES-237) data.
