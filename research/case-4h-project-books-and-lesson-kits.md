# Case: 4-H "grab and go" lesson plans and The Carpentries' two-day workshop template, kits meant for a stranger to run

**Kind:** maximum variation (youth clubs with untrained volunteers vs. research-software workshops with certified volunteer instructors)
**Bears on:** facilitators, pre-cycle, interfaces
**Product part:** kits | facilitators
**Logged:** 2026-11-06 (desk research, sources below)

## 1. What it is
Two volunteer-delivered curricula that package a session so that someone who did not write it can run it: 4-H project lesson plans built on the "Do, Reflect, Apply" experiential model, and The Carpentries' standard two-day workshop with a shared lesson repository and an instructor-training course.

## 2. Where it came from
4-H is the US Cooperative Extension youth program, run by county educators and adult volunteers. The Carpentries grew from Software Carpentry (1998) and merged with Data Carpentry and Library Carpentry in 2018. Sources: two Journal of Extension research briefs (2013, 2020), Greg Wilson's "Software Carpentry: lessons learned" (arXiv, 2013–14), and Wikipedia's Carpentries entry (for dates only).

## 3. When
Pennsylvania 4-H survey published October 2013. Idaho lesson-plan trial published June 2020. Software Carpentry versions: 1998, 2004–05, 2010, 2012; Wilson's paper 2013–14. Carpentries merger 2018.

## 4. What it says

**4-H: the model is in the book, not in the volunteer.** Experiential learning (Do, Reflect, Apply) is built into National 4-H curricula and volunteer training materials. In a Pennsylvania survey (52 educators, 92 volunteers, usable response 34%), about 96% of educators knew the term but only 47.8% of volunteers did; 37.5% of volunteers were familiar with Do, Reflect, Apply; the last volunteer training had been about five years earlier ([Journal of Extension, October 2013](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2013october/rb7.php)). Educators perceived less reflection in clubs than volunteers self-reported, a gap of nearly 20 points ([Journal of Extension, 2013](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2013october/rb7.php)).

**4-H: a kit designed for the untrained.** University of Idaho wrote 60 animal-science lesson plans to be "grab and go," deliverable by volunteers with no subject training, at three developmental levels. A trial planned 64 clubs; 42 completed the pretest and 22 the posttest; of 665 enrolled youth, 135 completed both. Treatment scores rose 74.0% to 83.4% versus 80.0% to 81.9% in controls (p = .087), which the authors call preliminary ([Journal of Extension, June 2020](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2020june/rb3.php)). Interpretation: the kit appears to work when run, but two-thirds of clubs did not finish the measurement, which is itself a datum about volunteer-run delivery.

**Carpentries: what failed before the template.** A week-long course ran ten times over four years then wound down; materials released under Creative Commons found no institutional home; after a 2010 relaunch with 120+ video lessons, an online class ran three times, and only 5–10% of online starters finished, most already knowing the material; five eight-hour days were "more wearying than enlightening" ([Wilson, Software Carpentry: lessons learned](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)). From January 2012 the format became two-day workshops, host sites paying local costs; in 2013, 91 workshops reached over 4,300 scientists with more than 100 volunteer instructors; rooms were capped around 40 with roughly one helper per eight learners; a $20–40 registration fee cut no-shows from about a third to about 5% ([Wilson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)).

**Carpentries: training the stranger.** Instructor training was an online course of 2–4 hours a week for 12–14 weeks covering basic educational psychology and instructional design, which the author calls "necessarily very shallow"; the project had "not yet done the long-term follow-up" to prove impact ([Wilson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)). Today's standard workshop is two days, volunteer-led, with live coding, helpers and a shared collaborative document; instructors are certified through the training program ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Carpentries)).

**What The Labs cannot assume.** 4-H volunteers work under paid county Extension educators and a school-year calendar with children; Carpentries workshops are hosted by universities that pay local costs and recruit from a captive research population.

## Failure modes seen
- **Kit outlives the training.** Pennsylvania volunteers had last been trained ~5 years earlier and under half knew the model the curriculum assumes ([JoE 2013](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2013october/rb7.php)).
- **Self-report inflates delivery.** Volunteers reported more reflection than educators observed ([JoE 2013](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2013october/rb7.php)).
- **Evaluation attrition.** 42 of 64 planned clubs did the pretest and 22 the posttest ([JoE 2020](https://archives.joe.org/joe/2020june/rb3.php)).
- **Self-serve content without a room fails.** 5–10% completion for the online course versus a workshop model that filled rooms ([Wilson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)).
- **Too much per sitting.** Week-long courses exhausted learners; two days became the unit ([Wilson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)).
- **No long-term follow-up.** The author concedes impact after the workshop was unmeasured ([Wilson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.5448v2.pdf)).

## 5. What it tells us about the problem
*Left for the pod. Questions to answer:*
- 4-H put the pedagogy in the book and found the volunteers did not know its name. Does a kit for The Labs need the facilitator to understand the method, or only to follow it?
- The Carpentries fixed scale with a short, repeatable unit and a long instructor course. Which is The Labs' two-day workshop and which is its 14-week instructor course?
- A small fee cut no-shows from a third to 5%. What does a free library program have instead of a fee?
- Both programs lack data on what people did afterward. What would The Labs record at the end of a kit that these did not?

## 6. Hearsay and limits
The 4-H findings are from one state survey with a 34% response rate and one small Idaho trial. Wilson's paper is a founder's own account, with no external evaluation; the Wikipedia entry carries a "multiple issues" notice and was used only for dates. Rival explanation for 4-H volunteers' low recognition of "Do, Reflect, Apply": they may practise the model under other names, so the survey measured vocabulary, not delivery. Rival explanation for Carpentries' online-course failure: 2010–11 online courses generally completed at those rates, so the format rather than the kit may be at fault. The Carpentries' assessment team and the Journal of Extension authors could confirm. Blocked: the PMC copy of Wilson's paper (browser check); the arXiv preprint was used instead.
