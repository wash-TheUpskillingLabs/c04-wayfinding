# Theory: Modularity and pathways (structure versus cafeteria choice)

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, facilitators, interfaces
**Product part:** kits | cycle | pre-cycle | post-between | facilitators
**Logged:** 2026-11-06 (desk research, sources below)

A body of work from community college research arguing that institutions built as a "cafeteria" of courses and services lose students who have to assemble a route themselves, and that mapped "guided pathways" with defaults and milestones keep more of them. Beside it sit two older learning-design traditions that also impose sequence (mastery learning, learning progressions) and one that imposes structure without imposing sequence (Montessori). The evaluation record is mixed: the problem is well documented, the fix is only partly tested.

## Works

### Scott-Clayton, *The Shapeless River: Does a Lack of Structure Inhibit Students' Progress at Community Colleges?* (2011)
- **Link:** [CCRC Working Paper No. 25, Columbia University (PDF)](https://www.clark.edu/tlc/faculty-development/Scott-Clayton_Shapeless-River.pdf).
- **Core idea:** The "structure hypothesis": students persist and finish more in programs that are tightly and consciously structured, leave little room to drift off the completion path by whim or accident, and impose few bureaucratic hurdles. The paper broadens "structure" to include norms and nudges, drawing on choice-architecture research (context effects, default bias, non-alignable options, cognitive overload). Evidence: qualitative and survey work showing students with poor information about requirements and transfer; a matched comparison finding higher completion at more structured private two-year colleges (Stephan, Rosenbaum & Person 2009; small sample, selection possible); K-12 curriculum coherence studies; and MDRC's Kingsborough learning-communities evaluation, whose gains faded. It proposes fewer hassles, planning tools, cohorts, "prix-fixe" pathways and smart defaults that keep choice available. The author says the evidence on the problem is strong and the evidence on solutions limited.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *structure hypothesis*; *hassle factors* (bureaucratic friction that drops people); *prix-fixe pathway* (a default bundle you can opt out of); *smart default*; *non-alignable options* (choices you can't compare on the same axes).
- **Touches:** Describes the pre-pathways community college as a place where the route is the student's problem. Describes what it looks like when people drift: wrong courses, lost credits, stopping out. This is the paper that named the "cafeteria" problem before the 2015 book popularized the term.
- **Critiques and limits:** A review paper; its causal evidence is thin by its own account.

### Bailey, Jaggars & Jenkins, *Redesigning America's Community Colleges: A Clearer Path to Student Success* (2015)
- **Link:** [CCRC publication page](https://ccrc.tc.columbia.edu/publications/redesigning-americas-community-colleges.html) (Harvard University Press, April 2015; the book itself was not opened).
- **Core idea:** Raising completion requires fundamental institutional redesign, not add-on reforms: improving developmental education, instruction and supports is necessary but not sufficient. The "guided pathways" model that followed: map every program to a clear end (transfer or job), help students choose a field early via meta-majors and exploratory defaults, keep them on the map with monitoring and advising, and ensure learning along the way. (The model description is drawn from the CCRC evaluation summary below, not from the book page, which does not use the word "cafeteria.")
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *guided pathways*; *meta-major* (a broad field chosen first, narrowed later); *program map* (a default sequence with milestones); *early momentum metrics* (credits in term one, gateway courses passed in year one).
- **Touches:** Describes an attempt to replace "assemble your own route" with "pick a broad direction, follow a default, get flagged if you drift." Describes the four thresholds the model owns: choosing a path, entering it, staying on it, learning on it.
- **Critiques and limits:** See Rose (2016) and the evaluations below.

### Community College Research Center, *Two major evaluations of guided pathways* (summary PDF, 2024)
- **Link:** [PDF hosted by North Seattle College](https://northseattle.edu/sites/default/files/2024-04/two-major-evaluations-guided-pathways.pdf). Secondary: [UT Dana Center summary of the CCRC practitioner packet](https://www.utdanacenter.org/resources/what-we-know-about-guided-pathways) (confirms the packet exists; no findings on that page).
- **Core idea:** Two non-causal evaluations.
  - *AACC Pathways Project* (30 colleges, 17 states; cohorts 2012–13 through 2020–21; Gates-funded; CCRC research lead): most colleges improved on most early-momentum metrics, and the 11 "fully scaled" colleges improved more (12+ credits in term one, 24+ in year one, first-year college math and English). No advantage for fully scaled colleges on college-level course completion rate or on term-one to term-two persistence (partly attributed to COVID). Equity gaps by race/ethnicity remained even where all groups improved.
  - *NSF-funded study* (70 public two-year colleges in Ohio, Tennessee and Washington; data 2010–2020; 62 colleges and over 800,000 students in outcome analyses): Tennessee showed gains in first-year credits and fall-to-fall persistence, but other reforms ran at the same time, so the effect cannot be isolated. Ohio and Washington showed no consistently positive trends. Adopting at least five practices was not strongly associated with better early outcomes. Two practices on their own, mapping career-technical programs and mandatory educational planning, were slightly negatively correlated with persistence (small estimates). Corequisite math and math pathways were linked to more credits. Combining practices across areas was associated with larger gains than single practices. Scheduling for on-time completion was the least-scaled practice.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *fully scaled* vs *scaling* (degree of implementation); *practice bundle* (combinations outperform single practices).
- **Touches:** Describes the measured consequence of the design decision: early momentum rises where the model is fully implemented; completion and persistence effects are not established; a mandatory plan on its own did not help. Bears on whether a mapped route beats an open one, and on how much of the gain is the map versus the surrounding supports.
- **Critiques and limits:** Neither evaluation is causal. Washington's data cover only two years post-launch. Tennessee is confounded by concurrent reforms (including its free-tuition program, not named on this page; unverified).

### Rose, *Challenges Facing the Guided Pathways Model for Restructuring Two-Year Colleges* (2016)
- **Link:** [Inside Higher Ed essay, June 23, 2016 (PDF copy at Pasadena City College)](https://prod-omniweb.pasadena.edu/academics/support/pathways/symposium/docs/Rose-Challenges-Facing-the-Guided-Pathway.pdf).
- **Core idea:** A sympathetic critique by a UCLA education scholar. The model underplays politics (turf, power, ideology); some faculty believe some students don't belong; facilitation techniques are offered for resistance but there is little about what happens when they fail; treating complex student problems as a curriculum problem can miss individual circumstances. Pathways add coherence but will not necessarily speed progress for part-time, working and stopping-out adults, who need evening, weekend, online and competency-based options and off-hours advising. Many young adults need time to find their goals, and that developmental process extends beyond the curriculum. Students' material hardship (poverty, housing and food insecurity, health, immigration, justice involvement) requires services alongside pathways.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *exploratory student* (someone whose goal is not yet formed); *stop-out* (leaving with intent to return).
- **Touches:** Describes the people a mapped route serves least well: part-time adults and people who don't yet know what they want. Describes the gap between a structure on paper and the politics of running it with people who disagree about who belongs.
- **Critiques and limits:** An essay, not a study. It predates the evaluations above.

### Bloom's mastery learning and its meta-analyses (1968–1990; summary read via Nintil, 2017)
- **Link:** [Nintil, "Bloom's two sigma problem"](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/), a secondary review with citations to Bloom (1984), Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), Slavin (1987, 1990), VanLehn (2011), Jerrim et al. (2015). None of the primaries were opened this round.
- **Core idea:** Mastery learning: students do not advance until they reach a criterion (e.g., 80–90% on a unit test), with corrective loops. Bloom's 1984 claim that tutoring plus mastery raised performance by about two standard deviations is, per this review, an outlier; VanLehn (2011) found average tutoring effects of d = 0.79, and a replication of one two-sigma study found d = 0.52. Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990) pooled 108 mostly college-level studies and found about d = 0.5, larger for weaker students (0.61) than stronger (0.4), and far larger on experimenter-made tests (0.5) than standardized tests (0.08). Slavin's (1987) best-evidence synthesis kept a quarter of the studies, adjusted for time, and found a median near zero; his summary was that effects were "essentially nil on standardized achievement measures." A 2015 RCT of a UK Mathematics Mastery program (over 5,000 students) found d = 0.06.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *mastery criterion* (a gate you must pass to move on); *corrective loop*; *teacher-made vs standardized test gap* (gains show on tests aligned to the sequence, far less elsewhere).
- **Touches:** Describes a sequence imposed on learning by gating. Describes a replication record in which the famous number did not hold, the structured version helped weaker learners more, and gains mostly appeared on tests aligned with the sequence.
- **Critiques and limits:** Secondary source. The Kulik–Slavin dispute is unresolved; Guskey (2015) sides with the Kuliks. Mastery learning takes more time, which Slavin adjusted for and the Kuliks did not.

### Learning progressions (NRC 2007 onward; critiques summarized in CBE—Life Sciences Education, 2019)
- **Link:** ["Recent Progress in Learning Progressions Research," CBE—Life Sciences Education (2019)](https://lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.19-09-0181), summarizing Jin et al. (2019), Sikorski (2019) and Alonzo & Elby (2019). The NRC 2007 report was not opened.
- **Core idea:** Learning progressions are hypothetical, research-based models of how understanding in a domain develops over time, organized around a discipline's foundational ideas, with a lower and an upper anchor. The critiques: they are conjectures that need validation; individual students do not consistently fit a single level (Jin et al.); one group argues the models "do not adequately fit empirical data" (Alonzo & Elby, p. 3); vocabulary-dependent items mistake unfamiliar words for missing understanding; and fixed upper anchors clash with how professional science works and narrow possible routes (Sikorski proposes context-dependent "upper reaches"). "Toolbox" models drop the assumption of progress altogether. Alonzo & Elby still find them useful to teachers, who used item-level data more than levels.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *upper anchor* (the endpoint the sequence points at); *levels* (the rungs); *context-dependent upper reaches* (endpoints that vary by setting).
- **Touches:** Describes the tension in any pathway: a single mapped endpoint makes the route legible and makes people who don't fit the rungs look off-track. Describes how practitioners used the fine-grained data rather than the stages.
- **Critiques and limits:** This is K-12 and undergraduate science; the critiques are about assessment validity as much as about structure.

### Montessori as counterpoint: structured environment, self-paced sequence
- **Links:** [Randolph et al., *Montessori education's impact on academic and nonacademic outcomes: A systematic review*, Campbell Systematic Reviews 19(3), 2023, DOI 10.1002/cl2.1330](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:camsys:v:19:y:2023:i:3:n:e1330); [Hechinger Report, "Rival studies shed light on the merits of a Montessori education" (2017)](https://hechingerreport.org/rival-studies-shed-light-on-the-merits-of-a-montessori-education/) covering Lillard et al. 2017 (Frontiers in Psychology) and Ruijs 2017 (Economics of Education Review).
- **Core idea:** Montessori classrooms impose heavy structure on the environment (prepared materials, mixed ages, a trained adult) while letting the learner choose what to work on and when; the sequence is in the materials, not the timetable. The Campbell review (32 studies with baseline equivalence, 204 effect sizes, searches through February 2020) found Montessori ahead on general academic ability (g = 0.26), math (0.22), language (0.17), executive function (0.36), creativity (0.26) and students' inner experience of school (0.41, low-quality evidence), with larger effects in randomized than non-randomized studies, in preschool and elementary than secondary, and in private than public settings, and some sign of publication bias. The Hartford preschool lottery study (about 140 children, 70 in Montessori) found faster math and literacy growth over three years, strongest for lower-income children, and no clear advantage on executive function or creativity. The Dutch secondary lottery study (hundreds of students) found no difference in completion, grades, exams, motivation or independence.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *prepared environment* (structure in the room, not the schedule); *self-paced within a sequence*; *lottery study* (admission by chance, so winners and losers are comparable).
- **Touches:** Describes a maximally different case from guided pathways: the route is open but the space is designed so that choosing is cheap and every choice is a legitimate next step. Describes the null at secondary level, where implementation was uneven and some teachers untrained.
- **Critiques and limits:** Per the Hechinger piece: gains may reflect teacher quality rather than method; the studies cannot say which part of the system does the work; the Hartford schools were unusually faithful to the original model; early gains may fade; the evidence base is small. Per the Campbell review: several outcomes rest on low-quality evidence. All of this is children with paid, trained teachers and a school day.

## Critiques and replication status (summary)
- **Guided pathways:** the problem (students lost in a cafeteria) is well documented; the remedy shows early-momentum gains where fully implemented, no established completion effect, no causal design, and at least two single practices (mandatory plans, CTE maps) with small negative correlations on their own.
- **Mastery learning:** real but modest effects, concentrated on aligned tests and weaker learners; the two-sigma headline does not replicate.
- **Learning progressions:** contested as empirical models; students don't sit cleanly on rungs.
- **Montessori:** modest positive effects in a 2023 systematic review, strongest in early years and randomized studies; null at secondary in the one large lottery study.

## What this body of work assumes that The Labs cannot
All of it assumes an institution with paid staff (advisors, teachers), enrollment (people are already in and must be kept), a credential at the end, and a calendar (terms, school years). Guided pathways in particular assumes the institution can require a plan and track credits. The exploratory, part-time, stopping-out adult that Rose says the model serves least is close to the typical Labs participant.

## Questions for the pod
- The evaluations found that a mandatory plan on its own did slightly worse. What is the Labs equivalent of "mandatory educational planning," and is there any sign it drops people?
- Guided pathways gains showed up as early momentum (credits in term one). What is the early-momentum metric for a Cycle, and does anyone track it?
- Montessori puts the structure in the room, not the schedule. For a kit-based product, is the structure in the kit, in the branch, in the facilitator, or in the calendar, and which one do people actually navigate by?
- Learning-progression critics found individuals don't fit single levels. Do Labs participants fit "workshop-goer → Cycle participant → maintainer → facilitator," or do real routes skip and loop?
