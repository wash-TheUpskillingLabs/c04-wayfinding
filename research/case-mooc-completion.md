# Case: MITx and HarvardX MOOCs, open self-paced courses at massive scale

**Kind:** failure (against its own founding goal) and extreme (millions of learners)
**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, activation-mobilization, interfaces
**Logged:** 2026-11-05 (desk research, sources below)

## 1. What it is
Massive open online courses (MOOCs) offered free, self-paced or loosely scheduled courses to anyone. Six years of MIT and Harvard data on edX show low completion, low return in the following year, and a shift by providers toward paid professional degrees.

## 2. Where it came from
- Justin Reich (MIT) and José A. Ruipérez-Valiente, "The MOOC Pivot," *Science* 363(6423), 130–131, 2019, using all MITx and HarvardX course data on edX.
- René F. Kizilcec, Chris Piech and Emily Schneider (Stanford), "Deconstructing Disengagement," LAK 2013, on learner trajectories in three computer science MOOCs (high-school, undergraduate and graduate level).

## 3. When
- MITx/HarvardX data: 2012 to May 2018. Paper published January 2019.
- Stanford data: three courses, paper 2013.
- edX began paywalling content in May 2018 and announced online professional master's degrees in October 2018 (per Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente).

## 4. What it says
**Scale and completion**
- The dataset covers 565 course runs of 261 courses, 12.67 million registrations and 5.63 million learners. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente, postprint](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- Completion among all participants fell from 5.91% (2014–15) to 3.13% (2017–18). [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- Among learners who said at the start they intended to complete, completion was 11.5–17.7% by year. Among paying "verified" learners it was 44–56%. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- About 52% of registrants never entered the courseware. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)

**Coming back**
- Of about 1.1 million unique learners in 2015–16, 12% took another course the next year. Second-year return fell from 38% for the first cohort to 7% for the 2016–17 cohort (the supplementary table gives 37.5% and 9.98%). [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)

**Who**
- Learners from "very high" Human Development Index countries made up 55–66% of enrolments and 62–72% of certificates; nearly all growth and decline came from the most affluent countries. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)

**The pivot**
- Providers moved from free courses toward fully online professional master's degrees priced well below campus programs. The authors' summary: "MOOCs pivot from teaching the world to online professional degrees." [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)

**Completion as the wrong lens**
- Clustering learners in three MOOCs found four trajectories: completing, auditing, disengaging and sampling. Sampling dominated in the undergraduate (74%) and graduate (80%) courses; completing ranged from 5% to 27%. [Kizilcec, Piech & Schneider 2013](https://web.stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deconstructingDisengagement.pdf)
- The authors argue a single completion rate hides different reasons for leaving and makes targeted responses harder. [Kizilcec, Piech & Schneider 2013](https://web.stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deconstructingDisengagement.pdf)

## Failure modes seen
- **Sign-up without arrival.** About half of registrants never opened the course. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- **No second course.** Only 12% of a 1.1 million learner cohort took another course the next year. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- **Open door used by the already advantaged.** Enrolment and certification skewed to the most affluent countries. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- **Commitment bought, not built.** Completion was roughly 9 to 15 times higher (by year) among learners who paid for a verified certificate. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)
- **Mission drift toward paying customers.** Providers turned to paid degrees after free courses did not reach their goal. [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://webs.um.es/jruiperez/papers/journals/2019_Science_MOOCPivot_postprint.pdf)

## 5. What it tells us about the problem

- If an open door mostly attracts people who are already well served, what would the equivalent data in a library branch show?

## 6. Hearsay and limits
- The data are from two elite universities on one platform; other providers and non-university MOOCs may differ. Verified learners self-select, so their completion does not show that paying causes completion.
- The postprint text and supplementary tables give slightly different return figures (38% vs 37.5%; 7% vs 9.98%); both are reported above.
- **Rival explanation:** most registrants may never have intended to complete (sampling and auditing are legitimate uses), so low completion measures a mismatch between the provider's metric and learners' goals rather than learner failure. Kizilcec et al. make this argument directly.
- Who could confirm: MIT Teaching Systems Lab; edX/2U research staff.
