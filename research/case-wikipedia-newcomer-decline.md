# Case: English Wikipedia, rejecting good-faith newcomers, then trying to welcome them back

**Kind:** failure (also paradigmatic: the most-studied newcomer decline in open collaboration)
**Bears on:** pre-cycle, post-cycle, facilitators, interfaces
**Logged:** 2026-11-05 (desk research, sources below)

## 1. What it is
English Wikipedia's count of active editors peaked in 2007 and then fell, and researchers traced the fall mainly to newcomers who quit after their first edits were reverted. Several later programs (the Teahouse, The Wikipedia Adventure, the Newcomer Homepage, the Wiki Education classroom program) tried to change what the first weeks look like.

## 2. Where it came from
- Core study: Aaron Halfaker, R. Stuart Geiger, Jonathan T. Morgan and John Riedl, "The Rise and Decline of an Open Collaboration System," *American Behavioral Scientist* 57(5), 2013. Authors were at the University of Minnesota, UC Berkeley and the University of Washington at the time; Morgan and Halfaker later worked at the Wikimedia Foundation.
- Replication across 769 other wikis: TeBlunthuis, Shaw and Hill (CHI 2018).
- Interventions: Morgan and Halfaker's Teahouse experiment (OpenSym 2018); Narayan et al. on The Wikipedia Adventure (CSCW 2017); Warncke-Wang et al. on the Newcomer Homepage (CSCW 2023); Li et al. on the Wiki Education program (CSCW 2020); Zhou, Sun and Terveen on Teahouse interactions (Wiki Workshop 2024).

## 3. When
- Wikipedia growth surge around 2005; active editors peak March 2007; decline thereafter (Halfaker et al.).
- Paper online December 2012, press release January 23, 2013; journal issue 2013.
- Teahouse launched on English Wikipedia around February 2012 (one-year draft blog); controlled evaluation published 2018.
- The Wikipedia Adventure evaluated 2017. Newcomer Homepage experiment ran February 1 to May 1, 2021. Wiki Education data covers 2015–2016 semesters.

## 4. What it says
**The decline**
- Active editors (5+ edits a month) peaked at 56,400 in March 2007 and began a steady decline. [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- The decline came from falling retention of new editors, not from veterans leaving. [Wikimedia research page](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline)
- The authors hand-coded newcomers as vandals, bad-faith, good-faith or "golden." The share of good-faith-or-better newcomers fell from 92.2% (early 2005) to 79.8% (early 2006) and then held steady, so newcomer quality does not explain the later fall. [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- Among desirable newcomers, the share still editing at least two months after their first session was 25.6% in early 2006 and 11.7% a year later, and it did not recover. [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- Desirable newcomers who had at least one first-session edit reverted rose from 6.1% to 18.2% between early 2006 and early 2007. Being reverted predicted leaving. [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- By mid-2008, 75% of new users got their first message from an algorithmic tool. Tool-based reverts of desirable newcomers went from 0% (2006) to 40% (2010). [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- Editors using the Huggle anti-vandalism tool replied to newcomers who tried to discuss a revert about 7% of the time, versus about 60% for manual reverters. [Wikimedia research page](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline)
- Policy pages became harder for newcomers to change; newcomers moved toward less formal "essay" pages, where their edits were more often accepted. [Halfaker et al. 2013, PDF](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- The university release summarized the cause as the restrictiveness of quality control and semi-automated rejection tools built after the 2005 surge. [University of Minnesota](https://cse.umn.edu/college/news/wikipedia-inadvertently-causing-its-own-decline-participation)

**The replication**
- Across 769 large Wikia wikis (2002–2010), the same pattern appeared: growth, then decline; more newcomer reverts; fewer newcomers staying; policy pages closing to newcomers. The pattern hit each wiki at a similar point in its own life, not at the same calendar date. [Community Data Science Collective blog, 2018](https://blog.communitydata.science/revisiting-the-rise-and-decline/)

**Interventions**
- *Teahouse* (Q&A space where volunteer "hosts" answer newcomer "guests"): in its first year, 137 Wikipedians served as hosts and about 1,670 guests were served; compared with similar early editors, guests made about three times as many article edits (draft, unpublished figures). [Wikimedia blog draft](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Wikimedia_Blog/Drafts/Wikipedia_Teahouse_Turns_One)
- In a controlled experiment, newly registered editors invited to the Teahouse were retained at a higher rate than uninvited editors, in both short- and long-term survival. [OpenSym blog, 2020](https://opensym.org/blog/2020/01/22/evaluating-the-impact-of-the-wikipedia-teahouse-on-newcomer-socialization-and-retention/)
- Only a small fraction of invited newcomers actually visited the Teahouse. [Li et al. 2020, PDF](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~diyiy/docs/cscw_li_2020_wiki.pdf)
- Across 16,007 Teahouse exchanges, more policy links in the first answer were associated with a 10.9% higher likelihood of the newcomer leaving (per standard deviation); longer answers and more messages were associated with staying. [Zhou, Sun & Terveen 2024](https://wikiworkshop.org/papers/investigating-social-interaction-factors-for-newcomer-retention-in-wikipedia-teahouse.pdf)
- *The Wikipedia Adventure* (interactive tutorial): a large invitation-based field experiment found no effect on contributions over 180 days, though users who chose it reported a positive experience. [NSF PAR record, CSCW 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10039617)
- *Newcomer Homepage* (suggested tasks, assigned volunteer mentor, help links): across 27 non-English Wikipedias and 244,060 new accounts in 2021, first-day activation rose about 1% in odds; two-week retention showed no significant difference. The mentor module was not evaluated. [Warncke-Wang et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.09642)
- The Homepage authors note the Teahouse is the only prior controlled experiment they know of with a significant retention effect. [Warncke-Wang et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.09642)
- *Wiki Education* (students edit Wikipedia as a course assignment): across 16,819 students in 2015–2016, 4.2% were still editing a year later versus 2.1% of matched controls. Messages students sent to others predicted staying; messages received from non-student Wikipedians did not. [Li et al. 2020, PDF](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~diyiy/docs/cscw_li_2020_wiki.pdf)

## Failure modes seen
- **Quality control that cannot tell good faith from bad.** Tool-assisted reverts of desirable newcomers grew from 0% to 40% between 2006 and 2010. [Halfaker et al.](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- **First contact by machine.** By mid-2008, three in four newcomers' first message came from a tool. [Halfaker et al.](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- **Rules that newcomers cannot touch.** Policy pages hardened against newer editors while essays stayed open. [Halfaker et al.](https://stuartgeiger.com/papers/abs-rise-and-decline-wikipedia.pdf)
- **Welcome that is never found.** Teahouse invitations worked, but few invitees visited. [Li et al.](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~diyiy/docs/cscw_li_2020_wiki.pdf)
- **Pointing to the rulebook as the answer.** Policy links in a first reply were associated with more newcomers leaving. [Zhou et al.](https://wikiworkshop.org/papers/investigating-social-interaction-factors-for-newcomer-retention-in-wikipedia-teahouse.pdf)
- **Self-paced tutorial without people.** The Wikipedia Adventure changed nothing measurable at 180 days. [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10039617)
- **Cliff after the course ends.** Even the best-performing program kept about 4 in 100 students editing a year on. [Li et al.](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~diyiy/docs/cscw_li_2020_wiki.pdf)

## 5. What it tells us about the problem
*Left for the pod. Questions to answer:*
- When a newcomer's first contribution to a shared commons is rejected or corrected, who sees it happen, and what does the newcomer see next?
- Which "rules" spaces in The Labs are open to newcomers to change, and which have hardened?
- The Teahouse worked when people arrived but few arrived. Where does an invitation go, and how many people open it?
- If a cohort-based program (Wiki Ed) roughly doubled a one-year retention rate from 2% to 4%, what number would count as success for people leaving a Cycle?

## 6. Hearsay and limits
- All retention findings are associations except the Teahouse and Homepage experiments. The Teahouse draft blog numbers were never confirmed in a published version that we opened.
- We could not open the full Teahouse 2018 paper or the Teahouse pilot report (the request to the pilot-report URL was blocked), so we have no effect sizes for it.
- **Rival explanation:** the TeBlunthuis et al. replication suggests rise-and-decline is a general life-cycle pattern of peer-production communities, so Wikipedia's tools and policies may be one expression of maturity, not the root cause. Halfaker et al. themselves list others: shrinking available work as articles fill in, an early-adopter effect, and post-2006 legal pressure.
- Who could confirm: Wikimedia Foundation Research team; Community Data Science Collective (Hill, Shaw, TeBlunthuis).
