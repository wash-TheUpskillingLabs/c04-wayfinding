# Frames built from the five needs, and the two that hold them

Companion to the post [Two Frames for Five Human Needs: The Quilting Bee and the Trail Club](/blog/frames/) and its data, `content/frames/frames.json`. Written Nov 9, 2026, and revised the same day so that every frame is an analogy: a place or system people already know, like a train station, a ski resort or a CrossFit gym. Revised again so that every frame says where its likeness ends, and to add the trail club beside the quilting bee, because a quilt and an open-source project differ in ways that matter. Written for Dorst's step 6: "identifying patterns in the themes to create frames." The themes are the five needs in [the field themes post](/blog/field-themes/) and `research/themes-psychology.md`: Mattering, Coherence, Support, Belonging, Becoming.

This is one reading, by one reader, offered as material for the week of prototypes (Nov 8 to 13) and the Nov 14 vote. The paradox and the frames are the pod's to write. The frames the builders took home on Nov 7 aren't in the README yet, so nothing here tests them; hold them up against these.

## How we looked

- **What a frame is.** An analogy: treat this problem as if it were a thing everyone already knows how to run, and see what follows. Dorst's form: "If the problem situation is approached as if it is …, then …". The pod's form: *If we treat this as if it were a problem of [theme], then…*. A frame proposes a way of working that turns a need into value; the prototype tests the thing built on it.
- **Where frames come from.** Dorst: "new frames can be created by linking to practices from other professional fields" (Dorst 2018). His best-known case, Kings Cross in Sydney, had been labelled "alcohol-related violence." Treated as a music festival, a crowd of mostly young people out for a good night, it opened late-night transport out, more toilets, chill-out spaces, varied entertainment, taxi queues, safe places to sober up, and Kings Cross Guides to welcome visitors.
- **How a frame fails.** The City of Sydney adopted the festival frame. After two young men died in separate attacks, the state government passed laws that cut opening hours, and the City was pushed back to the crime-scene frame. Vermaas, Dorst and Thurgood (2015) call this a frame the client can no longer accept. So a frame has to be one that the people who'd live with it can adopt.
- **Our method.** We started with the README's hunch and wrote it, and three more candidates, as paradoxes: two needs, or two people's needs, that pull apart. For each, we asked: where else in the world do people get both at once, without being paid to be there? Every answer had to be a place you could walk into. We kept one only when there was evidence its way of working does what we need where it comes from, and when the likeness ran through a need, not just the surface.
- **The test** (from the themes post, plus one to keep frames analogies, one to say where each likeness ends, and one from Kings Cross):
  0. Is it a place or system people already know? If you can't walk into it, it's an idea, not a frame.
  0b. Where does the likeness end? Name how the place differs from The Labs; that's where a prototype will surprise you.
  1. Which need does the likeness run through, and what research stands behind it?
  2. Do unlike organizations reach for that need, and does someone inside The Labs feel it?
  3. Which side of the paradox gives?
  4. Does it open many things to build, or only one? (fruitfulness)
  5. Can three real people try it by Nov 13?
  6. Who would have to live with it, and would they adopt it?

## Four candidate paradoxes

Written in the README's *because* form. The first is the pod's hunch from Oct 24; the other three are contemplated from the inside voices and `synthesis-thresholds.md`. None is the pod's paradox until the pod says so.

### P1. Seeing the route, making your own (Coherence and Becoming). The README's hunch.

> *Building for stability and adaptability. Is the self-managed, adaptive nature of the learning experience making it harder to understand the value, the learning and the belonging? Adaptive vs. static and visible.*

- **Upskillers need to see the route**, because people commit to what makes sense: where they are, what's next, and that they can handle it. Today how it all fits together isn't written down anywhere (problem owner), and organizers name cognitive load as the biggest problem (Record #4).
- **Upskillers need the route to be their own**, because The Labs is self-managed by design, and what people are building is a self nobody else could plan. People already aren't sure they're allowed to go off on their own (problem owner).
- **Both are true.** Every fixed path makes The Labs easier to see and harder to own; every open choice does the reverse.

Evidence on the Coherence side: CUNY's ASAP, which bundles structure, an advisor seen twice a month and a cohort, nearly doubled three-year graduation (22% to 40%) in a randomized trial, and a replication at three Ohio colleges did the same (19% to 35%) (MDRC). Guided pathways research names the cafeteria college's problem as too many choices and too little guidance (Jenkins 2014). On the Becoming side: the problem owner, Ibarra's career changers who become someone new by trying selves on, and Scouting's century-old compromise, a self-chosen catalog of merit badges beside a fixed rank ladder, whose failure mode is "mills" that inflate badge counts (`case-scouting-merit-badges.md`). Caveat: ASAP students study full time, with financial help; Upskillers come in the evenings.

### P2. Seen as yourself, part of a team (Mattering and Belonging)

- **An Upskiller needs what they did to be seen as theirs**, because employers read people one at a time and judge The Labs against multi-million-dollar institutions (problem owner).
- **A team needs its work to stay the team's**, because what alumni valued most was each other (alumni survey), and asking for individual contributions cut across a team's sense of itself (problem owner, Nov 7).
- **Both are true.**

### P3. A person at every crossing (Support, for two people at once)

- **A newcomer needs a person at each crossing**, because readiness without someone to cross with doesn't carry anyone: reminder texts got students to college when a counselor answered the replies, and campaigns that dropped the advising lost the effect (`case-summer-melt.md`).
- **Organizers need the work to fit around their lives**, because they're volunteers too: nobody's job is to help people find their way (problem owner), and organizer delays become participant emergencies (Record #4).
- **Both are true.** It's one need, felt by two people who can't both have it on today's terms.

### P4. When the Cycle ends (Belonging and the deadline)

- **A Cycle needs to end**, because a deadline is what makes a team ship together (`synthesis-thresholds.md`, paradox 1).
- **People need something that doesn't end**, because belonging takes frequent contact over time (Baumeister & Leary 1995), and today leaving the Cycle means leaving The Labs (problem owner). Seven of 11 alumni are still in touch with their team anyway (alumni survey).
- **Both are true.**

## The longlist: forty places

Dorst: make many frames before choosing. For each paradox we listed every place we could think of where people get both needs at once. **Bold** places are developed below. Strong runners-up worth a builder's week: summer camp (the closest likeness to a Cycle), a climbing gym (fixes the ski resort's weak spot), hockey assists, the dive buddy and the AA sponsor.


**Seeing the route, making your own**

- **The Camino**: waymarks that hold still, and a passport stamped by the people who host you.
- **A ski resort**: runs rated against this mountain; you pick by skill and mood.
- **A CrossFit box**: one workout on the whiteboard, scaled for each person.
- A climbing gym: the walls are reset every few weeks but the grades and tape colours stay, so the routes can change without the map changing. It fixes the ski resort’s weak spot.
- A jazz jam session: the chord changes are fixed and everyone knows them; the solo is yours, and anyone can sit in.
- A Montessori room: the materials sit on the shelf in order, and each child chooses their own work.
- IKEA: one marked path through the whole store, with shortcuts for people who know where they’re going.
- A hospital corridor: coloured lines on the floor: follow the blue one to radiology.
- The Appalachian Trail: a white blaze on the trees, and the hikers’ motto “hike your own hike”.
- An open-world game: the map fills in as you explore, with a main quest and side quests.
- A martial arts dojo: belts make progress visible, and you test when you’re ready.
- A pilot’s logbook: every flight logged, and the milestones signed by an instructor.

**Seen as yourself, part of a team**

- **Film credits**: every role named, on one list that belongs to the film.
- **A relay**: your leg is timed and seen; the result is the team’s.
- Hockey assists: a goal also credits up to two players who set it up, so helping shows on the scoresheet.
- A signature quilt: each block made and signed by one person, and the quilt shared.
- A potluck: every dish labelled with who brought it, one table.
- Album liner notes: who played what, on the band’s record.
- A kitchen brigade: every station named, and one plate goes out.
- A barn raising: a whole community builds one barn in a day, each by their skill.
- A curtain call: the crew comes out and bows too.
- A rowing eight: the boat only moves together, and nobody’s stroke stands out. A warning more than a model: all Belonging, little Mattering.

**A person at every crossing**

- **A train station**: the board for most travelers, a staffed desk for the rest.
- An airport transfer desk: a missed connection means rebooking, not being stranded. Folded into the train station.
- **A doula**: someone who knows the way stays beside you the whole way across.
- **A tailor’s shop**: newcomers start on real pieces, beside people a step ahead.
- A school crossing guard: standing at the dangerous crossings only, not walking every child home.
- A dive buddy: everyone is paired, and buddies check each other before every dive, so nobody needs an instructor of their own.
- An AA sponsor: someone who has worked the steps guides a newcomer, and sponsoring is part of their own recovery.
- A lifeguard: one person watches the whole pool and steps in only when someone needs it.
- A teaching hospital: see one, do one, teach one.
- A welcome committee: neighbors who knock on a newcomer’s door in the first week.

**When the Cycle ends**

- **A harbor**: a crew keeps its berth between voyages.
- **parkrun**: the same place and time every week, and nobody finishes last.
- Summer camp: a session that ends, campers who come back next summer, and counselors who were campers. The closest likeness to a Cycle we found.
- A theatre company: the show closes, the company stays and casts the next one.
- Burning Man: a city built and taken down every year, with regional gatherings in between.
- A sports club’s off-season: the season ends, and the pickup games don’t.
- A class reunion: the class stays a class, years after it ends.
- A choir: a weekly rehearsal toward each concert, then on to the next.

## Ten frames

Each frame: the frame; the needs it builds on (★ marks the two it lets a person have at once); where it's done well; what moves; what it opens; a prototype by Nov 13; who has to adopt it; what could go wrong; where it differs from our work; what it would feel like.

The themes post's four frames are all here, developed further: the film credits, the train station, the harbor and the Camino. (Its first draft named two ideas rather than places, vouching and a rite of passage; a vouch now rides on a film credit, and the Camino's stamps make each crossing witnessed.)

### 1. The Camino (P1)

**If we treat this as a problem of the Camino, a waymarked pilgrimage, then the way is marked and holds still, each person walks it at their own pace and for their own reasons, and a passport they carry is stamped by the people who host them.**

- **Needs:** Coherence ★, Becoming ★, Support.
- **Done well:** pilgrims to Santiago de Compostela carry a credencial and fill it with stamps (sellos). They show it to stay at pilgrim albergues, and in Santiago it confirms they walked at least the last 100 km (or cycled 200) for the Compostela (American Pilgrims on the Camino). There are many routes, from many starting points, all ending in Santiago. Many hosts are hospitaleros voluntarios, former pilgrims who host after a short training; American Pilgrims' training asks that you've walked at least 100 km, and those who've gone from walking to hosting call it a Camino of its own.
- **What moves:** the hunch's "adaptive vs. static and visible" splits along a new line. The marks and stamps are static and visible; the walk is adaptive. What gives is the idea of one path.
- **What it opens:** (1) a Labs passport stamped by hosts at each threshold (a workshop, an open lab, a Cycle, a teach-back), so progress is written by others, not self-reported: the record this pod owns; (2) signs in OLOS at every junction: where you are, the ways on from here; (3) alumni hosts after a short training; (4) a first stamp for each way in (a library, an agency, a friend); (5) an arrival someone witnesses.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** a passport with the journey map's thresholds as stamp boxes; a host stamps it at an open lab; three people at different steps say what it should show, and to whom.
- **Who adopts it:** hosts, who stamp; organizers, who set the marks; libraries, as the albergues.
- **Watch for:** one destination; The Labs has none, so no stamp reads as a finish line. A stamp book can become a checklist (Scouting's merit badge mills). The Compostela's distance rule is a gate; the passport is a record, never a gate.
- **Where it differs:** a pilgrimage is usually walked once, mostly alone, toward one place. The Labs is returned to, worked in teams, and has no single destination.
- **Feels like:** I can see how far I've come and the way on is marked, but the walk is mine.

### 2. The Ski Resort (P1)

**If we treat this as a problem of a ski resort, then every junction has a sign, every run is rated against this mountain, you choose your run by skill and mood, and a ski school and a patrol are there when you need them.**

- **Needs:** Coherence ★, Becoming ★, Support.
- **Done well:** the steering committee's parked example (`parked-frame-ski-trails.md`), extended to the whole resort. The green circle, blue square and black diamond were adopted by the National Ski Areas Association in 1968 (Ski Utah). Each mountain rates its own runs, relative to its other runs (NC State; Summit Daily).
- **What moves:** the route becomes a choice among rated runs, with the whole mountain on one map. What gives is one path.
- **What it opens:** a trail map of The Labs from the journey map; ratings per threshold that belong to this region and are revised each season from where people turned back; a ski school for those who want a lesson first; a patrol that sweeps for people stuck partway; a lift back to the top.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** rate the journey map's thresholds green, blue and black with three alumni; ask three newcomers to pick a first run and say why.
- **Who adopts it:** organizers, who rate and revise; hosts, as instructors and patrol.
- **Watch for:** the mountain mostly holds still and The Labs changes every Cycle. A rating rates the run but is read as rating the skier: ratings go on runs, never on people.
- **Where it differs:** skiers buy a day on a mountain that mostly holds still. Upskillers give their evenings to a place that changes every Cycle.
- **Feels like:** I can see the whole mountain, and I pick the run that fits me today.

### 3. The CrossFit Box (P1)

**If we treat this as a problem of a CrossFit box, then everyone does the same workout, written on the whiteboard, scaled to where each person is today, with a coach in the room.**

- **Needs:** Coherence ★, Becoming ★, Support, Belonging.
- **Done well:** one workout of the day for everyone. CrossFit's guidance: scaling meets the athlete at their current level while keeping the workout's intended stimulus (CrossFit, Scaling Part 2); most people scale most days, and scaled is still CrossFit (CrossFit, "Am I Even Doing CrossFit?"). Members of a CrossFit gym reported more social capital and belonging than members of a traditional gym, though neither predicted attendance (Whiteman-Sandland, Hawkins & Clayton 2018).
- **What moves:** the hunch, inside one night. The session is static and visible; how hard it is for you is adaptive. What gives is the idea that a visible structure means everyone at one level.
- **What it opens:** one build prompt per open lab in three scales; a coach in the room; scaling said out loud as normal; the same kind of night at every level (ship something small with people). Mostly inside a session, which is the Scaffolding pod's ground: build it with them.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** one open lab with a single prompt on the board in three scales and a coach; three people at different levels say how it felt.
- **Who adopts it:** hosts and coaches at open labs.
- **Watch for:** the whiteboard of scores. On one CrossFit podcast, a member describes hearing a full-weight (Rx) score called out and feeling they hadn't done enough. Post the workout, never the scores.
- **Where it differs:** a workout is over in an hour and leaves nothing behind. A Labs night is meant to leave something built that someone keeps.
- **Feels like:** we're all doing the same thing tonight, and mine fits me.

### 4. The Film Credits (P2)

**If we treat this as a problem of a film's credits, then everyone who made the work is named for what they did, on one list that belongs to the work.**

- **Needs:** Mattering ★, Belonging ★.
- **Done well:** a film's end credits name everyone by role. Science borrowed the idea: CRediT (ANSI/NISO Z39.104-2022) names 14 roles; one person can hold several, several can share one, optionally lead, equal or supporting (NISO), because byline order had become an unreliable sign of who did what (Dublin Core MSI). Peer recognition moves people: 100 of Wikipedia's most productive editors who had never had an award were given one at random and were 60% more productive over 90 days than 100 who weren't (Restivo & van de Rijt 2012).
- **What moves:** Mattering by role, not rank; Belonging holds because the list belongs to the work. What gives is the ranking.
- **What it opens:** a credits block on every OLOS project page, with roles for Labs work (research, building, design, testing, facilitating, hosting, writing, keeping the team together); credits that flow to a profile and export for a résumé; credits for hosts and organizers; a teammate's line on any credit, a vouch. In nine large firms, people hired by referral were 10–30% less likely to quit than others with similar skills (Burks et al. 2015).
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** credits for one finished project, every role agreed by the team; show three alumni and one person who hires; ask what they read first.
- **Who adopts it:** teams, employers, OLOS maintainers.
- **Watch for:** in the same Wikipedia study, award winners got more awards from other editors, 12 to 2, which the authors read as prestige rather than merit. Give everyone a line.
- **Where it differs:** a film is finished and its credits are fixed. Labs work keeps changing after it ships, so its credits have to keep being written.
- **Feels like:** my name is on what we made, next to what I did.

### 5. The Relay (P2)

**If we treat this as a problem of a relay, then each person's leg is their own, timed and seen, and the result belongs to the team.**

- **Needs:** Mattering ★, Belonging ★, Support.
- **Done well:** at the 2008 Olympics, freestyle swimmers in the second, third and last legs swam faster in the relay than in their individual races; first swimmers didn't (Hüffmeier & Hertel 2011, via Pacific Standard). In 199 swimmers, the gain held only when the leg mattered to the team and the team had a real chance at a medal (Hüffmeier et al. 2012). In medley relays, where teammates' effort is harder to judge, there was no gain (summary of their studies, Daily News of Open Water Swimming). Across 302,576 performances, later legs were faster only when the team outcome was meaningful; otherwise all legs were slower (Hüffmeier et al. 2017).
- **What moves:** being seen, in a part the team needs, is what makes people give more to the team. What gives is the idea that individual credit and team spirit pull against each other.
- **What it opens:** each person's part named on the team's work; a handoff moment between parts; a shared team result.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** show each person's part on one project page with a handoff note from the person before; ask three alumni whether it made them feel more part of the team or less.
- **Who adopts it:** teams.
- **Watch for:** first legs gave less; make the first handoff count too. Show the legs, never compare the times.
- **Where it differs:** a race has one clock and a clear finish. Labs work is judged by the people who use it, long after the handoff.
- **Feels like:** my part was mine, and we did it together.

### 6. The Train Station (P3)

**If we treat this as a problem of a train station, then the board shows every way on from here, the signs tell you where you are, and a staffed desk at every station tells you which train is yours.**

- **Needs:** Coherence ★, Support ★.
- **Done well:** signs and boards serve most travelers; the desk serves whoever can't find their way. Medicine built that desk on purpose: patient navigation, begun by Harold Freeman in Harlem in 1990 (Bush et al. 2017). A navigator, often a community health worker or trained layperson, finds what's in a person's way and guides them through; the Community Preventive Services Task Force found it increased colorectal cancer screening of every kind across 27 studies (The Community Guide). Reviews following Freeman treat barrier assessment as a key part (Domingo et al. 2017). Kings Cross's frame produced guides to welcome visitors. Airports add a transfer desk: a missed connection means rebooking, not being stranded.
- **What moves:** one person per station serves everyone passing through, so hours are bounded.
- **What it opens:** a departures board for every threshold; a named host at each station of the journey map; a desk whose first question is "what's in the way?"; a transfer desk for anyone who missed a Cycle; a name to ask in every notification.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** a named alum or moderator at each station of the journey map; three people at three stations find their next train.
- **Who adopts it:** whoever staffs the desk; organizers unless alumni take stations.
- **Watch for:** the desk as bottleneck; if every desk is an organizer, the organizers' side gives.
- **Where it differs:** trains run on a timetable whether or not anyone boards. At The Labs, the next train often doesn't exist until someone organizes it.
- **Feels like:** I always know where I am, and who to ask.

### 7. The Doula (P3)

**If we treat this as a problem of having a doula, then someone who knows the way stays beside you the whole way across, not to do it for you, but so you never face it alone.**

- **Needs:** Support ★, Becoming ★, Coherence.
- **Done well:** a Cochrane review of 27 trials and about 16,000 women found continuous one-to-one support in labour improved outcomes, among them more spontaneous births and fewer caesareans, with no harms found; support from someone in a doula role, trained but neither hospital staff nor family, seemed to help most (Bohren et al. 2017). Women said doulas helped them feel in control, boosted confidence and explained what was happening in plain words (Bohren 2019).
- **What moves:** only if the companion just crossed, for one crossing, as their own next step; then organizers don't carry it.
- **What it opens:** an alum companion for a newcomer's first Cycle; the first few weeks only; companions from the Cycle just finished; a companion role in the credits.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** ask three alumni whether they'd companion someone next Cycle and what they'd need; ask one recent joiner what they'd have wanted.
- **Who adopts it:** alumni.
- **Watch for:** dependence, uneven companions, burnout; a companion mustn't become unpaid staff.
- **Where it differs:** a birth lasts hours, and a doula is trained for it. A Labs crossing takes weeks, and the companion is a volunteer.
- **Feels like:** someone who's done this is walking it with me.

### 8. The Tailor's Shop (P3)

**If we treat this as a problem of a tailor's shop, then newcomers start on small, real pieces of real garments, beside people a step ahead, and helping the next apprentice is how you move toward the centre of the shop.**

- **Needs:** Becoming ★, Belonging ★, Support.
- **Done well:** Lave and Wenger (1991) studied tailors' apprentices, midwives, quartermasters, butchers and recovering alcoholics and found *legitimate peripheral participation*: newcomers start at the edge with small but real tasks and move toward full participation. The same pattern runs through the other places: Camino hosts were pilgrims, parkrun's volunteers are its runners and walkers. The region's decision log (Oct 28) makes contributing how an Upskiller becomes a trainer, facilitator or mentor.
- **What moves:** help isn't extra hours; it's the edge of the work, and the helper's own next step. Asks least of organizers.
- **What it opens:** real starter tasks on every project; roles stepping inward (visitor, contributor, maintainer, host), each credited; learning inside project nights; a newcomer's first piece checked by someone one step ahead.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** three real starter tasks on one live project, given to three people at the edge; see who comes back.
- **Who adopts it:** project maintainers.
- **Watch for:** getting stuck at the edge, or free labor; Lave and Wenger tie legitimacy to real access to the community's resources. Gatekeeping. Tests at each step inward lose people: about 57 in 100 Carpentries trainees made it from training to badge in 2018, and the test was shrunk in 2023 (`case-carpentries-instructor-pipeline.md`).
- **Where it differs:** an apprentice tailor works full time for years. An Upskiller gives a few evenings, so each step inward has to be much smaller.
- **Feels like:** I'm doing real work from the first night, and I can see how people got further in.

### 9. The Harbor (P4)

**If we treat this as a problem of a harbor, then a Cycle is one voyage, and a crew keeps its berth between voyages.**

- **Needs:** Belonging ★, Support ★, Becoming.
- **Done well:** Civic Tech DC kept meeting after its national hub let it go; Recurse Center's alumni "never graduate"; Indivisible groups that lasted met monthly and replaced leaders (themes post). In 167 married couples, a partner who was available, didn't interfere and encouraged them strongly predicted exploration (Feeney & Thrush 2010): a harbor is what lets people sail out.
- **What moves:** the Cycle keeps its deadline; the berth doesn't end. What gives is the Cycle's claim to be the whole of The Labs.
- **What it opens:** team pages that last; a standing night in port; visits between crews; crews that re-form.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** a standing night and a page where past teams keep their place; ask three alumni whether they'd come.
- **Who adopts it:** alumni crews, and whoever keeps the night.
- **Watch for:** berths for teams leave out people who weren't on one; an empty berth is worse than none.
- **Where it differs:** a ship has a captain and a crew that sails together for years. Labs teams form fresh each Cycle, and nobody commands them.
- **Feels like:** when the Cycle ends, I still have somewhere to go, and people who expect me.

### 10. parkrun (P4)

**If we treat this as a problem of parkrun, then the same thing happens at the same time and place every week, anyone can come without signing up for a season, everyone who helps is counted, and nobody finishes last.**

- **Needs:** Belonging ★, Coherence ★, Mattering.
- **Done well:** a free, timed 5 km every Saturday morning, run by local volunteers. In a UK study of over 7,000 parkrunners most weren't regular runners before joining; in 865 Australian adults, men's wellbeing went with the community connection they felt (Grunseit et al. 2018). Volunteering is logged and counts toward milestone shirts like running (University of Leeds). Every event has a tail walker so nobody finishes last (Nuffield Health).
- **What moves:** the Cycle stays a sprint; what people belong to is weekly. What gives is the Cycle as the only way in. Also moves the synthesis's first paradox: come any week you can.
- **What it opens:** open labs as the standing weekly thing; a tail walker who stays with whoever is newest or furthest behind; helping that counts like taking part; milestones on the person's own record.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** one open lab in parkrun's shape (next week's date announced, a named tail walker, every helper credited); ask three people whether they'd come back, and why.
- **Who adopts it:** hosts, every week.
- **Watch for:** weekly volunteers; counting attendance edges toward the activity telemetry the constitution rules out, so any count is the person's own, opt-in, never compared.
- **Where it differs:** a run asks nothing of you but showing up. A Labs night asks for work, which is harder to drop into.
- **Feels like:** it's on every week, I come when I can, and nobody gets left at the back.

## What the frames have in common

1. **The people who crossed host the crossing.** Camino hosts, parkrun volunteers, the tailor's apprentices, the companion. Both sides of P3 are met.
2. **Fix the marks, free the walk.** Waymarks, trail signs, the whiteboard. What holds still is the signs and structure; what moves is the person.
3. **Show the parts, never the ranking.** Credits without order, relay legs with a team time, the tail walker, stamps that record presence not speed. The warnings agree: the whiteboard of scores, first legs that gave less.
4. **A record the person carries, written by others.** The credencial, a film's credits, a parkrun profile: the pod's own ground.
5. **Something standing outlasts a season.** parkrun every Saturday, a crew's berth, the same class at the same hour.
6. **The frames lean away from what the field counts.** The field reaches most for Mattering (27 of 50), almost always with something countable; five of the twelve frames (with the quilting bee and the trail club) build on it. Support carries ten; Becoming, the field's quietest need (9 of 50), carries eight. A pattern in the places we chose, not a finding about the world.

## Two frames that hold everything

The patterns point at one kind of place: one pattern with room for each person, signed parts in a shared whole, help where people already sit, a record people keep, a gathering that outlasts each project. Looking back through the forty, we found two. Each holds all five needs and all four paradoxes, and each differs from our real situation in ways worth naming. The first was already on the list (as "a signature quilt", under P2). The second, the trail club, starts from where the quilt differs.

### The Quilting Bee (all four)

**If we treat this as a problem of a quilting bee, then everyone works from one pattern in their own fabric, each block is signed by the person who made it, newcomers learn at the frame beside people who have quilted for years, the finished quilt is given to someone at a threshold in their life, and the bee meets again for the next one.**

- **Needs:** all five, joined.
- **Done well:** friendship quilts of the 1840s and 1850s: every block the same pattern, each signed, made for wedding couples or as send-offs for migrating families (Northwest Museum of Arts and Culture); album quilts best known as group efforts, each maker contributing blocks (Colonial Williamsburg). Baltimore album quilts, 40 miles from Washington, many with blocks each made and signed by a different person (Wikipedia; the Met). Round robins: each member adds a border to a shared center; one bee of ten worked in pairs on each border of one quilt and draws names so every member ends up with a group-made quilt (American Quilter). In 29 interviews with a quilting group: new skills, flow, strong friendships, affirmation that boosted motivation to improve, and purpose from quilts given away (Burt & Atkinson 2012).
- **P1:** the pattern is static and visible; fabric and hand are yours. The hunch's worry, that an adaptive experience makes "the value, the learning and the belonging" hard to see, is what a quilt answers: the gift shows the value, each block what its maker learned, the quilt who belonged.
- **P2:** every block signed; the quilt belongs to everyone.
- **P3:** help happens at the frame, from whoever sits beside you; not extra hours.
- **P4:** each quilt is finished and given away; the bee meets again.
- **It holds the ten:** Camino passport → each member's album; film credits → signatures; relay → round robin; tailor's shop and doula → the frame; train station and ski resort → the pattern on the wall; CrossFit → one pattern in many fabrics; harbor and parkrun → the bee that meets again.
- **What it opens:** a pattern library (each kind of night or project as a named pattern with a set block size, so any lab can run it and every block fits; national keeps the patterns, regions make their own quilts, patterns that work go back upstream, as the region already does with OLOS); signed blocks and a label on every shipped project (who made it, for whom); the frame (open labs side by side on one team's project, newcomer beside someone who's done it); round robins (projects passed between Cycles, each team adding a border); a member's album; the sashing, the strips between blocks, which is this pod's own ground: the thresholds between parts.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** everyone at an open lab makes the same small block (one prompt, fixed size) their own way and signs it; join the blocks and give the result to someone who asked for it; ask three people whether they saw their part, the whole, and who it was for. Separately, lay out one project page as a quilt (signed blocks, handoffs as sashing, a label) for three alumni and one person who hires.
- **Who adopts it:** organizers (patterns), teams (blocks), experienced members (the frame), national (the library).
- **Watch for:** the expert who unpicks a newcomer's stitches. A quilter's memoir recalls her mother removing guests' stitches that didn't measure up (Mountain View Today); review at the frame has to be help, never a public redo. A pattern too tight for anyone's fabric. Pace: a quilt takes months, a block has to fit an evening. The quilt goes to someone who asked for it. It can read as quaint.
- **Where it differs:** a quilt is finished, it's one object, its blocks are made apart, and it's made in one room. Our work is none of those things; the table below says what that changes.
- **Feels like:** I made my block my way, and my name is on it. It's part of something bigger we gave away, and next week we start another.

### Where the quilt differs from our work

Much of what The Labs builds is open-source software: changes to OLOS, made in this region's copy and sent back to the national project, and each proposal has to say who keeps it in January (README). A quilt and an open-source project differ in ways that matter for exactly that work.

| A quilt | An open-source project | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Finished, and given away | Never finished. Like roads and bridges, it needs upkeep, and most of that labor goes unseen (Eghbal 2016) | Someone has to keep it after the Cycle, and the keeping needs credit |
| One quilt, one person | Copied freely, adapted, and sent back. This region's OLOS is itself a copy of national's (README) | The gift goes to people you'll never meet |
| Blocks made apart, meeting at the seams | Parts depend on each other, and a change in one can break another | Joining the parts is real work, not just a border |
| Every stitch shows | Review, upkeep and fixes rarely show (Eghbal 2016) | Signed blocks would credit what's visible and miss the rest |
| One room, one frame | Spread out, and mostly not at the same time | Help can't come only from whoever sits beside you |
| A host who might quietly redo a stitch | Review is built in: core developers check others' contributions before they go in (Crowston et al. 2006) | The gate is part of the work, so it has to be designed as help |
| About ten people around a frame | A small core and a long tail. In the Apache web server, about 15 developers wrote more than 80% of the code for new features (Mockus, Fielding & Herbsleb 2002) | Most contributors give a little, so design for them |
| Pull up a chair | Newcomers face many barriers to a first contribution; one model counts 58, 13 of them social (Steinmacher et al. 2015) | The first threshold is far harder than a seat at the frame, and it's this pod's ground |

None of this makes the bee wrong. It holds the people, the evening and the gift better than anything else we found. It just doesn't hold the code, so a team that takes it has to design for these differences.

### The Trail Club (all four)

**If we treat this as a problem of a trail club that keeps a long trail open, then one standard marks the whole trail so anyone can walk it their own way, each local club keeps its own section with a named overseer for every stretch, newcomers learn on work trips beside people who have done it for years, crews form for a season's big project and then disband, and the club and the trail go on.**

- **Needs:** all five, joined.
- **Done well:** the Appalachian Trail, about 2,200 miles, kept open mostly by volunteers: 4,730 of them gave 170,147 hours in 2023 (ATC 2023 Volunteer Impact Report). About 30 local clubs each maintain an assigned section (ATC, Cooperative Management); the Conservancy's paid staff serves primarily as a support team (Associations Now 2013). This region's own: the Potomac Appalachian Trail Club, formed in 1927 to build a key section, now cares for about 1,200 miles of trail (PATC). Overseers take "ownership" of a section; district managers recruit, train and support them (PATC South District); crews tackle the larger projects the maintainers identify, and maintainers file a report for each day of work (PATC volunteer roles). One standard: a white blaze 2 inches by 6, about 165,000 of them (NPS, archived); clubs choose between two kinds of double blaze and no others, and individual maintainers should not change the blazing on personal preference (ATC policy on Trail Design, Construction and Maintenance). Konnarock and the other ATC crews run 3 to 8 days, no experience needed (ATC, first-time crew volunteers), led by experienced crew leaders, and crew work has led to jobs with the Conservancy (ATC, Trail Crews). Each shelter has a log where hikers comment on the state of the trail (Appalachian Trail Histories); the Conservancy gathers condition reports from the ground and shares them (ATC, Hurricane Helene update). One club's newsletter logs who did what work, where and when (Smoky Mountains Hiking Club, May 2023), and clubs' volunteers and hours are reported each year (ATC 2022 VIP/VIF report).
- **It answers where the quilt differs.** A trail is never finished, and the overseer keeps it after the season. Most hikers never meet the people who keep their trail open, open source's problem too; the club's answer is a work log and a yearly count. Sections depend on each other, so one closure reroutes everyone. The work is spread out, each overseer on their own schedule, while work trips bring people together. Changes to the standard go through the club. A small core keeps the trail, and a long tail comes for a day. The first threshold is designed: "no experience needed." And it's held the way this region holds OLOS: local clubs keep sections of one trail to one standard, and a national body keeps the standard and carries the whole.
- **P1:** the blazes are the same everywhere, and every hiker walks their own hike.
- **P2:** the stretch is yours; the trail is everyone's.
- **P3:** district managers and crew leaders bring newcomers in, so helping is part of the work, not extra hours.
- **P4:** crews disband; the club and the trail go on.
- **It holds the ten:** Camino and ski resort → the marked way; CrossFit → a crew's single project, worked at every level; film credits and relay → the work log and the sections handed on; train station → the shelter and its register; doula and tailor's shop → the crew leader beside a newcomer; harbor → the club; parkrun → the regular work trip. From the quilt, keep two things the trail lacks: the signature where users can see it, and the gift.
- **What it opens:** one blaze (a small shared mark for the way on at every threshold in OLOS, the same in every region, with a short list of allowed local variations); sections and overseers (every stretch of the route, whether a threshold, a page or a notice, has a named keeper who walks it a few times a season: "who keeps it in January?" by design); work trips (open labs as Saturday work trips, small real fixes, newcomers beside overseers); crews (each Cycle as a crew that takes on one big project and disbands, while the club stays); a register at every threshold; a work log that credits the unseen work.
- **Prototype by Nov 13:** give three thresholds on the journey map each a named overseer, put a simple register at each, and run one evening's work trip to clear what the registers show; ask three people whether they could tell who keeps the way, and whether they'd take a stretch themselves.
- **Who adopts it:** regional organizers (district managers), alumni (overseers), national OLOS (keeper of the standard), the people using it (who write in the register).
- **Watch for:** overseers burning out alone on a stretch nobody visits; an orphaned section; a standard that hardens until nothing can change; unseen work staying unseen. It can read as outdoorsy: the frame is the club, not the hike.
- **Where it differs:** a trail can't be copied or adapted the way code can, so the copy-and-send-back part of OLOS has no likeness here. You have to be there to work on a trail; much of OLOS can be worked on from anywhere. A trail changes slowly, and a relocation can take years; software changes weekly. And the trail is rooted in one place, while The Labs moves between libraries.
- **Feels like:** I can find my way because someone marked it, I know who keeps my stretch, and one day that someone might be me.

### How the strongest candidates compare

Our judgments, from the evidence above. "Partly" counts as a part. "Open-source work" asks how much of the open-source side of the quilt table a place shares.

| Place | Needs | Paradoxes | Open-source work | What it lacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The trail club | All five | All four | Most of it | It can't be copied, it changes slowly, and hikers rarely see the keepers |
| The quilting bee | All five | All four | Little of it | It holds the people, not the code; risks are the unpicked stitch, the pace, reading as quaint |
| A cathedral workshop | All five | All four | Most of it | Its crafts are professionals' life work, kept up over centuries (UNESCO 2020) |
| Community theatre | All five | Two, and parts of two | Some of it | Auditions and casting rank people; the show goes to an audience, not a person |
| A jazz jam session | All five | Two, and parts of two | Some of it | Nothing kept or given; the bandstand can shut people out (Lebert, ASU thesis) |
| The Camino | Four, and part of Belonging | Two, and part of a third | Little of it | Walked alone: no team, no project |
| Summer camp | Four, and part of Mattering | Two, and parts of two | Little of it | For children; residential |
| parkrun | Three, and parts of two | One, and parts of two | Little of it | No team; nothing made |
| A community garden | Three, and parts of two | Two, and parts of two | Some of it | Plots worked alone; nothing records who grew what |

## Four to try first

If the pod takes one whole frame, there are two to choose between: the quilting bee holds the people and the evening best, the trail club holds the open-source work best. A team could also take the trail club and borrow the bee's signatures and its gift. If the pod wants one per paradox, these four, each a part of both:

| Paradox | Frame | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | The Camino, with the ski resort's signs | Moves the hunch across the whole route; opens the most to build; the pod's own ground, the record. The CrossFit box does the same inside a night: build it with Scaffolding |
| P2 | The Film Credits, with the relay's handoffs | Takes ranking away instead of adding recognition; the relay shows that being seen in a needed part makes people give more |
| P3 | The Tailor's Shop | Asks least of organizers, because helping is the helper's next step |
| P4 | parkrun | Keeps the Cycle a sprint while belonging gets something that doesn't end |

They compose: one passport could carry the stamps and the credits, and the weekly open lab is where hosts stamp it. That could be one proposal for the whole bee or the whole club, or four that share one record.

## Limits

- This is desk research about other places. The analogies are evidence that a way of working meets a need somewhere else, not that it will work here.
- The evidence is uneven. Doulas, patient navigation, ASAP, referrals and relay swimming rest on trials or large datasets; the CrossFit finding is one comparison of two gyms; the Camino, the ski resort and parkrun rest mostly on how they run.
- The quilting bee rests on museum records and one interview study; the trail club on the Conservancy's and the clubs' own accounts.
- Every frame is an analogy, so every frame breaks somewhere. Each says where its likeness ends; test there first.
- The paradoxes are candidates. P1 is the pod's hunch rewritten; P2–P4 are ours.
- The frames the builders wrote on Nov 7 aren't recorded, so we can't say how these compare.
- One reader chose the places. Someone else would choose others.
