# Evidence for the Wayfinding pod site: recruiting, landing pages, animated infographics, honest research

**Logged:** 2026-11-06 (desk research; every link below was opened unless marked otherwise)
**Scope:** evidence that bears on a one-page HTML site that must (a) recruit three builders in residence (4–6 h/week, five weeks, nights and weekends, with a coding agent), (b) recruit one-task contributors, and (c) present small-n pod findings as animated infographics.
**Rules followed:** at most one direct quote per source, under 15 words; everything else paraphrased. Hack for LA is already in the library and is not re-cited here.

---

## 1. Volunteer recruiting online: what the evidence says

### The ask: personal beats broadcast, and most people wait to be asked

- Freeman (1997), analysing US survey data, found that a large share of volunteering happens because someone asked. He models volunteering as a "conscience good": people would rather someone else did it, but comply when asked directly. His phrase: volunteering is "something that people feel morally obligated to do when asked." Source: [Working for Nothing: The Supply of Volunteer Labor](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:5435), NBER Working Paper 5435 / *Journal of Labor Economics* 15(1), 1997.
- Gerber and Green (2000) randomised about 30,000 registered voters in New Haven before the November 1998 election to non-partisan get-out-the-vote contact by door-to-door canvass, direct mail, or phone. Canvassing raised turnout substantially, mail slightly, phone calls not at all. The abstract: "Voter turnout was increased substantially by personal canvassing." Source: [The Effects of Canvassing, Direct Mail, and Telephone Contact on Voter Turnout: A Field Experiment](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps00-001), *American Political Science Review* 94(3), 2000. (Exact percentage-point effects are in the paper body, not on the page opened; not reproduced here.)
- Implication worth noting, not a recommendation: a web page is a broadcast channel. Both sources point to the page as the thing a personal ask links to, not the ask itself.

### Identity framing ("be a builder" vs "build")

- Bryan, Walton, Rogers and Dweck (2011) ran three experiments in which a short survey differed only in wording: noun ("be a voter") vs verb ("vote"). Experiment 2 (California, 2008; n=88 matched to records): turnout 95.5% vs 81.8%. Experiment 3 (New Jersey, 2009; n=214 matched): 89.9% vs 79.0%. The samples are small and the participants were already survey panel members. Source: [Motivating voter turnout by invoking the self](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150938), *PNAS* 108(31), 2011.
- A later large field experiment by Gerber and colleagues did not replicate the effect. Bryan, Walton and Dweck replied (2016) that the replication used congressional primaries, of which they say only 4 of 61 were meaningfully competitive, so the identity stakes were absent; they call it "not an authentic replication." Source: [Bryan, Walton & Dweck, PNAS letter, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087036). Treat the noun effect as contested and context-dependent: it may need a stake that people already see as identity-relevant.

### Functional motives: match the message to the motive

- Clary, Snyder and colleagues (1998) built the Volunteer Functions Inventory around six functions that volunteering can serve for a person, and in a lab study found that persuasive messages worked better when they matched the reader's own motive than when mismatched. The abstract's claim: "VFI motivations predicted the persuasive appeal of messages better when message and motivation were matched than mismatched." Source: [Understanding and assessing the motivations of volunteers: A functional approach](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/understanding-and-assessing-the-motivations-of-volunteers-a-funct/), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 74(6), 1998. (The six function names were not on the page opened; the usual list is values, understanding, enhancement, career, social, protective. Unverified from this page.)

### Social proof / conditional cooperation

- Frey and Meier (2004) told 2,500 randomly selected University of Zurich students either that a high or a relatively low share of students had contributed to two social funds. Contributions rose with the reported share, but only for some "types" of people identified from their past giving. Their summary: "The more others cooperate, the more one is inclined to do so as well." Source: [Social Comparisons and Pro-social Behavior: Testing "Conditional Cooperation" in a Field Experiment](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/zuriewwpx/162.htm), IEW Working Paper 162, University of Zurich, 2004 (later *American Economic Review*). Note: showing a *low* number of participants is itself social information, and it cut giving in this study.

### Foot in the door: real, modest, not a funnel design

- Burger's 1999 review of the foot-in-the-door literature put the average effect around r = 0.17 (roughly d = 0.30), far below the headline figures in Freedman and Fraser's 1966 original. The summary page opened reports that under favourable conditions the technique adds about 5–15 percentage points, that some studies find nothing or a reversal, and calls it "one of the more replicable compliance techniques in social psychology." Source: [Foot-in-the-door: replication status](https://www.atticusli.com/replication-crisis/foot-in-the-door/) (secondary summary; Burger 1999, *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 3(4), is the primary and was not opened directly).

### Time-boxing and episodic volunteering

- A systematic review of episodic volunteering (41 articles screened, 20 analysed in depth) defines it as short-term, time-limited, often project- or event-based work. Common motives were supporting a cause, personal enhancement, and socialising. The review could not say whether episodic volunteers become ongoing ones and lists that as an open question; it proposes a model, not a finding. Its abstract: "Episodic volunteers are a critical resource … but are poorly understood." Source: [A systematic review of episodic volunteering in public health and other contexts](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2458-14-992), *BMC Public Health* 14:992, 2014.
- Gap: I found no controlled study on whether stating a fixed hours-per-week or a fixed end date raises sign-up. The "4–6 hours a week for five weeks" claim on the site is an untested assumption, not a known lever.

### Recruiting technical volunteers: open-source evidence

- Steinmacher and colleagues' systematic review (20 primary studies) found 15 barriers for newcomers in five groups: social interaction, prior knowledge, finding a way to start, documentation, technical hurdles. "Difficulty to find appropriate task to start with" appears in four studies; "Not receiving a (timely) answer" in five or six. They cite earlier work that "receiving timely responses, especially within 48 hours, was positively correlated with future participation." Source: [A systematic literature review on the barriers faced by newcomers to open source software projects](https://users.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/IST_SysReview_PrePrint.pdf), *Information and Software Technology*, 2015 (preprint).
- Tan and colleagues analysed 48,402 closed "good first issues" in 964 GitHub repositories. About 70% of these issues had an expert take part; the median one had one expert and one newcomer. Expert involvement raised the odds a newcomer solved the issue, but in the retention model it was *negatively* associated with the newcomer staying (estimate −0.74, and the model fits poorly, pseudo-R² 0.05). Half of the issues got a first expert reply within 8.5 hours; about 10% waited over 56 days. About 30% of communities routed mentoring through one expert, a single point of failure. Source: [Is It Enough to Recommend Tasks to Newcomers? Understanding Mentoring on Good First Issues](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.05058), ICSE 2023 (arXiv 2302.05058).
- A 2024 study of 1,155 GitHub projects (about 6.0 million pull requests) found good-first-issue tags roughly doubled or more the odds of a newcomer's first patch being accepted in medium and high-activity projects (OR 2.02 and 5.57), with no effect in low-activity ones, and found the tags helped non-newcomers about as much. The authors: "GFI tags significantly improve newcomers' odds of accepting a patch." Source: [From First Patch to Long-Term Contributor: Evaluating Onboarding Recommendations for OSS Newcomers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.04159), arXiv 2407.04159, 2024.
- Reading across the three: a labelled small task helps a first contribution land; a fast first reply matters; neither guarantees a second contribution. Whoever answers the "one small task" contributors within two days is a load-bearing part of the design.

## 2. Landing-page and call-to-action evidence

### One primary action

- GOV.UK's "Start using a service" pattern specifies a start page with a single button into the service and ordinary links for any secondary action. The page should say what the service does, what the person needs to have ready, roughly how long it takes, and other ways to do it; complex eligibility rules are kept out of the start page and asked inside. Research note on the page: DWP found that putting the start point inside a guide improved the Carer's Allowance journey because people saw context first. Rule on the page: "GOV.UK services must start on a GOV.UK content page." Source: [Help users to start using a service](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/start-using-a-service/), GOV.UK Design System.
- I did not find a Nielsen Norman Group article that states a "one CTA" rule as a research finding; the search returned secondary blogs, which I have left out.

### Above the fold vs long pages

- NN/g eye-tracking: in 2010 about 80% of viewing time was above the fold; in 2018 about 57%, with 74% within the first two screenfuls. The drop after the fold persists. Minimalist layouts can create a false floor so people think the page has ended. Advice: "Reserve the top of the page for high-priority content," keep major calls to action above the fold, and signal that more follows (for example cut-off text). Source: [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/), Nielsen Norman Group, 2018.

### Form length and friction

- Baymard's checkout benchmark: the average checkout had 11.3 form fields in 2024 (down from 12.7 in 2019); they say most need only 8. Their survey finding is that 17% of users abandoned because the process felt "too long/complicated" (the page metadata shows 18% and 22% in places; the figures conflict). Source: [Checkout flow: average form fields](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields), Baymard Institute.
- Baymard also reports 18% abandoning when forced to create an account. The survey year and n are not on the page opened. Source: [How to Reduce Cart Abandonment](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment), Baymard Institute. These are e-commerce numbers; carry them over to a volunteer form only as a direction, not a rate.
- GOV.UK's "one thing per page" (2015): split a form so each page asks one thing. Reasons given: easier for low-confidence users, works on mobile, handles errors, branching and saving progress. The evidence is qualitative lab research on Register to Vote and GOV.UK Verify; the author says there is no easily shareable quantitative data. Quote: "you should start with the first approach and split all your questions out on to separate pages." Source: [One thing per page](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2015/07/03/one-thing-per-page/), GDS Design Notes, 2015.

### Progressive disclosure / progressive commitment

- Nielsen: show a few core options first and reveal the rest on request. Benefits: focus, fewer errors, less scanning for experts. Risks: people never find the secondary options if the route is not visible; three or more levels of disclosure leave people lost. His line: "Progressive disclosure defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen." Source: [Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/), Nielsen Norman Group.
- I found no rigorous source using the exact phrase "progressive commitment" for sign-up flows; the nearest evidence is the foot-in-the-door literature (section 1) and the one-thing-per-page research above. Treat "small ask first, then the five-week ask" as a hypothesis supported only by modest effects.

## 3. Scrollytelling and animated infographics: what works and what fails

### Animation for understanding: the core findings

- Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt (2002) reviewed studies comparing animated with static graphics for learning and found no consistent advantage for animation; where animation seemed to help, it carried extra information, interactivity, or a prediction task. They give two principles: congruence (the graphic's structure should match the idea) and apprehension (it must be perceivable and understood). Their diagnosis: "Animations are often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived." Source: [Animation: can it facilitate?](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf), *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 57, 2002.
- Heer and Robertson (2007) tested animated transitions between chart states (for example scatter plot to bar chart) in two controlled experiments and concluded that "animated transitions can significantly improve graphical perception." Their worked example stages the transition: first move points to a dot plot, then grow them into bars, rather than interpolating directly. Source: [Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/animated-transitions), IEEE InfoVis 2007. The reconciliation with Tversky et al.: animation helps when it shows *how one state becomes another*, not as decoration on a static fact.
- Amini and colleagues (2018) built a five-factor viewer-engagement questionnaire and tested it on Mechanical Turk with data videos that varied animation and pictographs. Both affected engagement but on different factors; the abstract reports no comprehension result. "Each technique has an effect on viewer engagement, impacting different factors." Source: [Hooked on Data Videos: Assessing the Effect of Animation and Pictographs on Viewer Engagement](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/publications/details/hooked-on-data-videos-assessing-the-effect-of-animation-and-pictographs-on), AVI 2018. Participant count not on the page opened.

### Scrollytelling research

- McKenna and colleagues (2017) analysed 80 published data stories, ran exploratory studies, then a crowdsourced study with 240 participants. They name seven "flow-factors" (navigation input, level of control, navigation progress, story layout, role of visualization, story progression, navigation feedback). Visuals and navigation feedback affected engagement; level of control (stepper vs continuous scroll) did not show a comparable effect. Source: [Visual Narrative Flow: Exploring Factors Shaping Data Visualization Story Reading Experiences](https://vdl.sci.utah.edu/publications/2017_eurovis_narrative-flow/), EuroVis 2017.
- Seyser and Zeiller (2018) catalogued which infographic types appear in long-form scrollytelling journalism and how they are integrated. The abstract calls infographics "a powerful tool to communicate complex information" but reports no results; the full paper was not opened. Source: [Scrollytelling: An Analysis of Visual Storytelling in Online Journalism](https://people.hochschule-burgenland.at/entities/publication/360687e9-e683-431c-b576-7f372b279621/details), IEEE IV 2018.

### NN/g on scroll-triggered animation: named failure modes

From usability sessions reported in [Scroll Fading 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-fading-101/), [Parallax Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/) and [Animation for Attention and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/), Nielsen Norman Group:

1. **Too slow to arrive.** Fades of about 500 ms or more led people to scroll past before content appeared. NN/g: "Users are likely to skip over text if it fades in too slowly." Recommended range 100–400 ms.
2. **Replays on scroll-back.** Content that re-fades each time someone scrolls up (their example: a calculator whose outputs kept vanishing) reads as broken.
3. **False floor.** Content that fades in far below the fold makes the page look finished; people leave.
4. **Blank screens on fast scroll.** Parallax assets that load late produced near-empty screens; one tester had to scroll past and back to read each section.
5. **Scroll-speed-linked text.** When motion is tied to scroll velocity, text leaves before it is read ("that happened so quickly I didn't even get to read it" is NN/g's participant, not quoted here as a source quote).
6. **Motion sickness.** Heavy movement, especially of text, caused dizziness; NN/g points to Apple's 2013 Reduce Motion setting as the precedent.
7. **Peripheral and decorative motion.** Sliding elements at the edges and "fun" effects distract goal-directed adults; animations that replay on every visit with no skip wear people down. NN/g's rule: "Just because you can implement an animation, it doesn't mean that you should."
8. **Mobile.** A thumb covers subtle effects; small screens multiply whitespace and scrolling; NN/g advises against scroll fading on mobile outright.
9. **Stacking effects.** Scroll fading plus scrolljacking caused confusion and overload.

NN/g's positive cases: feedback within 0.1 s of a user action; showing continuity between two states; showing relationships (a field expanding under the field that triggered it). Both the Heer–Robertson result and these cases point the same way: animate the *change*, once, briefly.

### Accessibility: the two criteria that apply

- **WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A).** Any moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds and sits alongside other content must be pausable, stoppable or hideable; auto-updating content likewise. Exceptions: movement that is essential, or content shown alone (a loading screen). Intent: "Let users control content changes that occur in parallel with other content." Source: [Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html), W3C.
- **WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (Level AAA).** Motion triggered by interaction (scrolling counts) must be switchable off unless essential. The `prefers-reduced-motion` media query is listed as a sufficient technique (C39 for CSS, SCR40 for JavaScript). Rationale: "Non-essential movement can trigger vestibular disorder reactions." Source: [Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html), W3C.
- **Implementation note.** `prefers-reduced-motion` has values `reduce` and `no-preference`; it works in CSS, in `matchMedia`, and in `<picture>` (serve a static image unless motion is allowed). For users who opt out, remove parallax, zoom and autoplay; keep essential feedback. When forcing reduced motion by stylesheet, shorten durations to about 1 ms rather than `animation: none`, because some pages wait on `animationend`. Source: [prefers-reduced-motion: Sometimes less movement is more](https://web.dev/articles/prefers-reduced-motion), web.dev. (This is a Google developer-education page, not peer-reviewed.)

### Performance and mobile

- I did not find a credible primary source specifically on scroll-driven-animation performance in the time available; the search returned vendor and SEO blogs, which I have left out. The mobile failure modes above (NN/g) are the verified evidence. Unverified but standard practice: animate only `transform` and `opacity`, and avoid layout-triggering properties on scroll.

## 4. Showing research honestly

### Small-n presentation

- NN/g's own rule for five-user studies: they are formative, meant to find problems to fix, not to measure a population. Do not report success rates, averages or percentages from them as if they generalise; describe what specific participants did, and rank issues by severity and context, not by how often they appeared. Reserve percentages and confidence intervals for studies built to measure them (usually 30–40+ participants). Source: [Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/5-test-users-qual-quant/), Nielsen Norman Group. For a pod with roughly a dozen informants, this argues for "7 of 12 said…" at most, and for counts rather than percentages in any infographic.

### Why authors drop uncertainty, and what it costs

- Hullman (2019) surveyed 90 visualization authors and interviewed 13. Of those who left out uncertainty they had meant to show, 62% said they did not want to confuse viewers, 47% lacked the uncertainty information, 26% did not know how to compute it, and 17% did not want the data to look questionable. Most said uncertainty should be shown more often, and still omitted it. She frames fixing this as "a long-term strategic problem." Source: [Why Authors Don't Visualize Uncertainty](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.01697), IEEE VIS 2019 (arXiv 1908.01697).
- Hullman, Resnick and Adar (2015): hypothetical outcome plots, which animate one draw from a distribution at a time, let untrained observers judge which of two trends produced a sample at lower levels of evidence than static error bars or line ensembles. Their result: observers could "correctly infer the underlying trend in samples conveying a lower level of evidence." Source: [Hypothetical Outcome Plots Help Untrained Observers Judge Trends in Ambiguous Data](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends), *PLOS ONE*, 2015. This is one of the few places where animation has a measured comprehension benefit, and it is animation *of uncertainty*, not of a fixed number.

### "From the problem owner, to be tested" framing: precedents in government design

- A UK government service team phrased each assumption as a plain statement plus the consequence if wrong, scored impact and confidence out of 10, computed risk as impact × (10 − confidence), and tested in that order. Their tag line: "Hunches are only dangerous when we don't test them." Source: [Prioritise the riskiest assumptions in big problem spaces](https://services.blog.gov.uk/2022/11/03/prioritise-the-riskiest-assumptions-in-big-problem-spaces), GOV.UK Services blog, 2022.
- A Department for Education team wrote five design hypotheses in the form "We believe that… we feel that… will result in…", logged every interview's findings against each hypothesis on a shared board, tagged to anonymised participant codes, and said the format "felt natural to the team." Source: [How we are using design hypotheses](https://design-histories.education.gov.uk/check-an-early-years-qualification/how-we-are-using-design-hypotheses), DfE design histories. Both are practice write-ups, not studies; they show a published vocabulary for labelling a finding as a hypothesis that came from someone and awaits a test.

## 5. Examples worth looking at

Pages I opened. One line each on what they do; "animation" notes are from the text capture, so motion claims are limited.

1. [GOV.UK Design System: Community](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/) — opens with "Everyone can help improve the Design System"; lists small contributions (flag a defect, fix a page) beside large ones (propose a component), with published criteria for the latter. No animation; structure from headings.
2. [mySociety: Get involved](https://www.mysociety.org/get-involved/) — three routes (volunteer, contribute code to named projects, join a list), framed as collective effort and "as much or as little time as you like"; one primary action, a mailing-list sign-up.
3. [Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org/) — "People-Powered Research"; the whole proposition is one small task repeated; the home page leads with partner logos rather than a sign-up button, which is worth noticing as a choice, not a model.
4. [The Pudding: Film Dialogue](https://pudding.cool/2017/03/film-dialogue/) — data essay that states its method and limits in the body (100-word matching threshold, IMDB labelling, 90% coverage); charts are filterable rather than scroll-driven. Good precedent for showing limits inline.
5. [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/) — publication of visual data essays; useful as the reference point for what "scrollytelling done by specialists" costs and looks like.
6. [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/) — 14,000+ charts, each paired with a short written insight; interactive on click, static preview by default, no animation. The strongest example of research presented without motion.
7. [Parametric Press](https://parametric.press/) — issue-based interactive essays (current: The Climate Issue, five essays); the page opened does not describe its interaction model, so look at an essay directly.
8. [GOV.UK: Start using a service pattern](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/start-using-a-service/) — not a recruiting page but the clearest published spec for a page whose only job is to get someone to press one button, with what to say before it.

Not opened (fetch required approval that was not available during this run): Mozilla's contribute page, Code for America's brigade network page, and explorabl.es. They are plausible candidates for the pod to check by hand.
