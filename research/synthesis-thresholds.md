# Synthesis: what the research says about the thresholds

*A reading of the research library (54 cases, 11 theory files, one internal record) against the product The Labs is building: modular kits run by any lab, the Cycle as the flagship kit, and the pre-, post- and between-cycle experience that does not yet exist. Written Nov 6, 2026 for the Wayfinding pod. This is one reading, by one reader, offered as material for Saturday. The paradox and the frames are the pod's to write. Every claim below traces to a file in this folder; check the file before leaning on a number.*

## The theory, in one page

Five bodies of work explain why people are lost at thresholds and what carries them across.

**People move through a place by its landmarks and paths (Lynch), and keep going only while the scent stays warm (Pirolli & Card).** A newcomer builds a mental map from a few fixed things. The Labs currently offers one landmark, the Cycle, with no visible paths to it or from it. Workshops are what most people experience and they do not read as a route.

**People persist at unpaid things when the setting feeds three needs: competence, belonging, and choice (Deci & Ryan).** The one direct study of volunteers found competence and belonging predicted staying; choice on its own did not. Across 344 samples, the kind of reason that predicts *persistence* is not "this is fun" but "this matters to me," which is the rung people climb to over time. Rewards for things people already wanted to do can crowd that out (128 studies).

**Self-taught never means alone (Tough, Candy).** Adults plan most of their own learning and still draw on about ten helpers per project. Capacity for self-direction depends on the situation, not the person; the same person is autonomous in one domain and lost in another.

**You learn a community by doing small real work at its edge, witnessed (Lave & Wenger).** The boundaries *between* communities are where much learning happens (Akkerman & Bakker). Self-managed groups that claim no structure run on hidden structure, and newcomers get in through a sponsor and spare time (Freeman).

**Working memory is small (Sweller), and the cost of "what's next" is a cost of packaging, not content.** A long menu does not on average stop people choosing (50 experiments, mean effect near zero), but a menu with non-comparable options and no default does. A visible head start speeds completion ("endowed progress"); an if-then plan with a concrete cue roughly doubles follow-through (94 tests); a broken streak is recoverable if repair is visible.

Put together: **a threshold is crossed when a person can see the next step, can see that they are ready, and can see who is going with them.** Each threshold at The Labs can be tested against those three.

## What the cases say about each part of the product

### Kits outside the Cycle (workshops, trainings, project nights)

- **Kits run short of their design, and the kit outlives the training.** In a randomized trial of Code Club, only 3 of 22 clubs reached the second project unit and half did not run the intended three-term model; most "volunteer-led" clubs were one teacher alone. Fewer than half of 4-H volunteers could name the learning model their project books assume; their last training was about five years earlier.
- **Self-serve content without a room fails.** Software Carpentry's online course finished 5–10% of starters; the two-day workshop filled rooms. Charging a small fee cut no-shows from a third to about 5%.
- **Branching catalogs get replaced by single paths, and the self-directed minority loses.** Duolingo (2022) and Khan Academy both retired a map in favor of a line because most learners could not tell the "right" way through. The people who objected were the self-directed learners. A linear feed then hid the overview and separated review from the route.
- **Modular catalogs with volunteer experts choke on expert supply and drift into "badge mills."** Scouting's own complaint form lists counselors signing off requirements never completed. About 6% of eligible Scouts reach the top rank in the best years.
- **Structure helps only with people attached.** Guided pathways in community colleges raised early momentum where fully implemented, but a mandatory plan on its own correlated slightly negatively with persistence.

*What this suggests for a kit:* a kit is not the document. It is the document plus a person trained to run it plus a room plus a visible next kit. The Labs cannot assume paid county educators (4-H), university hosts (Carpentries) or a school timetable (Code Club).

### The Cycle as a shared clock

- **The fixed sprint burns out the volunteers who run it.** FIRST retired its fixed six-week build window in 2018, citing mentor workload. Most teams that fold do so because no one will lead them, not for lack of money. NaNoWriMo (400,000 participants, several hundred volunteer regional leads, a staff of three) ran deficits four years in six and shut down in 2025 after pushing liability onto its volunteers and banning their local channels; local regions survived on their own.
- **What happens in the sprint predicts little; what was prepared before, and who was matched, predicts survival.** Across 73 hackathons, 35% of projects had any commit afterward and 3.6% were alive at five months. Technical preparation before the event and skill diversity on the team predicted continuation; stated intent to continue did not, and heavy first-week activity raised the chance of stopping.
- **The clock excludes people who cannot give a season.** Google Summer of Code widened to non-students and stretchable deadlines in 2022 because a fixed summer excluded people with jobs. The Labs' own alumni survey has the same note: one respondent asked for routes that let people watch from the sidelines or play smaller roles depending on availability.
- **The off-season is where teams recruit, train and get ready.** FIRST's mentor guide treats May–September as recruiting and training time; veteran teams lend robots to pre-rookie teams before kickoff.

*What this suggests:* the Cycle's length and intensity are organizer-side costs as much as participant-side ones. Who is matched and what is prepared before day one matters more than anything inside the twelve weeks.

### Pre-cycle (not yet designed)

- **In most programs the pre-stage is a gate because the thing beyond it is scarce.** Outreachy's contribution period filters 1,936 applicants to 121 funded interns; pre-apprenticeship exists because apprenticeship slots are limited; summer melt is about a college seat already won. The Cycle is not scarce that way. A pre-cycle at The Labs is not for choosing people in. It is for getting them ready and attached.
- **Prior contribution predicts staying; readiness without attachment does not.** Google Summer of Code students who had contributed before the program were the ones who stayed after. Pre-apprenticeship completers entered apprenticeships at 63% in one federal program and 8% in another, depending on whether slots and relationships existed on the other side.
- **Nudges work only when a known local person is behind them.** Summer-melt text messages cut melt in two cities with counselors behind the texts, did nothing in a city that already had support, and failed when scaled statewide with no advisor behind the number. Peer mentors alone had no measurable effect.
- **The first rung is the steepest, and the gate itself leaks.** A large share of Outreachy applicants dropped at the eligibility form. A Kubernetes intern with a full-time mentor still found the pre-member stage dominated by scale and fragmentation. The Labs' own alumni survey: "onboarding was tricky and joining projects was not obvious."

*What this suggests:* a pre-cycle is small real work, witnessed, with a named person, and a concrete plan for day one ("if it's Tuesday at 6, I go to the branch"). Not a course.

### Post- and between-cycle (not yet designed)

- **The cliff is universal and intent does not predict who stays.** 57% of Google Summer of Code students intended to continue; about 16% did. 98% intended in 2022. Hackathon intent did not predict survival. AmeriCorps' four-year effects were half gone by year eight; 72% of Teach For America teachers were out of teaching by year five.
- **The ties survive the program; the activity does not.** The Labs' alumni say the most valuable thing was people, and 7 of 11 team members are still in touch with their team months later. What ended was the shared work.
- **Ladders fill with ghosts unless there is a visible way down.** Kubernetes pruned 146 inactive members in 2020, over 600 in 2023 and 300 in 2025; a third of those removed had been owners, leaving empty teams; its activity meter cannot see organizing or moderation work. Apache's rungs have no published thresholds.
- **Survival by handover, not by team.** Both continued projects at a corporate hackathon were taken over by other units; one had no original member left. A maintained project at The Labs may need to outlive its founding team to survive at all.
- **The between is what holds a volunteer clock up.** NaNoWriMo's regions ran their own events between Novembers and kept going after the center closed. FIRST teams meet once or twice a week through summer. Recurse Center's "Never Graduate" keeps alumni access open with no obligation.

*What this suggests:* the post-cycle route is not "keep your project going." It is a visible set of places to go next (a maintained project to join, a workshop to teach, the next Cycle's prep, a role in running things) plus a visible, honorable way to step back without disappearing.

### Facilitators, trainers, organizers

- **The one leader is the single point of failure everywhere:** Code Club, FIRST, 4-H, NaNoWriMo's regional leads, open-source maintainers (60% have quit or considered it; the xz Utils handover). A third of trained Carpentries instructors never taught.
- **Organizer delay becomes participant emergency** (the organizers' own account, #4), and the C02 calendar shows the "Communicating in Slack" session marked "didn't happen too early in the cycle."
- **Organizers cannot see who is active.** Extinction Rebellion said so outright; Python had 20,000 list subscribers and no idea who participated; Indivisible's map showed 6,000 groups where 2,000–3,000 existed.

*What this suggests:* the kit has to make the runner's job visible and small, and the system has to show organizers who is stuck before participants feel it. The route into running things (participant → teach-back → facilitator → organizer) is itself a threshold with no map.

### Channels (multi-headed, headless OLOS)

- **One record, many doors, works:** NPR's COPE, GetCalFresh's text reminders, Python's consolidation onto one forum, GOV.UK's step-by-step maps across departments.
- **Automated first contact and private-only help hurt:** Wikipedia newcomers whose first contact was a bot left; Stack Overflow shrank as questions moved into private AI chats; two government chatbots gave wrong answers with legal consequences.
- **A single-channel rule breaks the local layer:** NaNoWriMo banned local Discord and Facebook groups; volunteers left.
- **Notifications fatigue; batching helps, and a message from a known person outperforms one from a system** (summer melt; channel-overload experiments).

*What this suggests:* OLOS as source of truth, people as the sender. At thresholds especially, a person must meet a person.

## What the internal evidence confirms

The alumni survey (12 respondents, C01–C03, Sep–Oct 2026) names the same thresholds the outside research does, in participants' own words: joining was not obvious; participants did not understand what to do next; the move from individual work into a team pod needed a roadmap; people moved in and out of teams with nothing holding them; one asked for routes with different roles for different availability. Nine of twelve "one thing you'd change" answers were about thresholds. "Most valuable" was almost entirely people. That matches the organizers' account (#4) and the C02 calendar annotation. What is still missing is anyone who got lost at the first door and never came back; they do not answer alumni surveys.

## Candidate paradoxes, for the pod to test against its own reading

Written in the *because* form. These are candidates, not the pod's paradox.

1. **The Cycle has to be an intense, time-boxed sprint, because deadlines are what make people ship together. The sprint is what burns out the volunteers who run it and what most people cannot fit, because it assumes a season of free evenings.** (FIRST, NaNoWriMo, GSoC 2022, the alumni who asked for sideline roles.)
2. **People need a visible single path, because a map most people cannot read gets replaced by a line. The people The Labs most wants, the self-directed ones, are the ones who lose when the map goes.** (Duolingo, Khan Academy, guided pathways.)
3. **A pre-cycle has to be real work with a real person, because readiness without attachment does not carry anyone across. Real work with a real person is exactly what organizers do not have the hours to give before a Cycle starts.** (Outreachy's mentor overwhelm, summer melt, #4.)
4. **The post-cycle has to offer a visible place to go, because intent to continue predicts nothing. Most of what people value survives anyway, because it was the people, not the project.** (GSoC, hackathons, the alumni survey.)
5. **OLOS has to reach people where they are, because one channel breaks the local layer. The message that moves someone across a threshold has to come from a person, because automated first contact loses them.** (NaNoWriMo, Wikipedia, summer melt.)

Underneath several of these: **seeing the route, seeing you are ready, seeing who goes with you.** Whether that is the theme, and which paradox the pod owns, is Saturday's work.

## What this synthesis cannot tell you

- Whether C04 has already fixed any of this. All internal evidence is from C01–C03 and from organizers.
- Who got lost at the first door. No early-exit responses exist; the alumni who answered are the ones who stayed.
- Whether The Labs' thresholds behave like the cases'. Every case assumed something The Labs cannot: paid staff, children, a school calendar, a scarce prize, a captive population. Each file names its assumption.
- Numbers were read through a page-summarizing tool. Spot-check before anything goes on a slide.
