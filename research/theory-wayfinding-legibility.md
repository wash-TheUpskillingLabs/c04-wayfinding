# Theory: Wayfinding, legibility and information scent

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, interfaces
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand is about how people find their way through places and information spaces they do not yet know: what mental picture they build of the place, how they make route decisions, and which cues tell them a path is worth following. It started in urban design (Lynch) and environmental psychology (Passini), and was carried into screens by human-computer interaction researchers (Darken and Sibert; Pirolli and Card). All four treat getting lost as a design and information problem, not a personal failing.

## Works

### Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City* (1960)
- **Link:** [Wikipedia summary with bibliographic data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Image_of_the_City) (MIT Press, 1960, ISBN 0-262-62001-4); [MIT Libraries, "150 Years in the Stacks" entry](https://libraries.mit.edu/150books/2011/04/16/1960/); [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4477193W).
- **Core idea:** People who live in a city carry mental maps of it. Lynch says these maps are built from five kinds of element, and that a city is easier to use when they are easy to pick out. The book came from a five-year study of Boston, Jersey City and Los Angeles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Image_of_the_City)). It drew on the 1954–1959 "Perceptual Form of the City" study, in which researchers asked people in Boston to give directions between named points and describe what they would see ([MIT Libraries](https://libraries.mit.edu/150books/2011/04/16/1960/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *paths*: channels people move along.
  - *edges*: boundaries, real or perceived.
  - *districts*: areas with a shared character that you enter and leave.
  - *nodes*: focal points you can enter, from which other elements can be seen.
  - *landmarks*: memorable reference points you look at but don't enter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Image_of_the_City)).
  - *imageability / legibility*: how easily a place can be pictured, and how easily its parts can be recognised and fitted together.
- **Touches:** Lynch's subjects were people describing a place they already used. The model describes how someone holds a whole territory in their head. It does not describe the first visit. Nielsen Norman Group applies Lynch's districts and landmarks to digital products, to help users tell where they are and where they can go ([NN/g video, 2021](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/digital-wayfinding/)).
- **Critiques and limits:** Lynch revisited the study himself in "Reconsidering *The Image of the City*" ([Springer chapter in *Cities of the Mind*, 1984, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-9697-1_9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-9697-1_9)). *Unverified: we confirmed the chapter exists but did not read it, so its content is not summarised here.* Fredric Jameson took the term "cognitive mapping" from Lynch and moved it from streets to a person's place in social and economic structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Image_of_the_City)). That move suggests that a clear picture of the physical layout does not by itself show someone where they stand. Donald Appleyard's follow-up study of how different residents structure a city ([*Environment and Behavior*, 1970, DOI 10.1177/001391657000200106](https://doi.org/10.1177/001391657000200106)) is often read as a qualification of Lynch. *Unverified: we did not read the article, so its findings are not reported here.*

### Paul Arthur and Romedi Passini, *Wayfinding: People, Signs, and Architecture* (1992), and Passini, "Wayfinding design" (1996)
- **Link:** [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/367500) (McGraw-Hill, 1992, 252 pp., ISBN 0075510162); Passini, ["Wayfinding design: logic, application and some thoughts on universality," *Design Studies* 17(3):319–331, 1996, DOI 10.1016/0142-694X(96)00001-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0142-694X(96)00001-4).
- **Core idea:** Arthur and Passini treat wayfinding as solving a spatial problem, not as reading a map. The book covers three channels of wayfinding help: architecture, graphics, and spoken or other human interaction. It explains why people have trouble finding their way and how the process works ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/367500)). In a summary by later researchers, Passini's model breaks wayfinding into three sub-processes: *decision-making*, *decision-execution* and *information-processing*. Decisions are nested. An overall goal ("get to the destination") is reached through intermediate decisions, which are reached through immediate decisions that turn directly into actions. His studies in Montreal shopping centres found that complex buildings left newcomers, and even some frequent visitors, uncertain and stressed ([Hao and Yen, DRS 2008, Sheffield Hallam repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/527/1/fulltext.pdf)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *decision plan*: the hierarchy of decisions from the overall goal down to the next step.
  - *decision-execution*: turning a decision into movement.
  - *wayfinding protocol*: having people think aloud while finding their way. Passini brought this method into wayfinding research ([Hao and Yen](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/527/1/fulltext.pdf)).
- **Touches:** Describes people who are new to a complex environment, and what happens at each decision point: what they need to see and hear, and from whom (signs, the building itself, or a person). Arthur and Passini name human interaction as one of the three channels, alongside architecture and graphics ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/367500)).
- **Critiques and limits:** Hao and Yen call Passini's model "rigid" and strongly cognitivist. They say it assumes that enough information produces a decision and then a behaviour. They also find that some lower-level wayfinding moves are improvised or automatic rather than planned. They note that Passini studied trips to known destinations, not people setting off from any point to any point ([Hao and Yen](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/527/1/fulltext.pdf)).

### Peter Pirolli and Stuart Card, "Information Foraging" (1999)
- **Link:** [*Psychological Review* 106(4):643–675, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643); [PhilPapers record](https://philpapers.org/rec/PIRIF); practitioner explainer: [Budiu, "Information Scent: How Users Decide Where to Go Next," NN/g, 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/).
- **Core idea:** The theory models people looking for information on the pattern of animals foraging for food. They weigh how much a path is likely to yield against what it costs to follow it. The key working concept is *information scent*: the cues a person picks up about what lies behind a link or option before choosing it. In the NN/g account, scent comes from the label, the context around it, and what the person already knows about the source, including recommendations from other people ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *information scent*: cues that predict what lies down a path.
  - *patch*: a cluster of information worth staying in.
  - *social foraging*: networks of people foraging together and passing on tips ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/)).
- **Touches:** Describes the choices people make when several routes are on offer, such as a menu of programs, roles or channels. It also explains why people leave a path when its cues look weak.
- **Critiques and limits:** The NN/g explainer notes that scent depends on prior knowledge. Background cues that help an experienced visitor are not available to someone new to the domain ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/)). So the same labels can carry strong scent for insiders and none for newcomers. The model also assumes people roughly optimise gain against cost. *We found no formal critique of that assumption in this pass.*

### Rudolph Darken and John Sibert, "Wayfinding strategies and behaviors in large virtual worlds" (1996); Bachiochi et al., "Usability studies and designing navigational aids for the World Wide Web" (1997)
- **Link:** [CHI '96 proceedings, pp. 142–149, DOI 10.1145/238386.238459](https://doi.org/10.1145/238386.238459); [Bachiochi et al., WWW6 proceedings, 1997](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www6/proceedings/Technical/Paper180/Paper180.html).
- **Core idea:** Darken and Sibert tested whether people use physical-world wayfinding strategies in large virtual spaces. Subjects given no extra environmental cues were often disoriented and had great difficulty finishing a search task. Where cues were present, they shaped people's strategies in the ways the design principles predicted ([CHI '96 abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/238386.238459)). Bachiochi et al., citing Darken, framed wayfinding on the web as four questions a traveller asks: where am I, where do I want to go, am I on the right path, and am I there yet ([WWW6](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www6/proceedings/Technical/Paper180/Paper180.html)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *wayfinding vs. navigation*: purposeful, oriented movement vs. working out a path ([WWW6](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www6/proceedings/Technical/Paper180/Paper180.html)).
  - *the four orientation questions*: where am I, where do I want to go, am I on the right path, am I there yet.
- **Touches:** Describes what happens when one territory is reached through several surfaces (web, chat, email, SMS, agent). Each surface has to answer the four questions on its own, or the person loses their bearings.
- **Critiques and limits:** Both are lab and usability studies from the 1990s. The task was finding a known target, not deciding which goal to pursue. Bachiochi et al. derived numeric usability limits (four page changes, 60 seconds) from their own tests ([WWW6](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www6/proceedings/Technical/Paper180/Paper180.html)). Those numbers come from one study and are not general findings.

## Questions for the pod

- Passini's newcomers lost their way at decision points. Which decision points between workshop, Cycle, pod and maintained project have no sign, no built cue and no person present?
