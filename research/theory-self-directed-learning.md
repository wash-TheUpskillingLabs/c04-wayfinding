# Theory: Self-directed learning and autodidaxy

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, post-cycle, facilitators, interfaces, activation-mobilization
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand asks how adults learn when no one has set a curriculum for them: how much of their learning they plan themselves, what help they look for, and what institutions or networks could support it. It runs from adult-education research in the 1960s–70s (Tough, Knowles), through a conceptual cleanup (Candy, who named *autodidaxy*), to proposals that go further than self-direction (heutagogy) or beyond schools altogether (Illich, connectivism). Its critics keep raising the same objection: the "self" in self-directed learning is always embedded in a social setting and in relations of power.

## Works

### Allen Tough, *The Adult's Learning Projects* (1971)
- **Link:** [Full text by chapter on the author's site](https://www.allentough.com/books/alp.htm) (OISE, Toronto, 1971); [Tough interviewed by Donaghy, 2005](https://www.allentough.com/learning/donaghy.htm); [Tough's interview protocol, hosted by R. Hiemstra](https://roghiemstra.com/toughschedule.htm).
- **Core idea:** Tough counted *learning projects*: deliberate efforts to learn something that pass a minimum-hours threshold. His interview protocol counts an effort only if enough hours were spent on it within a set period ([protocol](https://roghiemstra.com/toughschedule.htm)). He sorted projects by who did the planning: a group with a teacher, one-to-one with a teacher, peers without a teacher, or the person alone. About 70 per cent were planned by the learners themselves. He compared organised courses to the visible tip of an iceberg ([Donaghy interview](https://www.allentough.com/learning/donaghy.htm)). His original study expected self-teachers to work alone. Instead they got help from an average of ten or eleven people per thing learned. Tough concluded that self-directed learners may be more social than students in a class ([Donaghy interview](https://www.allentough.com/learning/donaghy.htm)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *learning project*: a sustained, deliberate effort to learn that passes an hours threshold.
  - *planner*: whoever decides content and method from one session to the next.
  - *helpers*: the many people a self-directed learner draws on.
  - *the iceberg*: courses are the visible part of adult learning.
- **Touches:** Describes the learning people do between and outside organised sessions, and the network of helpers they gather. Most of that learning is invisible to the institution that offers the sessions.
- **Critiques and limits:** Tough's definition of a learning project is a counting rule. Hiemstra notes that some researchers disagree with it ([protocol page](https://roghiemstra.com/toughschedule.htm)). Smith summarises work by Spear and Mocker: self-directed learners often pick from the limited options in front of them rather than plan ahead. Smith also calls the empirical base thin, made up of small studies whose cross-cultural reach is questionable ([infed, "Self-direction in learning"](https://infed.org/dir/self-direction-in-learning/)).

### Malcolm Knowles, *Self-Directed Learning: A Guide for Learners and Teachers* (1975), and andragogy
- **Link:** [ERIC ED114653](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED114653) (Association Press, 1975, 135 pp.); [Smith, "Andragogy," infed](https://infed.org/mobi/andragogy-what-is-it-and-does-it-help-thinking-about-adult-learning/).
- **Core idea:** The guide has three parts: the learner, the teacher, and learning resources. An appendix gives guidelines for *contract learning* ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED114653)). Knowles defined self-directed learning as a process in which individuals take the initiative to diagnose their own needs, set goals, find resources, choose strategies and evaluate outcomes ([infed, self-direction](https://infed.org/dir/self-direction-in-learning/)). His andragogy rests on assumptions about adults. Self-concept moves from dependence toward self-direction. Experience becomes a resource. Readiness to learn follows social roles. Orientation shifts to problems that need solving now. Motivation becomes internal; Knowles added this last assumption later ([infed, andragogy](https://infed.org/mobi/andragogy-what-is-it-and-does-it-help-thinking-about-adult-learning/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *learning contract*: a learner-written plan of objectives, resources and evidence.
  - *readiness to learn*: tied to the roles a person is taking on.
  - *problem-centred orientation*.
- **Touches:** Describes adults arriving with their own goals and experience. It assumes they can diagnose what they need and find the resources for it.
- **Critiques and limits:** Hartree argued that it is unclear whether andragogy is a theory of learning or of teaching, and whether its assumptions describe adults or prescribe how to treat them ([Hartree, *International Journal of Lifelong Education* 3(3), 1984, DOI 10.1080/0260137840030304](https://doi.org/10.1080/0260137840030304); summarised by [infed](https://infed.org/mobi/andragogy-what-is-it-and-does-it-help-thinking-about-adult-learning/)). Tennant pointed out that children are also self-directed. Jarvis called the contrast with pedagogy crude. Humphries warned that treating social roles as given accepts existing relations as legitimate ([infed, andragogy](https://infed.org/mobi/andragogy-what-is-it-and-does-it-help-thinking-about-adult-learning/)). Knowles's linear five-step model is criticised for not matching how people actually learn ([infed, self-direction](https://infed.org/dir/self-direction-in-learning/)).

### Philip C. Candy, *Self-Direction for Lifelong Learning* (1991)
- **Link:** [CiNii Books record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA1264910X?l=en) (Jossey-Bass, 1991, foreword by S. Brookfield); [review by Judith Fretz, *Canadian Journal for the Study of Adult Education* 7(1), May 1993](https://cjsae.library.dal.ca/cjsae/article/download/2242/1957/2747).
- **Core idea:** Candy argued that "self-directed learning" had been used for four different things. Two are processes: *learner-controlled instruction* inside formal institutions, and *autodidaxy* outside them. Two are personal attributes: *self-management* and *personal autonomy*. He held that running the four together had produced confusion ([Fretz review](https://cjsae.library.dal.ca/cjsae/article/download/2242/1957/2747)). In autodidaxy, the learner sees themselves as in control even when they ask for help. On this account, people who assist autodidacts are outside instruction and should respect the learner's sense of ownership. Candy also argued that autonomy depends on context. Someone autonomous in one domain is not automatically autonomous in another. And simply handing learners freedom can backfire if they lack the skills or confidence, or would rather be taught ([Fretz review](https://cjsae.library.dal.ca/cjsae/article/download/2242/1957/2747)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *autodidaxy*: self-directed learning outside formal institutions.
  - *learner-controlled instruction*: self-direction inside a program.
  - *situational autonomy*: autonomy specific to a context, not a general trait.
- **Touches:** Distinguishes people charting their own course outside any program from participants given choices inside one. It also describes what helpers do when the learner, not the helper, owns the project.
- **Critiques and limits:** Fretz found that Candy's psychological, constructivist base made it hard to bring in the social side of learning. She called his treatment of social barriers cursory, and noted that his research agenda falls back on the individual ([Fretz review](https://cjsae.library.dal.ca/cjsae/article/download/2242/1957/2747)). Candy acknowledged some of this himself, as quoted in the review.

### Stewart Hase and Chris Kenyon, "From andragogy to heutagogy" (2000)
- **Link:** [Southern Cross University research portal record](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Fromandragogy-to-heutagogy/991012821597602368) (*ultiBASE*, December 2000); review: [Blaschke, *IRRODL* 13(1), 2012, DOI 10.19173/irrodl.v13i1.1076](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v13i1.1076) ([open PDF](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1076/2113)).
- **Core idea:** Hase and Kenyon proposed going beyond andragogy to "truly self-determined learning," which they called heutagogy, with the aim of building learners' *capability* ([SCU record](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Fromandragogy-to-heutagogy/991012821597602368)). Blaschke explains its key mechanism as *double-loop learning*: learners question their own assumptions and values, not just the task. She also distinguishes *competency* (having knowledge and skills) from *capability* (confidence in using them in unfamiliar situations) ([Blaschke](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1076/2113)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *heutagogy*: self-determined learning.
  - *capability vs. competency*: confident use in new situations vs. having the skill.
  - *double-loop learning*: questioning one's own assumptions.
  - *pedagogy–andragogy–heutagogy progression*: a scale of rising learner autonomy.
- **Touches:** Describes a progression from more structure to less. Blaschke, citing Canning and Kenyon and Hase, says less mature learners need more guidance and scaffolding, while more mature learners need less instructor control ([Blaschke](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1076/2113)).
- **Critiques and limits:** Blaschke's own review says more research is needed on whether the approach works. She also says criteria for heutagogy as a framework have not yet been defined or tested. She stresses that learners new to self-determined learning need ongoing guidance ([Blaschke](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1076/2113)). Most of the evidence she reviews comes from higher education.

### Ivan Illich, *Deschooling Society* (1971), chapter on "learning webs"
- **Link:** [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deschooling_Society) (Harper & Row; 1971 text); [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2848901W).
- **Core idea:** Illich argued that institutionalised schooling institutionalises society. He proposed replacing it with four networks open to anyone: a reference service to educational objects, a skills exchange, peer-matching, and a reference service to educators-at-large. He imagined a computer matching people by the activity they wanted a partner for ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deschooling_Society)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *learning webs*.
  - *skills exchange*: people listing what they can teach and on what terms.
  - *peer-matching*: connecting people who want to learn the same thing.
  - *educators-at-large*: a directory of people willing to teach.
- **Touches:** Describes a learning system with no front door. People reach resources, peers and teachers through directories and matching instead of through enrolment.
- **Critiques and limits:** Gintis argued that Illich misread why schools take the form they do. In Gintis's account schools are shaped by the need to produce a workforce for capitalist production, so reforming education also requires changing who holds economic power ([Gintis, *Harvard Educational Review* 42(1):70–96, 1972](https://www.harvardeducationalreview.org/content/42/1/70)). Kop and Hill note that Illich placed his webs in local communities, linking local people with learners. Global online networks, by contrast, can let the dominant culture of the network crowd out local concerns ([Kop and Hill, 2008](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/9.3.4/1137)). Illich himself called advocates of free-market education the most dangerous kind of reformer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deschooling_Society)).

### George Siemens, "Connectivism: A Learning Theory for the Digital Age" (2005)
- **Link:** [*International Journal of Instructional Technology and Distance Learning*, January 2005](https://www.itdl.org/Journal/Jan_05/article01.htm); critique: [Kop and Hill, *IRRODL* 9(3), 2008, DOI 10.19173/irrodl.v9i3.523](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v9i3.523).
- **Core idea:** Siemens treats learning as making and keeping connections between nodes: people, sources, communities and even devices. He argues that *know-where*, knowing where to find what you need, now sits alongside know-how and know-what. Among his principles: learning rests in diversity of opinion, the capacity to know more matters more than what is currently known, and connections have to be nurtured. He draws on weak-tie theory to say that new opportunities often come through loose links ([Siemens](https://www.itdl.org/Journal/Jan_05/article01.htm)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *know-where*: knowing where to find knowledge.
  - *nodes and connections*.
  - *weak ties*: loose links that bridge clusters.
  - *personal learning network*.
- **Touches:** Describes learners who move between many sources and communities, and treats the ability to find the next one as a skill in its own right.
- **Critiques and limits:** Kop and Hill review the claim that connectivism is a new learning theory. They report Verhagen's (2006) view that it adds no principles missing from earlier theories, and works at the level of curriculum and pedagogy rather than theory ([Kop and Hill](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/9.3.4/1137)). They also cite adult-education research in which most participants in personalised online environments lacked the confidence and autonomy to learn well. Nearly all preferred a local or online tutor to guide and validate their work ([Kop and Hill](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/9.3.4/1137)). They warn that people online tend to connect with others who already agree with them.

## Cross-strand critique
Smith's infed overview gathers the common objections. Brookfield warned that someone can be technically skilled at self-direction without ever asking whether what they learn is worthwhile. He also argued that real self-direction needs real control and access to resources, which makes it a political matter. Brockett and Hiemstra say self-direction cannot be separated from its social setting. Following Lindeman, Smith argues that no project is wholly self-directed ([infed, "Self-direction in learning"](https://infed.org/dir/self-direction-in-learning/)).

## Questions for the pod
- Tough found self-directed learners drew on about ten helpers per project. Who are those helpers for someone between Labs workshops, and does The Labs know they exist?
- Candy separates autodidaxy (outside any program) from learner control (inside one). Which people moving through The Labs are which, and do they need different things from a facilitator?
- Kop and Hill found most online learners wanted a tutor to guide and validate. If OLOS can run without a front end, who or what fills that role for someone working alone?
- Illich's webs had no enrolment step. What does a person have to do today before The Labs can match them to a peer, a project or a teacher?
