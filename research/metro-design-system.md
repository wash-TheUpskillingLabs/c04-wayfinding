# Designing Our Maps: The Washington Metro, Wayfinding Research, and What We Take From Them

Our site is a sign system after the Washington Metro. This note is what we learned studying the real one in November 2026, so the next person who draws a map for the pod starts from the source and not from memory. Every claim links to where we found it. Where a rule is ours and not Metro's, it says so.

## Who Made What

Two designers, two jobs. Massimo Vignelli, then at Unimark International, designed the system's signage from 1968 and its 1970 design manual; he also suggested the name "Metro." Lance Wyman and Bill Cannan, as Wyman & Cannan, designed the map from 1974. Vignelli did not design the map, though people often say he did. Wyman came back to revise his own map for the Silver Line, first shown in 2012, and again in 2022 for the line's second phase. [Wikipedia: Lance Wyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Wyman), [Unrealised Vignelli maps](https://imjustcreative.com/dc-metro-subway-map-designs-massimo-vignelli/2018/06/18), [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html)

## The Map: A Few Strict Rules

- **A diagram that keeps some geography.** The map is geometric and diagrammatic, but it keeps rivers, parks, landmarks, the Beltway and the state and county borders, so riders can still place themselves. It puts clear routes, stations and transfer points ahead of true distances. [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Wyman)
- **Built on a grid, so it can grow.** The original was drawn on a grid so new lines could be added later without starting over. [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html)
- **One colour per line, used everywhere.** A line's colour on the map is the same colour on every other sign in the system. [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html)
- **Few shapes.** It has primary colours, simple shapes and one sans serif. Wyman wanted each station to have its own pictogram for riders who don't read English, but that wasn't adopted. [Washington Post, via Made Good](https://madegooddesigns.com/?p=106218), [Washingtonian](https://www.washingtonian.com/2006/05/01/metros-plan-get-the-picture-or-get-lost)
- **It must look simple.** In Wyman's words, at its best the map "should look simple," however complicated the system is. It should work as well for a visitor from abroad as for a daily rider, which is one reason he pushed for shorter station names. [Washingtonian Q&A](https://www.washingtonian.com/2011/07/07/qa-with-metro-map-designer-lance-wyman)
- **It reads at every size.** The same map works as a 36 by 48 inch backlit panel, an 18 inch map in each car, and a 6 inch handout. [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html)

## Shared Track: The Hardest Problem

Where two lines share track, the map puts a small dot between them at each station. No stretch had three lines until the Silver Line, and fitting a third line through downtown forced a redesign. The drafts tried "whiskers" on the station dot (most riders disliked them), pill-shaped stations across all the lines (popular), and thinner lines. The final map cut line width, lightened the station symbols, and ran Silver between Orange and Blue rather than on top of them. Designers in the public contest also tried narrowing bundled lines, so three lines take the width of two, and sizing a station symbol by how many lines it crosses. [GGWash: Metro listens](https://ggwash.org/view/31116/metro-listens-to-feedback-tweaks-future-map), [GGWash: next rail map](https://ggwash.org/view/amp/32323), [GGWash: contest winners](https://ggwash.org/post/10797)

What critics hold the map to: keylines of one consistent weight, station dots centred on their lines, and labels set horizontally or at one angle, never whatever angle happens to fit. [Transit Maps: DC redesign](https://transitmap.net/?p=13459), [Transit Maps: Rush+ review](https://transitmap.net/page/163)

## Lines Have Letters, Not Just Colours

Rail service discs gained letters in the 1990s for accessibility. In 2025 Metro cut them from two letters to one, so the Blue Line is "B." A disc's letter is black or white, whichever contrasts more with the disc. The disc lettering moved from Extra Bold to Medium for larger type and less glare. On the pylons outside stations, discs are replacing the coloured stripes, because discs are easier to read, especially for colour-blind riders. Vignelli's original system used only discs, so this restores it. [WMATA wayfinding FAQ](https://www.wmata.com/initiatives/plans-and-projects/wayfinding/wayfinding-faq.html), [WMATA wayfinding guidelines, June 2026](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/board-affairs/riders%27-advisory-council/4%20Wayfinding%202026-07-01.pdf)

Lines appear in one fixed order everywhere: Red, Yellow, Green, Orange, Silver, Blue. Lines that share track, and their end stations, are grouped in consistent blocks, so a service change doesn't mean reprinting thousands of signs. [WMATA wayfinding](https://wmata.com/wayfinding), [WMATA service order, Nov 2025](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/migrated-assets/about/riders-advisory-council/rac-pdfs/upload/4-2025-11-05-rac-metrorail-service-order.pdf)

## Signs: Show What's Needed Where It's Needed

- **One typeface since 1976.** Signs have always been Helvetica. In 2025 Metro moved to Helvetica Now, in the medium weight Vignelli specified in 1970. White Helvetica on the brown pylon is the system's signature. [WMATA FAQ](https://www.wmata.com/initiatives/plans-and-projects/wayfinding/wayfinding-faq.html), [SEGD](https://www.nxtbook.com/ygsreprints/ygs/G12272SEGD_Nextbook/index.php?startid=59)
- **Progressive disclosure.** Each sign carries only what a rider needs at that point in the trip. Less to read means fewer wrong platforms, wrong exits and wrong directions. [WMATA guidelines](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/board-affairs/riders%27-advisory-council/4%20Wayfinding%202026-07-01.pdf)
- **A kit of parts.** Signs are assembled from a shared library of components in a few standard sizes, so they stay consistent and quick to update. [WMATA guidelines](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/board-affairs/riders%27-advisory-council/4%20Wayfinding%202026-07-01.pdf)
- **Station Ahead Maps.** These new vertical strip maps show every stop ahead and skip none, with complete station names. They answer three questions: where is my train, which train do I get on, and is my destination ahead? [WMATA SAM update, Dec 2025](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/migrated-assets/about/riders-advisory-council/rac-pdfs/upload/5-station-ahead-maps-rac-2025-12-03.pdf)
- **Neighbourhood maps.** These place a "you are here" with walking times to nearby places, and an alphanumeric grid indexes what's on the map. [Bill Cannan Design](https://billcannandesign.com/metro.html)
- **Exits and icons.** Exits are red type on white, like the national standard exit sign. Icons follow international standards, sit on an even background, and match the weight of the type around them. [WMATA guidelines](https://www.wmata.com/content/dam/wmata-com/board-affairs/riders%27-advisory-council/4%20Wayfinding%202026-07-01.pdf)

## What Our Maps Take From It

| Metro does | Our journey map does | Status |
|---|---|---|
| One colour per line, the same on every sign | Each pod's line keeps its colour from the vote to the showcase | Done |
| A letter on every line, for colour-blind riders | A lettered disc (A, B, C) on each pod's line, with the letter in ink on a paper disc because our sign system keeps text off colour | Done |
| One fixed order for lines | Pods keep the same top-to-bottom order everywhere they appear | Done |
| Shared track as parallel lines; pill stations across them | Proposals run as a bundle; votes are pills across every line they serve | Done |
| Lines named for where they end | Each way out is named for where alumni went | Done |
| A diagram, not to scale, said plainly | The key says steps are evenly spaced, not by time | Done |
| Discs and dashed lines for future service | Dashed for planned steps | Done |
| Helvetica, brown pylons | Not ours. The pod's sign system uses Inter on ink and paper | Not taken |
| A "you are here," and what's ahead of you | A static map can't know where you are. A web version could let you pick your step, mark it in red (our colour for position) and show what comes next | Proposed |
| Show only what's needed at each point | The rows under each step do this. A web version could open one step at a time | Proposed |
| Line width shows importance; bundles narrow so three lines fit in the width of two | Branches are thinner than the trunk; bundles could narrow further as the count grows | Partly |

## Beyond Metro: What Wayfinding Research Says

Metro is one system. These four bodies of work tell us why it works, and where our maps can go further.

**Kevin Lynch, The Image of the City (1960).** People build a mental image of a city from five things: paths (where they move), edges (boundaries they don't cross), districts (areas with a shared character they feel they're inside), nodes (junctions and gathering points they enter), and landmarks (points they navigate by but don't enter). He found paths dominate most people's image. A legible place makes all five easy to see. [Lynch, summarised](https://www.supersummary.com/the-image-of-the-city/summary/), [UCL on Lynch](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1104/1/SIC.pdf.pdf)

**Arthur and Passini, Wayfinding: People, Signs, and Architecture (1992).** Wayfinding is spatial problem solving in two stages: deciding on a plan, then carrying it out. Decisions happen at decision points, and "information received at the wrong place is as good as no information at all." Landmarks placed at decision points make the decision easier to remember. Good wayfinding lets people work out where they are, where they're going, and how to get there, then follow the plan and adjust it. [Hunter, citing Arthur and Passini](https://www.scribd.com/document/441202525/SpatialOrientation-pdf), [Foltz, MIT](https://cognitivehealth.csail.mit.edu/pubs/mfoltz/mfoltz-thesis/node8.html), [Signs of the Times](https://signsofthetimes.com/wayfinding-is-not-signage/amp/)

**Marvin Levine, You-Are-Here Maps (1982).** A correct map with a "you are here" marker isn't enough. Readers need structure matching: labels that appear both in the world and on the map, so they can line the two up. The map also has to be oriented the way they're facing. [Levine 1982](https://cindy.informatik.uni-bremen.de/www3/www.sfbtr8.spatial-cognition.de/aigaion/index.php/publications/show/1704.html), [Klippel et al.](https://cindy.informatik.uni-bremen.de/www3/www.sfbtr8.spatial-cognition.de/aigaion/index.php/attachments/single/klippel-freksa-winter-YAH-final-h.pdf)

**Legible London (Applied, from 2006).** London's citywide walking system rests on progressive disclosure: the right information at the right time. Its maps are heads-up, turned to face the way the reader faces, which users preferred in testing, and they mark 5- and 15-minute walking circles. The team prototyped on street corners for months and watched how people used the signs. One principle was to remove signs the new system made obsolete rather than keep adding more. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legible_London), [Slate](https://www.slate.com/id/2246105), [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/2-november-2006/disparate-signs/), [Medium, From Wayfinding to Interaction Design](https://medium.com/design-ux/3e7fef5a6512)

**Maxwell Roberts on schematic maps (Essex).** Roberts judges a transit diagram on five criteria: simplicity (lines with few bends), coherence (an overall shape that hangs together), balance (no crushed areas next to empty ones), harmony (whether the elements sit well together), and topographicity (how far it distorts reality). In his studies, how much people like a map has no correlation with how well they use it, so a map has to be tested with tasks, not a vote. Octilinear (45-degree) layout is a convention, not a law; it isn't always the most usable. [NPR interview](https://www.wbaa.org/post/bostonians-vote-new-subway-map), [Preference versus performance](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/17237/1/1-s2.0-S1071581916300751-main.pdf), [Theory of effective design](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/11368/1/Roberts-Theoretical.pdf)

**Jug Cerovic, INAT.** One graphic language for every city's metro: consistent symbols and syntax, compact maps, and most lines with no more than five bends. [Short List](https://www.shortlist.com/news/world-subway-maps-simplified), [beSpacific](https://www.bespacific.com/?p=57386)

## Audit: The Journey Map Against These Principles

| Principle | How the map does | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| **Simplicity** (Roberts, INAT: five bends or fewer) | Followed end to end, a pod's line bends about 14 times, because each proposal round fans out and back in. Measured from the drawing. | Keep the bundles for the two votes. For paradoxes and frames, consider bundles that stay parallel for the whole pod stretch instead of opening and closing three times. That says the same thing with a third of the bends. |
| **Balance** (Roberts) | The Cycle is dense; steps 1 to 5 are sparse. | Partly honest: little exists before the Cycle yet. As steps 3 to 5 gain content, give them room. Don't stretch them out to look full. |
| **Coherence** (Roberts) | One axis, left to right, and one trunk. Strong. | Keep. |
| **Paths, nodes, districts** (Lynch) | Paths are lines, nodes are stations and votes, districts are Learn, Build and Share. | Good. |
| **Edges** (Lynch) | The Cycle's outline is an edge, and so are the red rings. | Good. |
| **Landmarks** (Lynch; Arthur and Passini put them at decision points) | None. Nothing marks the moments people actually remember. | Add landmarks at decision points: Sensemaking Day at step 6, the Paradox session, the Showcase. A Cycle's dates belong as data next to them, not in the drawing. |
| **Structure matching** (Levine) | Step names are ours, not always what people see in the app, Slack or emails. | Use the same names on the map as in OLOS, Slack and the calendar. A mismatch is a wayfinding failure, not a copy issue. |
| **Where am I, what's next** (Metro's Station Ahead Maps, Levine's you-are-here) | The static map can't know where you are. | See "The Map Is a Spec" below. |
| **Progressive disclosure** (Metro, Legible London) | All four rows are visible at every step. That's right for a map read once, wrong for a sign. | In the app, show one step's rows at a time. |
| **Preference is not performance** (Roberts) | We've only asked people whether they like it. | Test it with tasks: "Where would you go after the showcase?" "Who helps you when your team is stuck?" Time and score the answers. |

## Audit: The Pod's Maps as One System

The pod now has three maps: the Problem Situation Map, the region chart and the journey map. Metro's lesson is that a symbol must mean the same thing on every sign. Here is where ours already agree, and what this audit fixed.

- **Agree:** red dotted lines end in a ring where people leave; dashes mean planned; hollow ink rings are stations; each key is titled "How to Read It."
- **Fixed in this change:** the region chart set its line labels in blue and red, against our rule that small text is always ink. Its labels and box titles were also in sentence case. Both now follow the system.
- **Fixed in this change:** every title, button and label across the site is now title case: post titles, section titles, nav labels, buttons, the timeline's "You Are Here." Sentences, body text and quotes stay in sentence case.
- **Still different, on purpose:** blue means people moving on the region chart and the journey on the journey map. Both fit the sign system's "blue is direction," so they don't conflict.
- **Already there:** the home page's timeline marks "You Are Here" by date. That's a Station Ahead Map, and the pattern to reuse.

## The Map Is a Spec

The deepest lesson is Arthur and Passini's: information at the wrong place is as good as none. Our journey map gathers everything a participant needs, but only in one place, on a blog post. Each column of the map is really a sign that should stand at that step: in OLOS, in the Slack welcome, in the email the week a vote happens. Each one should answer Metro's three questions in our terms: where am I, which line am I on, and what's next. The map is the spec for that. Building it is the Wayfinding pod's work, after the frames are chosen.

## What Stays Ours

Metro's palette is the line colours plus brown, white and concrete. Ours is ink, paper, signal blue and signal red, after the Swiss Style scheme the site was built on. Maps may add green, violet and silver to tell lines apart. Those colours are for lines only, never for text and never in place of red. Red means a position: where you are, or where people stopped. That rule is recorded at the top of `assets/wayfinding.css`.
