# Theory: Cognitive load and choice overload

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, interfaces
**Product part:** kits | pre-cycle | post-between | channels
**Logged:** 2026-11-06 (desk research, sources below)

Two bodies of work about what happens when a person is shown too much at once. Cognitive load theory (Sweller) is about learning: working memory is small, and instruction that wastes it on things that are not the lesson produces less learning. Choice overload (Iyengar & Lepper) is about deciding: a famous study found that more options led fewer people to choose anything, but two later meta-analyses found the average effect is about zero and that the effect appears only under specific conditions. Hick's law is a much older and narrower finding about reaction time rising with the log of the number of options.

## Works

### Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, *Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later* (2019)
- **Link:** [Educational Psychology Review, 31(2), 261–292 (open PDF via CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/237588613.pdf). This is the founders' own retrospective on the theory first set out in Sweller 1988 and Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 1998 (those originals were not opened this round).
- **Core idea:** Working memory is limited, long-term memory is not, and instruction should be designed around that. *Intrinsic load* is the complexity of the material itself, which depends on how many elements interact and on what the learner already knows; it can only be changed by changing the material or the learner's expertise. *Extraneous load* comes from how the material is presented and what the learner is made to do, and can be cut by design; most of the theory's documented effects come from varying it. *Germane load* has been revised: in the 2019 account it no longer adds load of its own but describes working-memory resources redirected toward the intrinsic task, so only intrinsic and extraneous remain as basic categories.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *element interactivity* (how many things must be held in mind at once); *extraneous load* (effort spent on the packaging, not the lesson); *expertise reversal* (what helps novices can hurt experts); *guidance fading* (withdrawing scaffolds as skill grows).
- **Touches:** Describes why a newcomer and a maintainer need different amounts of scaffolding at the same step, and why the same interface can be too much for one and too little for the other. Also describes how onboarding material, navigation and instructions compete for the same working memory as the thing being learned.
- **Critiques and limits:** In the authors' own words, the fixed-capacity assumption may need revision (evidence of resource depletion after sustained effort, which they say needs confirmation); measurement of load is imperfect (the standard subjective rating scale measures total load, not its types, and some researchers are skeptical of it); some of the ego-depletion literature they draw on is contested; evidence that self-regulation can be taught as a general skill is weak. The germane-load revision is itself a sign that an earlier core concept did not hold up.

### Iyengar & Lepper, *When Choice is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?* (2000)
- **Link:** Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Summaries verified at [AcaWiki](https://acawiki.org/When_Choice_is_Demotivating:_Can_One_Desire_Too_Much_of_a_Good_Thing%3F) and [a replication-status write-up](https://www.atticusli.com/replication-crisis/choice-overload-jam-study/); the original article was not opened.
- **Core idea:** Three studies. (1) A grocery tasting booth with 24 jams drew more shoppers to stop than one with 6, but of those who stopped, about 3% bought at the 24-jam booth versus about 30% at the 6-jam booth (figures per the replication write-up; the AcaWiki summary gives the direction but not the numbers). (2) Students offered 6 essay topics for extra credit were more likely to write the essay, and wrote better ones, than students offered 30. (3) People choosing one chocolate from 30 enjoyed choosing more but were less satisfied and more regretful than those choosing from 6.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *choice overload* (more options, fewer choosers); *attraction vs conversion* (the big display drew more people and converted fewer).
- **Touches:** Describes a split between what gets people to stop and look (many options) and what gets them to act (few). Bears on first contact and on any menu of kits, projects or roles.
- **Critiques and limits:** The headline effect does not survive meta-analysis on average (next two entries). The write-up also notes sample-selection and publication-bias concerns in how the original effect size should be read.

### Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, *Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload* (2010)
- **Link:** [Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409–425, DOI 10.1086/651235 (record at University of Basel)](https://edoc.unibas.ch/20586).
- **Core idea:** Pooling 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036), the authors found a mean effect size of virtually zero with large variance between studies. They could identify preconditions that seemed to matter but no sufficient conditions for overload to occur.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *preconditions vs sufficient conditions* (things that must be present vs things that guarantee the effect).
- **Touches:** Describes the state of the evidence: "fewer options" is not a general law. Any claim that a shorter menu will raise action at The Labs needs the moderators below to hold.
- **Critiques and limits:** Chernev et al. (next) argue that the zero mean is an artifact of averaging across moderators.

### Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, *Choice Overload: A Conceptual Review and Meta-Analysis* (2015)
- **Link:** [Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333–358 (Kellogg research page)](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual).
- **Core idea:** Re-analyzing 99 observations (N = 7,202), the authors found four moderators under which more options do produce overload: *choice-set complexity*, *decision-task difficulty*, *preference uncertainty*, and a prominent *effort-minimizing goal*. With moderators included, the overall effect is significant, which they describe as counter to the prior meta-analysis. The replication write-up reports the effect as roughly d = 0.5–0.6 when all four are high and absent or reversed when they are not (that figure is from the secondary write-up, not verified against the paper).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *preference uncertainty* (not knowing what you want yet); *choice-set complexity* (options that are hard to compare); *decision-task difficulty* (time pressure, format, no defaults).
- **Touches:** Describes when a menu hurts: when the chooser does not yet know what they want, the options are not comparable, and they want to spend little effort. That is a plausible description of a first-time visitor reading a list of kits, and a less plausible description of a maintainer choosing a project.
- **Critiques and limits:** Still an aggregate of lab and field studies in consumer settings (jams, chocolates, pens); nobody in these studies was choosing a twelve-week commitment.

### Hick (1952) and Hyman (1953), the Hick–Hyman law
- **Link:** Hick, *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 4(1), 11–26; Hyman, *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 45(3), 188–196. Verified via [Wikipedia's entry with the original citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law); the originals were not opened.
- **Core idea:** In choice-reaction tasks, time to respond rises with the logarithm of the number of equally likely alternatives (approximately T = b·log₂(n+1)), so doubling the options does not double the time. The generalization uses the entropy of the option probabilities.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *log-time choice* (people subdivide options rather than scanning them one by one).
- **Touches:** Describes a narrow, well-replicated effect on reaction time for simple choices; it is often cited in interface design for menus.
- **Critiques and limits:** Per the same page, the law does not hold for unordered search (which is linear), for some familiar verbal responses, or for eye movements, and sequence predictability changes the shape. It says nothing about the quality of a choice or about commitment, only speed.

## Critiques and replication status (summary)
- **Cognitive load theory** is widely replicated for extraneous-load effects in instruction, but its founders have revised germane load out of the core model, and measurement of load remains unresolved.
- **Choice overload** is the paradigm case of a famous finding that did not replicate on average (Scheibehenne et al. 2010: mean effect about zero across 50 experiments) and then was partly rescued as a conditional effect (Chernev et al. 2015: four moderators). The honest summary is "sometimes, under these conditions."
- **Hick's law** replicates in its narrow domain and is routinely over-extended to complex decisions it was never tested on.

## What this body of work assumes that The Labs cannot
Cognitive load work assumes a designed lesson with a defined learning goal and, usually, a classroom. Choice-overload work assumes low-stakes, one-shot consumer choices made alone. Neither assumes a person choosing whether to give twelve weeks to strangers, or choosing with a friend, or choosing across several channels over weeks.
