# Case: US employers and skills-based hiring, dropping degree requirements on paper vs. in hiring

**Kind:** failure (with a minority of extreme positive cases inside it)
**Bears on:** post-cycle, interfaces
**Field claims:** 2, 4
**Evidence:** complicates 2; contradicts 4 at scale. Most firms that removed degree requirements from postings kept hiring degree holders, so a changed signal on the employer side did not change who was hired ([HKS summary of Sigelman, Fuller & Martin 2024](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)); a minority of "leader" firms did change, and state-level postings shifted ([Heck et al. 2024](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33220)).
**Logged:** 2026-11-05 (desk research, sources below)

## 1. What it is
A joint Harvard Business School / Burning Glass Institute study that tracked whether US firms that publicly dropped bachelor's degree requirements from job postings then actually hired more people without degrees. Read alongside a 2024 NBER paper on state governments doing the same.

## 2. Where it came from
- The Burning Glass Institute is a labor-market research nonprofit; the Harvard Business School Project on Managing the Future of Work is an academic research project. Authors: Matt Sigelman, Joseph Fuller, Alex Martin ([Burning Glass Institute report page](https://burningglassinstitute.org/research/skills-based-hiring-2024)).
- The full PDF could not be retrieved (the download link returned an HTML page). Figures below come from a Harvard Kennedy School summary page and a commentary by Brent Orrell at the American Enterprise Institute, which report the figures independently of each other.
- A separate check on the public sector: Heck, Corcoran de Castillo, Blair and Debroy, NBER Working Paper 33220 ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33220)).

## 3. When
- Study period: 2014–2023 ([Orrell, AEI, 2024-02-22](https://cosm.aei.org/a-slow-start-for-skills-based-hiring/)).
- Report released February 2024 ([Burning Glass Institute](https://burningglassinstitute.org/research/skills-based-hiring-2024)).
- NBER state-government paper: December 2024 ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33220)).

## 4. What it says
- Between 2014 and 2023, the number of roles that dropped degree requirements rose almost fourfold ([Orrell, AEI](https://cosm.aei.org/a-slow-start-for-skills-based-hiring/)).
- In a sample of about 11,300 roles that removed the requirement, hiring of people without a degree rose 3.5 percentage points ([Orrell, AEI](https://cosm.aei.org/a-slow-start-for-skills-based-hiring/)).
- Across all hires, the change touched fewer than 1 in 700 hires in the most recent year ([HKS summary](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice); [Orrell, AEI](https://cosm.aei.org/a-slow-start-for-skills-based-hiring/)).
- The report sorts firms into three groups. "In Name Only" (about 45%): removed the requirement from postings with no meaningful change in who was hired. "Backsliders" (about one-fifth): opened up briefly, then hired a smaller share of non-degree workers over time. "Leaders": raised their non-degree share by nearly 20% in the roles analyzed ([HKS summary](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)).
- Where firms did hire without degrees, the hires did well on employer metrics: at Leader firms, non-degree hires stayed at a rate 10 percentage points higher than degree-holding peers, and non-degree workers hired into formerly degree-gated roles earned about 25% more on average ([HKS summary](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)).
- Orrell's reading: hiring managers kept defaulting to degrees out of habit, and employment-law exposure makes employers wary of subjective alternative measures ([AEI](https://cosm.aei.org/a-slow-start-for-skills-based-hiring/)).
- Public sector: 25 states adopted executive orders or laws to cut degree requirements over two years; degree requirements in state postings fell about 2.5 percentage points per additional year of policy exposure. The paper measures postings and public awareness, not hires ([NBER w33220](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33220)).

## Failure modes seen
- **Changed the label, not the reader:** Postings dropped the degree line, but about 45% of firms showed no meaningful change in who was hired ([HKS summary](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)).
- **Backsliding after the announcement:** About one-fifth of firms hired a smaller non-degree share over time after an initial opening ([HKS summary](https://pw.hks.harvard.edu/post/skills-based-hiring-the-long-road-from-pronouncements-to-practice)).
- **Measuring the posting instead of the hire:** The state-government evaluation could only show that postings changed, not that hiring did ([NBER w33220](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33220)).

## 5. What it tells us about the problem
*Left for the pod. Questions to answer:*
- If the gatekeeper's default is the degree, what does an alternative record have to carry to displace a habit rather than just sit next to it?
- The firms that did change are a minority; what, if anything, is documented about who inside those firms read the alternative evidence (recruiters, hiring managers, screening software)?
- Postings are the visible, cheap signal on the employer side; is there an equivalent "posting-only" change on the participant side (badges or profiles that exist but nobody downstream reads)?

## 6. Hearsay and limits
- We did not read the full report; firm counts, the data source behind hires (likely résumé/profile data) and the definition of "Leader" are taken from two secondary summaries.
- Rival explanation: the small effect may reflect slack in hiring, low turnover, or applicant pools (few non-degree people applying to roles they still assumed were closed) rather than employer reluctance to read non-degree evidence. The summaries do not separate these.
- Who could confirm: the Burning Glass Institute's full report and methodology appendix; the NBER authors on whether hires followed postings in state government.
