# Theory: Organizing, mobilizing, activation and informal structure

**Bears on:** activation-mobilization, facilitators, cycle, post-cycle, interfaces
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand comes from social movements and civic associations. It asks how organisations turn people who show up once into people who take on more, and whether they do it by building people's capacity or by getting more people to act. Han and McAlevey both distinguish *mobilizing* (many people, low involvement, staff-led) from *organizing* (developing leaders who build power themselves). Ganz supplies the practice: public narrative, and teams that develop other leaders. Freeman's 1970s essay warns that groups without formal structure still have structure; it is just informal and hidden. The practitioner's "ladder of engagement" and its critics close the strand.

## Works

### Hahrie Han, *How Organizations Develop Activists* (2014), and "The Organizational Roots of Political Activism" (2016)
- **Link:** [Oxford University Press, 2014, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199336760.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199336760.001.0001); [*American Political Science Review*, 2016, DOI 10.1017/S000305541600006X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000305541600006X).
- **Core idea:** Han compared membership associations in health and environmental politics, some strong and some weak at engaging people, using observation, surveys and field experiments. She sets out three models: *organizing*, *mobilizing*, and the *lone wolf*. She found that the most active associations combined *transactional* mobilizing, which gets many people to act, with *transformational* organizing, which changes members' motivations and capacities, and that they did both online and offline ([OUP abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199336760.001.0001)). Her 2016 field experiments found that when organisations built a relational context around people, those people were more likely to sign petitions, recruit others and attend meetings ([summary, Political Science Now](https://politicalsciencenow.com/the-organizational-roots-of-political-activism-field-experiments-on-creating-a-relational-context)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *transactional mobilizing vs. transformational organizing*.
  - *lone wolf*: an activist acting without building a base.
  - *breadth and depth of activism*: many people acting, and a core of leaders.
  - *relational context*.
- **Touches:** Explains why some people move from one-off attendance to sustained involvement and others don't. It traces the difference to what the organisation does, not to fixed traits of the person.
- **Critiques and limits:** *No published critique located in this pass.* By its own description, the research covers health and environmental associations only ([abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199336760.001.0001)).

### Jane McAlevey, *No Shortcuts: Organizing for Power in the New Gilded Age* (2016)
- **Link:** [Oxford University Press, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190624712.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190624712.001.0001); reviews: [Joel Dignam, Commons Library, 2019](https://commonslibrary.org/no-shortcuts-to-building-power/); [Mike Parker, *Labor Notes*, 2017](https://www.labornotes.org/node/5678).
- **Core idea:** Drawing mainly on union campaign cases, McAlevey argues that labor's decline comes from how unions organise, not only from employer strength. Unions shifted their energy to corporate campaigns aimed at employers and away from the workers themselves ([OUP abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190624712.001.0001)). She separates three approaches. *Advocacy*: professionals act on people's behalf. *Mobilizing*: large turnouts, with staff still making the decisions. *Organizing*: ordinary people do the power analysis, design the strategy and win ([Dignam](https://commonslibrary.org/no-shortcuts-to-building-power/); [Parker](https://www.labornotes.org/node/5678)). She distinguishes *organic leaders*, who already have influence and followers, from *activists*, who are committed but may have no following. Reaching majorities depends on the organic leaders ([Dignam](https://commonslibrary.org/no-shortcuts-to-building-power/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *advocacy / mobilizing / organizing*.
  - *organic leader vs. activist*.
  - *power mapping*.
  - *bounded constituency*: the whole group you need a majority of.
- **Touches:** Distinguishes people who already have followings in a neighbourhood or branch from the most visibly committed volunteers. Also describes the difference between bringing people out for a push and developing the people who decide on the push.
- **Critiques and limits:** Parker, who is broadly positive, says McAlevey overdraws the contrast between organic leaders and activists when both are needed. He says open bargaining sessions alone do not make bargaining democratic. He argues one of her community-group examples relied on an unusually strong union state. And he notes she does not address how to replace leaders who resist member power ([Parker](https://www.labornotes.org/node/5678)). Dignam notes her evidence comes mostly from unions, and asks whether the organic-leader idea holds outside workplaces and congregations ([Dignam](https://commonslibrary.org/no-shortcuts-to-building-power/)).

### Marshall Ganz, "Public Narrative, Collective Action, and Power" (2011), and the "snowflake" team model
- **Link:** [Ganz, in *Accountability Through Public Opinion*, World Bank, 2011, pp. 273–289 (Harvard DASH PDF)](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/29314925/1/Public_Narrative_Collective_Action_and_Power.pdf); [Ganz, "Organizing Obama," 2009 working paper, Leading Change Network listing](https://oldweb.leadingchangenetwork.org/resource_center/organizing-obama-campaign-organizing-movement-prepared-for-american-sociological-association-annual-meeting-san-francisco-august-2009-working-paper/); [David P. Ball, "Snowflake Organizing," *The Tyee*, 2015](https://www.thetyee.ca/News/2015/11/11/Snowflake-Organizing/).
- **Core idea:** Ganz defines leadership as getting others to pursue a shared purpose under uncertainty, through hands, head and heart: skills, strategy and motivation. *Public narrative* addresses the heart. A *story of self* conveys the values that move the speaker. A *story of us* conveys values shared with the people being called on. A *story of now* names an urgent challenge to those values and a choice to act, along with a credible strategy and hope ([Ganz, 2011](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/29314925/1/Public_Narrative_Collective_Action_and_Power.pdf)). In his account of the 2008 Obama campaign, volunteers formed neighbourhood teams with defined roles (leader, data manager, volunteer coordinator, communications), and many people who started as volunteers left as organisers ([LCN listing](https://oldweb.leadingchangenetwork.org/resource_center/organizing-obama-campaign-organizing-movement-prepared-for-american-sociological-association-annual-meeting-san-francisco-august-2009-working-paper/)). The "snowflake" image describes leadership spreading out from a core through teams that develop more leaders. Local teams get real authority over tactics ([*The Tyee*](https://www.thetyee.ca/News/2015/11/11/Snowflake-Organizing/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *story of self / us / now*.
  - *hands, head, heart*.
  - *snowflake*: teams that develop leaders, who form more teams.
  - *defined team roles*.
  - *action inhibitors vs. motivators*: for example inertia against urgency, isolation against solidarity ([Ganz, 2011](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/29314925/1/Public_Narrative_Collective_Action_and_Power.pdf)).
- **Touches:** Describes how a newcomer's motivation is engaged and how team roles are handed on. It also describes the dip in energy between peaks.
- **Critiques and limits:** Ganz says narrative deals with motivation only. Strategy and skills have to be learned separately, and the hope it offers has to rest on facts ([Ganz, 2011](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/29314925/1/Public_Narrative_Collective_Action_and_Power.pdf)). Canadian groups that used the snowflake model in 2015 reported three problems. It worked best when there was money to pay organising staff. It was hardest to keep going between elections, when the issue was out of the news. And election finance rules stopped some decisions being decentralised. ACORN Canada's director argued the model was being treated mainly as a way to get out the vote ([*The Tyee*](https://www.thetyee.ca/News/2015/11/11/Snowflake-Organizing/)). Ganz's own paper says the 2008 campaign did not use this approach everywhere; New Hampshire ran with almost no organising. The paper is marked as a draft ([LCN listing](https://oldweb.leadingchangenetwork.org/resource_center/organizing-obama-campaign-organizing-movement-prepared-for-american-sociological-association-annual-meeting-san-francisco-august-2009-working-paper/)).

### Jo Freeman ("Joreen"), "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (talk 1970; published 1972)
- **Link:** [Author's site, jofreeman.com](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm). First official publication: *The Second Wave* 2(1), 1972. Later versions: *Berkeley Journal of Sociology* 17 (1972–73), pp. 151–165, and *Ms.*, July 1973 ([jofreeman.com](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm)).
- **Core idea:** Written about the women's liberation movement's preference for leaderless groups. Freeman argues that there is no such thing as a structureless group. Groups without formal structure develop an informal one, and that informal structure is where elites form. Newcomers enter it the way they would a sorority or private club: they find a well-regarded member to sponsor them and cultivate that friendship. Anyone working full time usually lacks the hours for the meetings and relationships needed to get a voice in decisions ([jofreeman.com](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm)). She says unstructured groups work only when they are task-oriented, small and homogeneous, communicate a lot, and have little skill specialisation. She proposes principles of democratic structuring. These include delegating specific authority for specific tasks by democratic means; holding those delegated responsible to whoever selected them; spreading authority as widely as reasonable; rotating tasks; and assigning tasks on rational criteria ([jofreeman.com](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *informal structure*.
  - *elite*: a small group with power over a larger one, a term Freeman applies only to groups, never to individuals.
  - *being "rushed"*: being drawn into the informal circle.
  - *the sponsor*.
  - *delegation by democratic procedure*.
  - *rotation of tasks*.
- **Touches:** Describes how someone joins a self-managed group in practice: through a personal sponsor and spare time, not through any published route. It also describes who is shut out, such as people with full-time jobs.
- **Critiques and limits:** Cathy Levine's reply, "The Tyranny of Tyranny," defends small, trust-based groups as a deliberate strategy rather than an immature stage. She argues that adding structure does not supply political direction, and that Freeman left out women's culture ([libcom.org copy](https://libcom.org/library/tyranny-of-tyranny-cathy-levine)). *Unverified: the date and venue of Levine's original publication; the libcom copy does not give them.* Wikipedia summarises other critics, who say Freeman's prescriptions can lead to too much structure and inefficiency in some organisations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tyranny_of_Structurelessness)).

### The "ladder of engagement" (practitioner concept), its origin in Arnstein (1969), and critiques
- **Link:** [Sherry Arnstein, "A Ladder of Citizen Participation," *Journal of the American Institute of Planners* 35(4):216–224, 1969, DOI 10.1080/01944366908977225](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944366908977225); [Beautiful Trouble, "Ladder of engagement" (contributors D. Nyaribi and J. Abujbara)](https://beautifultrouble.org/tool/ladder-of-engagement); critique: [Brad Caldana, "Matrix of Engagement," The Digital Plan, Medium, undated](https://medium.com/thedigitalplan/matrix-of-engagement-537e2680ff16).
- **Core idea:** Arnstein arranged forms of citizen participation in federal programs (urban renewal, anti-poverty, Model Cities) as rungs on a ladder. Each rung corresponds to how much real power citizens have over a plan or program ([abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944366908977225)). Campaigners later turned the image into a *ladder of engagement*: a planned path from low-effort asks up to a top-rung "big ask." Beautiful Trouble says this version was inspired by Arnstein and popularised by the 2008 Obama campaign. Its guidance includes typically five rungs and the maxim that activities attract people while relationships keep them. It also cautions that people move down as well as up, and that the ladder should not be used to sort people into fixed categories ([Beautiful Trouble](https://beautifultrouble.org/tool/ladder-of-engagement)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *ladder of citizen participation*: rungs as degrees of power.
  - *ladder of engagement*: rungs as degrees of effort.
  - *the big ask*.
  - *activities attract, relationships retain*.
  - *matrix of engagement*: clusters of past behaviour rather than one sequence.
- **Touches:** Describes the step-by-step model most volunteer organisations assume when they plan how people move from attending to maintaining or organising.
- **Critiques and limits:** Caldana argues the ladder assumes everyone enters at the same place, with the same motives and the same end goal, and that engagement moves in a straight line. He says no organisation actually sees people climb predictably. His alternative is a "matrix" built from records of each supporter's past actions: people are invited to the next action that fits their pattern, and asks they keep turning down are dropped ([Caldana](https://medium.com/thedigitalplan/matrix-of-engagement-537e2680ff16)). The two ladders measure different things: Arnstein's measures citizens' *power*, the campaign ladder measures supporters' *effort*. Tritter and McCallum argue for moving beyond Arnstein's ladder in health-service user involvement ([*Health Policy* 76(2):156–168, 2006, DOI 10.1016/j.healthpol.2005.05.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthpol.2005.05.008)). *Unverified: we confirmed only the article's bibliographic record and title, not its argument.*

## Questions for the pod
- Han and McAlevey both separate mobilizing from organizing. When The Labs "mobilizes" people for a collective push, who decides what the push is, and does anyone come out of it with more capacity than they went in with?
- Freeman says newcomers to structureless groups get in through a sponsor and spare time. In volunteer-run labs and regions, who has sponsored the current facilitators and organizers, and what happens to people who have neither a sponsor nor free evenings?
- The snowflake groups found energy hardest to sustain between elections. What is The Labs' equivalent of "between elections," and who keeps teams in contact during it?
- Arnstein's ladder measures power and the campaign ladder measures effort. When people move from workshop to Cycle to maintainer, does their say over The Labs grow as their effort grows?
