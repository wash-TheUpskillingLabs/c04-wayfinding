# Theory: Progress, commitment and their limits

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, activation-mobilization, interfaces
**Product part:** cycle | pre-cycle | post-between | channels
**Logged:** 2026-11-06 (desk research, sources below)

A cluster of findings about what makes people start, keep going and finish: effort rises as a goal gets close (goal gradient), it rises faster if you feel you have already started (endowed progress), deciding in advance when and where to act roughly doubles follow-through (implementation intentions), people start things at calendar boundaries (fresh starts), and people will pay to bind their future selves (commitment devices). The same cluster has well-documented limits: broken streaks demotivate, and expected rewards can crowd out the motivation they were meant to support.

## Works

### Hull, *The goal-gradient hypothesis and maze learning* (1932)
- **Link:** Psychological Review, 1932. Not opened directly; verified through Hull's own 1934 follow-up, [*The Concept of the Habit-Family Hierarchy and Maze Learning: Part II*](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Hull/Hierarchy/part2.htm), Psychological Review 41, 134–152, which cites and uses the 1932 principle.
- **Core idea:** In the 1934 text, the goal gradient is a learning principle: responses closer to the goal (food) gain more strength per trial than responses farther from it, so in a maze the shorter path to food is reinforced more strongly at the choice point than the longer one. The popular gloss ("animals run faster as they near the goal") is Kivetz et al.'s reading of Hull; the 1934 text as read here treats it as a gradient of reinforcement, not speed.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *goal gradient* (effort or reinforcement rising with proximity to the goal).
- **Touches:** The origin of the idea; relevant only as lineage.
- **Critiques and limits:** Rat maze work from the 1930s; Kivetz et al. (next) say the hypothesis had been largely dormant in human research before their 2006 paper, hence "resurrected."

### Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, *The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention* (2006)
- **Link:** [Journal of Marketing Research, 43 (February), 39–58 (Columbia Business School page)](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal); [PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf).
- **Core idea:** In a real café loyalty program (buy ten, get one free), customers bought more often as they neared the free coffee. In an online song-rating program, users visited more often, rated more per visit and persisted longer as they approached the reward. The *endowed progress* study: customers given a 12-stamp card with two stamps already on it completed their ten required purchases faster than customers given a plain 10-stamp card, even though the task was identical. Customers who accelerated more were also more likely to stay in the program and to come back faster after a lapse. The authors checked that habituation, expiration worries, time trends and heterogeneity did not explain the effect.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *endowed progress* (a head start, even an illusory one, speeds completion); *purchase acceleration* (shorter gaps between acts as the goal nears); *proportional goal distance* (what matters is how much of the original distance remains).
- **Touches:** Describes a measured consequence of a design decision (two free stamps). Describes why the gap between sessions might shrink late in a Cycle and stretch early on, and why the first weeks are the loss zone.
- **Critiques and limits:** Coffee and song ratings, not twelve weeks of work with a team. Retention here means "kept buying coffee." The 2006 authors note that acceleration data can be confounded by who self-selects into programs; they address it statistically but not by random assignment in the field study.

### Gollwitzer & Sheeran, *Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes* (2006)
- **Link:** [Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119, DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 (University of Konstanz repository)](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f).
- **Core idea:** An implementation intention is an if-then plan: "If situation Y, then I will do X." Across 94 independent tests, forming one raised goal attainment by d = .65, a medium-to-large effect. Proposed mechanisms: it helps people get started, shields the pursuit from distraction, helps them disengage from failing approaches, and makes the cue more accessible so the response becomes more automatic.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *implementation intention* (if-then plan with a concrete cue); *goal intention* (the wish without the plan).
- **Touches:** Describes the difference between "I'll come to a Build Cycle sometime" and "If it's Tuesday at 6 I go to the Shaw branch." Bears on the gaps between sessions and on pre-cycle, where the plan is made.
- **Critiques and limits:** Many of the 94 tests are short-horizon lab or health behaviors (exercise, screening, homework). Effect sizes in meta-analyses of this era were not corrected for publication bias with later methods; no bias-corrected re-estimate was found this round (unverified).

### Dai, Milkman & Riis, *The Fresh Start Effect: Temporal Landmarks Motivate Aspirational Behavior* (2014)
- **Link:** [Management Science, 60(10), 2563–2582, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901 (EconPapers record)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582); [author PDF at Wharton](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf).
- **Core idea:** Three archival field datasets (Google searches for "diet," gym visits, and goal commitments) all show spikes after temporal landmarks: the start of a week, month, year or semester, and after birthdays and holidays. The authors' explanation: landmarks open a new mental accounting period, push past failures into the old period, and prompt a big-picture view.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *temporal landmark* (a date that feels like a boundary); *fresh start* (a landmark that resets the ledger).
- **Touches:** Describes when people are most likely to start something, which bears on when a Cycle opens and when a lapsed person might come back.
- **Critiques and limits:** Archival and correlational; the abstract reports no effect sizes. Later work by the same group has found that fresh starts can also disrupt ongoing streaks (not verified this round; mark unverified). The datasets are individuals acting alone, not teams.

### Bryan, Karlan & Nelson, *Commitment Devices* (2010)
- **Link:** [Annual Review of Economics, 2(1), 671–698 (IDEAS/RePEc record)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/anr/reveco/v2y2010p671-698.html).
- **Core idea:** A review of lab and field evidence on devices people use to bind their future selves (deposit contracts, deadlines, lockboxes). It distinguishes hard commitments (real penalties) from soft ones (psychological or social cost only) and argues that soft commitments can resolve many self-control dilemmas. The abstract reports no take-up rates or effect sizes.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *hard vs soft commitment*; *present bias* (overweighting now).
- **Touches:** Describes the family of mechanisms under which a public vote, a team, a deadline or a stamp card can be read. A twelve-week Cycle with a team and two votes is, in these terms, a soft commitment device.
- **Critiques and limits:** Only the abstract was read. Economics literature generally finds demand for commitment devices is lower than theory predicts (a widely reported finding; not verified against a specific source this round).

### Silverman & Barasch, *On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions* (2023)
- **Link:** [Journal of Consumer Research, 49(6), 1095–1117 (University of Colorado summary)](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions).
- **Core idea:** Across seven studies, showing someone an intact streak in a log raised later engagement more than showing a broken one, regardless of what they had actually done; what mattered was how the log represented it. People treat keeping the logged streak as a goal in itself. The gap between intact and broken widened when people blamed themselves for the break and shrank when they could repair the streak.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *streak* (a run represented in a log); *streak repair* (a way to restore the run after a miss); *self-attributed break*.
- **Touches:** Describes the cost of streak mechanics: the same display that keeps people going makes a miss demotivating, and people who miss once and blame themselves disengage. Bears on between-session gaps and on any interface that shows attendance.
- **Critiques and limits:** Consumer-research studies (fitness and learning app contexts); engagement was measured over short horizons. "Streak anxiety" as a named phenomenon is folk usage; this paper measures disengagement after breaks, not anxiety.

### Deci, Koestner & Ryan, *A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation* (1999)
- **Link:** [Psychological Bulletin, 125(6), 627–668 (PDF hosted at University of Colorado)](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf).
- **Core idea:** 128 studies. Expected tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement (d = -0.36); performance-, engagement- and completion-contingent rewards all reduced it (d = -0.28 to -0.40). Unexpected tangible rewards had no effect (d = 0.01). Verbal rewards (positive feedback) increased free-choice engagement (d = 0.33) and interest (d = 0.31), though the free-choice enhancement showed in college students and not children. The paper is also a rebuttal of Cameron & Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger & Cameron (1996), who had concluded the undermining effect was largely a myth; Deci et al. say those reviews collapsed across moderators, used wrong control groups and dropped about 20% of studies as outliers.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:** *undermining effect* / *crowd-out* (an expected reward lowers the motivation it was meant to support); *free-choice measure* (what people do when no longer required to); *performance-contingent reward*.
- **Touches:** Describes what happens when a program that ran on interest adds badges, points, certificates or prizes: expected tangible rewards for doing the thing tend to reduce later unprompted doing of it; unexpected rewards and specific verbal feedback do not. Bears on the two votes, on recognition for maintainers, and on any gamified interface.
- **Critiques and limits:** The Cameron/Pierce side did not concede; the dispute continued through the 2000s (a 2001 exchange in Review of Educational Research exists but could not be opened this round). Most of the 128 studies are short lab tasks with children and college students. The self-report interest effects are much smaller than the free-choice effects (d = -0.07 for expected tangible rewards).

## Critiques and replication status (summary)
- **Goal gradient / endowed progress:** replicated in loyalty and app contexts (as reported by the 2006 authors and widely cited); field evidence is from low-stakes repeated purchases.
- **Implementation intentions:** one of the better-replicated interventions in social psychology (94 tests, d = .65), with the usual caveat about publication-era meta-analyses.
- **Fresh start:** archival and correlational; has been extended by the same group; not an experiment.
- **Commitment devices:** the review is solid; the field's recurring finding is that few people take them up.
- **Streaks:** seven studies in one paper (2023); not yet independently replicated as far as found.
- **Crowd-out:** the undermining effect survives meta-analysis for expected, tangible, contingent rewards; the counter-camp disputes it. Both sides agree verbal feedback does not undermine.

## What this body of work assumes that The Labs cannot
Almost all of it studies individuals acting alone (buying coffee, going to the gym, rating songs, doing a lab task). Teams, public votes and shipped projects are social commitments, which this literature mostly does not model. Rewards in the crowd-out studies are money or prizes, not recognition inside a community.
