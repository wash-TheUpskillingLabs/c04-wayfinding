# Theory: Community building, assets and social capital

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, facilitators, activation-mobilization
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand asks what makes people feel they belong to a place or group and act on its behalf. One branch is about conversations and gatherings (Block). One is about starting from what a community already has rather than what it lacks (Kretzmann and McKnight). One is about measuring the networks and trust that bind people within and across groups (Putnam). The Community Capitals Framework (Flora and Flora), which The Labs already uses, connects these to other kinds of community resources. The main critiques are that these approaches can leave out power and money, and can be used to justify cutting public services.

## Works

### Peter Block, *Community: The Structure of Belonging* (2008)
- **Link:** [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2347436W) (Berrett-Koehler, 2008); [review by B. A. De Oliveira, *Journal of Applied Christian Leadership*, December 2008](https://jacl.andrews.edu/community-the-structure-of-belonging/); [Block, "Six Conversations That Matter: A Quick Review," *Abundant Community*, 2011](https://www.abundantcommunity.com/six-conversations-that-matter-a-quick-review/).
- **Core idea:** Block argues that community is built in small gatherings, and that each gathering should model the future people want. The review quotes his line: "The future is created one room at a time" ([JACL review](https://jacl.andrews.edu/community-the-structure-of-belonging/)). He sets out six conversations: *invitation*, *possibility*, *ownership*, *dissent*, *commitment* and *gifts*. Invitation comes first. After that, order matters less. Some conversations carry more risk and need more trust, so a meeting should start with the less demanding ones. The dissent conversation gives people room to say no, because a yes means nothing if no isn't allowed. The commitment conversation is about promises made to peers ([Block, 2011](https://www.abundantcommunity.com/six-conversations-that-matter-a-quick-review/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *invitation over mandate*.
  - *the six conversations*: invitation, possibility, ownership, dissent, commitment, gifts.
  - *commitments without barter*: promises made with nothing asked in return.
  - *one room at a time*.
- **Touches:** Describes how a first contact is framed (an invitation, not a requirement), the right to decline, and a commitment made to peers rather than to an institution.
- **Critiques and limits:** *No scholarly critique located in this pass.* The review found is favourable and was published in a leadership journal. Block is described there as a consultant and author ([JACL review](https://jacl.andrews.edu/community-the-structure-of-belonging/)). The book draws on his practice, not on systematic study.

### John Kretzmann and John McKnight, *Building Communities from the Inside Out* (1993) — asset-based community development (ABCD)
- **Link:** [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL13538270W) (ACTA Publications); [Wikipedia, "Asset-based community development"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset-based_community_development).
- **Core idea:** Kretzmann and McKnight wrote the book at Northwestern University's Institute for Policy Research, and the ABCD Institute grew out of that program ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset-based_community_development)). They contrast *needs-based* development, which lists a community's deficits and looks to outside agencies, with *asset-based* development, which starts from the strengths already present. Its principles include: everyone has gifts; relationships build community; residents act rather than receive; leaders involve others; listen to people's interests instead of assuming apathy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset-based_community_development)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *needs map vs. asset map*.
  - *gifts*: what each person can contribute.
  - *citizens at the centre*.
  - *apathy as a misreading*: what looks like apathy often reflects interests nobody has asked about.
- **Touches:** Describes an approach to newcomers that starts with what they bring, not what they lack. That is relevant to how a first workshop or a pre-Cycle conversation is framed.
- **Critiques and limits:** Critics say ABCD implies that disadvantaged communities already have everything they need, and some policy analysts see it as giving governments cover to cut public services. The ABCD Institute replies that systemic injustice may require outside help ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset-based_community_development)). MacLeod and Emejulu traced ABCD's adoption in Scotland. They argue it gives in to neoliberal values of individualisation and privatisation. In their fieldwork, some practitioners used it to renew democracy, while others used it to make inequality a private matter and to justify welfare cuts ([*Journal of Community Practice* 22(4):430–450, 2014, DOI 10.1080/10705422.2014.959147](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705422.2014.959147); [open repository record](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/73018687-6680-4055-b688-44bc483b786a)).

### Robert Putnam, "Bowling Alone" (1995) and *Bowling Alone* (2000)
- **Link:** [*Journal of Democracy* 6(1):65–78, 1995, DOI 10.1353/jod.1995.0002](https://doi.org/10.1353/jod.1995.0002); [Open Library work record for the book](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4291077W) (Simon & Schuster, 2000); [Wikipedia, "Bowling Alone"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Alone).
- **Core idea:** Putnam argued that American civic association and social trust had declined since the 1960s. He separated *bonding* social capital, within a group, from *bridging* social capital, which links different groups. He noted the growth of membership organisations whose only membership act is paying dues. He attributed roughly half the post-1965 decline to generational change, a quarter to electronic entertainment, and about a tenth each to work pressure and to suburbanisation, with some overlap and some left unexplained ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Alone)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *bonding vs. bridging*.
  - *dues-only membership*: belonging consists of paying dues (our label for Putnam's point).
  - *social capital*: networks, norms and trust that make cooperation easier (gloss ours).
- **Touches:** Separates ties among people who are already alike from ties across differences. A Labs participant forms both kinds within a branch, a pod or a region.
- **Critiques and limits:** Portes reviewed the concept's origins in Bourdieu, Loury and Coleman. He documented its negative effects as well as its positive ones, and warned that stretching it from an individual asset to a property of whole communities and nations puts its usefulness at risk ([*Annual Review of Sociology* 24:1–24, 1998, DOI 10.1146/annurev.soc.24.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.24.1.1)). DeFilippis argued that Putnam's version ignores power in how communities are produced and is cut off from economic capital, so community development built on it is flawed ([*Housing Policy Debate* 12(4):781–806, 2001, DOI 10.1080/10511482.2001.9521429](https://doi.org/10.1080/10511482.2001.9521429)). Nicholas Lemann argued that civic life had changed form, not shrunk: bowling leagues declined while youth soccer grew. Everett Carll Ladd said Putnam ignored earlier field studies such as Middletown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Alone)).

### Mary Emery and Cornelia Flora, "Spiraling-Up: Mapping Community Transformation with Community Capitals Framework" (2006) — brief, as The Labs already uses it
- **Link:** [*Community Development* 37(1):19–35, 2006, DOI 10.1080/15575330609490152](https://doi.org/10.1080/15575330609490152).
- **Core idea:** The paper uses the Community Capitals Framework to look at community change as a system. In a Nebraska program, HomeTown Competitiveness, it found that social capital, both bonding and bridging, was the resource that turned a downward spiral of loss into an upward one, which the authors call *spiraling-up* ([abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15575330609490152)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *spiraling-up*.
  - *flows among capitals*: investment in one capital producing others.
- **Touches:** Describes how bonding and bridging ties start the movement of other resources. Like Putnam, it uses both kinds of tie.
- **Critiques and limits:** The evidence is a single program case ([abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/15575330609490152)). Because it builds on Putnam's bonding and bridging capital, the Portes and DeFilippis critiques above apply to it too.

## Questions for the pod
- Block puts invitation first and protects the right to say no. At which Labs thresholds is a person invited, at which are they enrolled or assigned, and where can they say no without leaving?
- ABCD starts from people's gifts. At which point does The Labs first learn what a newcomer can do, and is that information visible to anyone forming a pod or looking for a maintainer?
- Putnam separates bonding from bridging. Which Labs moments create ties across branches or across kinds of participant, and which only deepen ties within a group?
- MacLeod and Emejulu found the same method used for opposite ends. What would it look like if volunteer-run Labs structures were being used to stand in for services that someone else had cut?
