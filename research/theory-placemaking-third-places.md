# Theory: Placemaking, third places and social infrastructure

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, facilitators, activation-mobilization
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand is about how physical places produce, or fail to produce, casual contact between strangers and repeat encounters among regulars. It runs from Jacobs' street-level observations (1961) and Whyte's filmed studies of plazas (1970s), through Oldenburg's "third places" (1982/1989) and the Project for Public Spaces' placemaking practice, to Klinenberg's case for libraries as "social infrastructure" (2018). The critiques mostly concern who a "good" place ends up serving: gentrification, private control of public space, and nostalgia.

## Works

### Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961)
- **Link:** [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities) (Random House, 1961); [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL93641W).
- **Core idea:** The book attacks 1950s planning and large-scale urban renewal. Jacobs argues for dense, mixed-use, walkable districts, where passers-by keep public order through *eyes on the street*. She proposed four *generators of diversity*: mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of different ages, and density ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities)). On *sidewalk contact*, she argued that many small public exchanges, like asking directions, chatting with the grocer or nodding to a neighbour, add up to a web of public trust. That web asks for no private commitment and leaves privacy intact ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *eyes on the street*.
  - *sidewalk contact*: low-stakes public exchanges that build trust.
  - *generators of diversity*.
  - *no private commitments*: contact that doesn't oblige you to anything further.
- **Touches:** Describes how people get used to a place and its regulars through repeated light contact before they make any commitment. It also describes how a place stays watched over between busy moments.
- **Critiques and limits:** Lewis Mumford faulted her method while encouraging her early writing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities)). Sharon Zukin's *Naked City* ([CiNii record, Oxford University Press, 2010](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB00390132?l=en)) was written as a critique and update of Jacobs. It follows New York neighbourhoods in which the lively, "authentic" streets Jacobs praised became places low- and middle-income people could no longer afford ([*Times Higher Education* review, 2010](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/naked-city-the-death-and-life-of-authentic-urban-places/411129.article)).

### William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* (1980)
- **Link:** [Project for Public Spaces biography of Whyte](https://www.pps.org/article/wwhyte); [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL5093463W) (Conservation Foundation, 1980); [Popova, *The Marginalian*, 2013](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/22/the-social-life-of-small-urban-spaces-whyte/).
- **Core idea:** In 1969, while working with the New York City Planning Commission, Whyte began asking whether newly planned spaces actually worked. That led to the Street Life Project, which used direct observation and film to study corporate plazas, streets and parks ([PPS](https://www.pps.org/article/wwhyte)). The team studied 16 plazas, 3 small parks and other spaces ([*The Marginalian*](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/22/the-social-life-of-small-urban-spaces-whyte/)). Its findings were concrete. One rule of thumb was that seating should make up 6–10 per cent of a plaza's open space, or about one linear foot of seating for every thirty square feet. And the edge where plaza meets street decides whether a space succeeds ([*The Marginalian*](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/22/the-social-life-of-small-urban-spaces-whyte/)). PPS's founder worked as Whyte's research assistant and based the organisation on Whyte's methods ([PPS](https://www.pps.org/article/wwhyte)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *sittable space*.
  - *the street–plaza transition*: where passing becomes staying.
  - *observation before design*: watch first, then change things.
- **Touches:** Describes the threshold between passing by and stopping, and argues it can be studied by watching people instead of asking them.
- **Critiques and limits:** Zukin describes how Whyte guided a study of Bryant Park in New York, funded by a business improvement district. After the BID took over management of the park from the Parks Department, it posted rules, hired security guards to enforce them, and limited who could do what and where. Zukin also grants that the park became safer and busier ([Zukin, "Politics and aesthetics of public space: the 'American' model," 1998, republished by PublicSpace/CCCB 2004](https://www.publicspace.org/multimedia/-/post/politics-and-aesthetics-of-public-space-the-american-model)).

### Ray Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place* (1989), and Oldenburg and Brissett, "The third place" (1982)
- **Link:** [Oldenburg and Brissett, *Qualitative Sociology* 5(4):265–284, 1982, DOI 10.1007/BF00986754](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00986754); [Open Library work record for the book](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2231781W) (Paragon House, 1989); [Wikipedia, "Third place"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place).
- **Core idea:** A *third place* is a social setting separate from home (the first place) and work (the second). Examples include cafés, bars, libraries, barbershops and parks. Oldenburg argued that third places matter for democracy, civic engagement and a sense of place ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place)). The characteristics listed for a third place: it is free or cheap and you can come and go as you like; it is comfortable, informal, close to home and unpretentious; it has regulars, often a host who greets people, conversation as the main activity, and a playful mood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place)). A 2023 edition was co-authored with Karen Christensen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *first, second and third place*.
  - *regulars*.
  - *the host*: the person who greets arrivals.
  - *come and go as you please*: low entry and exit costs.
- **Touches:** Describes informal settings that people return to between commitments without having to sign up, and the role of a greeter who knows the regulars and notices newcomers.
- **Critiques and limits:** The term was taken up as commercial branding. Wikipedia's references include a 2008 *Fast Company* piece on Starbucks and "the third place" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place)). A reviewer summarises Klinenberg's argument that Starbucks is too expensive for many people and restricts how long customers stay and how they interact ([*The Key Reporter*, 2019](https://www.keyreporter.org/book-reviews/2019/palaces-for-the-people-how-social-infrastructure-can-help-fight-inequality-polarization-and-the-decline-of-civic-life/)). *We did not locate a scholarly critique of Oldenburg's concept itself in this pass.*

### Project for Public Spaces, placemaking principles ("placemaking" in steady use from the mid-1990s)
- **Link:** ["What is Placemaking?" PPS, 2008, updated 2025](https://www.pps.org/article/what-is-placemaking); ["Eleven Principles for Creating Great Community Places," PPS, 2009, excerpt from *How to Turn a Place Around*](https://www.pps.org/article/11steps).
- **Core idea:** PPS describes placemaking as a collaborative process in which people reshape shared public space together, with community participation at the centre and a community's existing assets as the starting point. It credits Jacobs and Whyte as its intellectual mentors, and says it began using the term "placemaking" consistently in the mid-1990s ([PPS, "What is Placemaking?"](https://www.pps.org/article/what-is-placemaking)). Its eleven principles include: the community is the expert; create a place, not a design; you can't do it alone; have a vision; start with the petunias (small, cheap moves first); triangulate; form supports function; money is not the issue; you are never finished ([PPS, Eleven Principles](https://www.pps.org/article/11steps)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *triangulation*: placing things together so that strangers have a reason to interact (gloss ours).
  - *start with the petunias*.
  - *the community is the expert*.
  - *never finished*.
- **Touches:** Describes the move from design to continuing management, and the idea that a place is made by how people use it, not only by how it is laid out.
- **Critiques and limits:** Kenneth Reardon reviewed a 2013 report on placemaking in post-industrial "legacy cities." He argued that small physical improvements have done little to fix weak regional economies. Several of the projects held up as good practice were driven by business and institutional interests, with little input from low-income communities of colour. His main example is Cincinnati's Washington Park, where a men's shelter was relocated and a school closed as part of the expansion ([*Shelterforce*, 2014](https://shelterforce.org/2014/10/02/placemaking_for_and_by_whom2/)). PPS itself says the link between public space and gentrification is a frequent, if sometimes misdirected, criticism of placemaking ([PPS, 2015](https://www.pps.org/article/gentrification)).

### Eric Klinenberg, *Palaces for the People* (2018)
- **Link:** [Open Library work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL19753504W) (Crown/Penguin Random House, 2018); review: [David Madden, *The Key Reporter* (Phi Beta Kappa), 2019](https://www.keyreporter.org/book-reviews/2019/palaces-for-the-people-how-social-infrastructure-can-help-fight-inequality-polarization-and-the-decline-of-civic-life/).
- **Core idea:** Klinenberg's *social infrastructure* means the physical places and organisations that shape how people interact. Libraries are his central example. Others include schools, playgrounds, pools, sidewalks and community gardens. He argues that public attitudes and underfunding hold these places back from addressing isolation, division and crime. The title borrows Andrew Carnegie's phrase for the 1,689 US libraries he funded from 1903 ([*The Key Reporter*](https://www.keyreporter.org/book-reviews/2019/palaces-for-the-people-how-social-infrastructure-can-help-fight-inequality-polarization-and-the-decline-of-civic-life/)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *social infrastructure*.
  - *hard vs. social infrastructure*.
  - *palaces for the people*: public buildings open to all.
- **Touches:** Describes the branch library as a place where people meet across differences without paying or committing to anything, which is the setting where Labs workshops happen.
- **Critiques and limits:** Joshua Kim wrote a friendly critique. He argues the book lacks "useful complicators": some social infrastructure has improved even as other parts eroded. He also says it overlooks academic libraries and treats online learning as if it were only MOOCs ([*Inside Higher Ed*, 2018](https://www.insidehighered.com/blogs/technology-and-learning/friendly-critique-palaces-people%E2%80%99)). Madden's review describes main libraries he visited in North Carolina whose busiest-day users were almost entirely homeless people. That contrasts with the mixed public the book describes ([*The Key Reporter*](https://www.keyreporter.org/book-reviews/2019/palaces-for-the-people-how-social-infrastructure-can-help-fight-inequality-polarization-and-the-decline-of-civic-life/)).

## Questions for the pod
- Jacobs and Oldenburg both describe contact that asks for no private commitment. Which Labs moments are like that, and which ones ask for a commitment before a person has had a few low-stakes contacts?
- Oldenburg's third places have regulars and a host. Who is the host at a branch workshop, and does that person still exist for a participant between sessions?
- Whyte watched how people used spaces before changing them. Has anyone watched what first-time attendees do in the ten minutes before and after a workshop?
- The critiques of Whyte (Bryant Park) and PPS (Washington Park) are both about who gets moved out when a place "works." Who stops coming to a branch when The Labs makes the branch busier?
