# Theory: Communities of practice, trajectories and boundary crossing

**Bears on:** pre-cycle, cycle, post-cycle, facilitators
**Logged:** 2026-11-05

This strand treats learning as taking part in the shared work of a group and gradually becoming a member of it. It is not mainly about acquiring knowledge in your head. It starts with Lave and Wenger's study of apprenticeships (1991). Wenger then extended it into a theory of identity and membership (1998). Later work by Wenger-Trayner and colleagues, and by Akkerman and Bakker, looked at what happens when people move between several communities rather than deeper into one. Critics question whether the model fits modern workplaces, and whether its later managerial versions have dropped the attention to power that the original contained.

## Works

### Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, *Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation* (1991)
- **Link:** [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/situated-learning/6915ABD21C8E4619F750A4D4ACA616CD) ([DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511815355](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355)).
- **Core idea:** Learning is a social process. Its defining feature is *legitimate peripheral participation* (LPP): newcomers take part in a community's real practice, starting at the edges, and move toward full participation over time ([CUP description](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/situated-learning/6915ABD21C8E4619F750A4D4ACA616CD)). The book analyses five apprenticeship settings: Yucatec midwives, tailors, navy quartermasters, meat cutters, and members of Alcoholics Anonymous. In each, novices picked up skill gradually by working alongside experts in everyday activity ([Kearsley, Theory Into Practice database, hosted by Universität zu Köln](https://uni-koeln.de/hf/konstrukt/didaktik/situierteslernen/TIP%20Theories.htm)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *legitimate peripheral participation*: real but partial involvement that the community recognises.
  - *newcomers / old-timers*.
  - *periphery to centre*: the direction of movement.
- **Touches:** Describes how a first-time arrival can take part in real work before being a full member. It also describes what makes that early, partial involvement count in the eyes of existing members.
- **Critiques and limits:** Fuller, Hodkinson, Hodkinson and Unwin studied UK manufacturing firms and secondary schools. They found the theory insightful but limited when applied to modern workplaces. They argue that institutional settings shape who gets opportunities to learn and who meets barriers, and that participation takes more varied forms than the model allows ([*British Educational Research Journal* 31(1):49–68, 2005, DOI 10.1080/0141192052000310029](https://doi.org/10.1080/0141192052000310029)). Contu and Willmott argue that the original attention to power relations was dropped as the idea was taken up in management ([*Organization Science* 14(3):283–296, 2003, DOI 10.1287/orsc.14.3.283.15167](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.14.3.283.15167)).

### Etienne Wenger, *Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity* (1998)
- **Link:** [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/communities-of-practice/724C22A03B12D11DFC345EEF0AD3F22A) ([DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511803932](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511803932)).
- **Core idea:** Engagement in social practice is how we come to know what we know and to become who we are. The unit of analysis is neither the individual nor the institution. It is the informal community of practice that people form as they pursue a shared enterprise over time ([CUP description](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/communities-of-practice/724C22A03B12D11DFC345EEF0AD3F22A)). Wenger described several kinds of *trajectory* a person can follow relative to a community. *Peripheral* trajectories give access without leading to full membership. *Inbound* trajectories move from the edge toward the centre. *Insider* trajectories stay central. *Boundary* trajectories span two or more communities. *Outbound* trajectories lead out. He called belonging to several communities at once a *nexus of multimembership* (summarised in [Lane and Headley, arXiv 2112.05581, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.05581)). Lane and Headley add a warning: if insiders favour newcomers who came in the way they did, membership will not reflect the wider population ([Lane and Headley](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.05581)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *trajectories*: peripheral, inbound, insider, boundary, outbound.
  - *nexus of multimembership*.
  - *mutual engagement on a joint enterprise*: Cox's summary of what Wenger means by a community of practice ([Cox 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551505057016)).
- **Touches:** Names five ways of being related to a community. Only one of them, inbound, is movement toward the centre. The others include staying peripheral, working across boundaries, and leaving.
- **Critiques and limits:** Cox compared four founding texts. He found that Lave and Wenger (1991) is often read as being about how newcomers are socialised into an existing body of knowledge. Wenger (1998), by contrast, focuses on individual identity. And the 2002 managerial follow-up (Wenger, McDermott and Snyder) redefines the concept toward groups that management deliberately fosters. Cox also questions whether the concept fits highly individualised, tightly managed work ([*Journal of Information Science* 31(6):527–540, 2005, DOI 10.1177/0165551505057016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551505057016)).

### Etienne Wenger-Trayner, Mark Fenton-O'Creevy, Steven Hutchinson, Chris Kubiak and Beverly Wenger-Trayner (eds.), *Learning in Landscapes of Practice* (2014)
- **Link:** [Open Research Online record, Open University](https://oro.open.ac.uk/40657) (Routledge; [DOI 10.4324/9781315777122](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315777122)).
- **Core idea:** The book treats a profession's body of knowledge as a *landscape* made of many communities of practice. A person's learning is their journey through that landscape. Contributors write from different positions within it, about partnerships across agencies, disciplines and countries, about social media, and about the link between academic study and workplace practice ([ORO record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/40657)).
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *landscape of practice*: many communities taken together.
  - *journey through the landscape*.
  - *knowledgeability*: being able to find your way across many practices, as opposed to mastering one (term from the book's subtitle).
- **Touches:** Describes people whose learning comes from moving between communities, not from going deeper into one.
- **Critiques and limits:** *Not located in this pass.* The ORO record does not define "knowledgeability" or "boundary," so the gloss above follows the book's framing and has not been checked against its text.

### Sanne Akkerman and Arthur Bakker, "Boundary Crossing and Boundary Objects" (2011)
- **Link:** [*Review of Educational Research* 81(2):132–169, DOI 10.3102/0034654311404435](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311404435).
- **Core idea:** A literature review that treats boundaries between practices as places where learning can happen. It names four learning mechanisms at boundaries: *identification*, *coordination*, *reflection* and *transformation*. Differences and breaks in action between practices become resources for developing intersecting identities and practices ([abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311404435)). The one-line glosses of the four mechanisms under Vocabulary are ours. The abstract names the mechanisms but does not define them.
- **Vocabulary worth borrowing:**
  - *boundary crossing*.
  - *boundary object*: an artefact that works in more than one practice.
  - *boundary crosser*: a person who carries work or meaning between practices.
  - The four mechanisms (our glosses): *identification* (working out how practices differ), *coordination* (finding ways to cooperate across the difference), *reflection* (seeing your own practice through another's eyes), *transformation* (changing practices, or creating a new one in between).
- **Touches:** Describes what happens at the seams between communities, for example between a workshop audience, a project team and a group of maintainers, and the artefacts that cross those seams.
- **Critiques and limits:** The authors say the literature had not spelled out how boundaries carry learning potential, and they offer their review as a first account of it ([abstract](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311404435)). It is a synthesis of other studies, not new empirical work.

## Questions for the pod
- Wenger names five trajectories, and only one leads inward. Which of the five do Labs participants actually follow, and which ones does The Labs currently treat as success?
- Lane and Headley warn that insiders favour newcomers who arrived the way they did. Who are the current maintainers and facilitators, and which entry route did each come through?
- Akkerman and Bakker's mechanisms happen at the boundaries between practices. Where are the boundaries between workshop, Cycle, pod and maintained project, and what artefacts already cross them?
- Fuller et al. found institutions shape who gets opportunities to learn. What in how branches, regions and the hub are set up decides who gets to participate in real work early?
