      What 54 programs taught us about the spaces between

      Before we write a line of code, we read how everyone else lost people at the edges, and what carried the ones who stayed.

      Why we read first

      The Labs builds in a fixed order, and the first step is archaeology: before we decide what the problem is, we find out how it became one and what’s already been tried. So before the pod writes its paradox on Saturday, Nov 7, we read 54 programs that tried to move people from a first taste to lasting involvement, from Google Summer of Code to 4-H, and mapped 50 organizations doing related work in Greater Washington and nationally.

      This post is what kept coming up. It’s a reading, not a conclusion. The paradox, and the frames that follow it, are the pod’s to write.

      Everyone has the cliff

      Every program we read loses most people at the moment it ends. In Google Summer of Code, about 57% of students said they meant to keep contributing; a few months later, about 16% had.1 Of the projects from 73 hackathons, about a third had any commit after the event, and fewer than 4 in 100 were still active five months later.2

      The part that stuck with us: saying you’ll stay doesn’t predict staying. In the hackathon study, a team’s stated intent to continue made no measurable difference. A route that depends on people deciding to come back is a route most people won’t take.

      What predicts staying happens before day one

      In both the Summer of Code data and a closer study of KDE, the students most likely to stay after the program were the ones who had already contributed before it.1 Hackathon projects lasted longer when the team’s skills were diverse and matched to the work.2 Both are settled before the event starts.

      For The Labs, that points at the part we haven’t built. The Cycle is our front door today, and there’s no pre-cycle: nothing that gets someone ready, attached to a person, and doing small real work before day one.

      A message works when someone is behind it

      Text reminders over the summer helped admitted students actually start college, in the first trials, where counselors stood behind the texts. When campaigns scaled statewide and nationally, the effect mostly disappeared; the researcher’s own explanation is that the advising was dropped and students didn’t know or trust the sender.3

      Wikipedia saw the same thing from the other side. Its newcomers were turned away by semi-automated rejection tools, and newcomers invited to the Teahouse, a help space run by people, stayed at higher rates.4 Notifications are cheap. The person is the point.

      The people outlast the project

      Our own alumni survey says it plainly. Asked what mattered most, almost everyone named the people. Of the eleven who joined a team, seven are still in touch with it months later.5 What ended was the shared work.

      That changes what a route after the Cycle might be. Maybe not “keep your project alive”, but a visible next place to go, with people you already know.

      One leader is one point of failure

      Code Club was designed to be volunteer-led; in its trial, 10 of 14 club leaders ran the club alone.6 A third of trained Carpentries instructors hadn’t taught.7 FIRST robotics teams, 4-H clubs and open-source projects all lean on one person, and fold when that person goes.

      NaNoWriMo, the November novel-writing challenge, ran on hundreds of volunteer regional leads and closed in 2025. Some of its local regions kept going under new names, on channels they owned.8 The local layer outlived the hub.

      What we don’t know yet

      Everyone who answered our survey is someone who stayed. We haven’t heard from people who came to one workshop and didn’t come back, and they’re the ones this work is for.

      If that’s you, or you know them, tell us what happened. You don’t need to code, and what you tell us goes into the record with your name on the contributors list.
