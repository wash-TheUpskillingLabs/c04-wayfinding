      What 54 case studies are teaching us about wayfinding

      Most programs design the last day. The people who stay were won on the days nobody designed. Here’s what 54 of them are teaching us.

      We watched before we built

      Before you redesign a train station, you stand in it at rush hour and watch where people hesitate.

      So before writing a line of code, we watched. Not our own station yet, but 54 others: programs that tried to move people from a first taste to something lasting, from Google Summer of Code to 4-H. We also mapped 50 organizations doing related work in Greater Washington and across the country.

      This is what they keep teaching us. It’s a reading, not a verdict. The pod writes its paradox on Nov 7.

      The cliff is the default

      Every program we read loses most of its people at the moment it ends.

      Not some programs. Every one.

      About 16 in 100 Google Summer of Code students were still contributing a few months later.1 Of the projects from 73 hackathons, about a third saw a single commit after the weekend, and fewer than 4 in 100 were alive five months on.2

      That isn’t a failure of the people. It’s what happens when the last day of a program is designed and the day after isn’t.

      Saying isn’t staying

      Here’s the uncomfortable part. About 57% of those Summer of Code students said they meant to keep going.1 In the hackathon study, a team’s stated intention to continue made no measurable difference at all.2

      We design for the person on the last day: inspired, grateful, full of plans. The person who actually has to come back is someone else. They’re tired on a Tuesday, and their inbox has opinions.

      Design for that person.

      The real work happens before day one

      The students most likely to stay after Summer of Code had contributed before it started.1 The hackathon projects that lasted had teams with a mix of skills, matched to the work.2 Both were settled before anyone said “go.”

      That’s awkward for us, because The Labs has no “before.” The Cycle is our front door. Nothing gets someone ready, introduces them to a person, and hands them something small and real to do first.

      How might we build the hallway, not just the room?

      A message is only as good as the person behind it

      About ten text messages over a summer helped admitted students actually show up to college, in the first trials, where a counselor stood behind every message. Scaled statewide and nationally, the effect mostly vanished. The researcher’s own best guess: the advising was dropped, and students didn’t know or trust the sender.3

      Wikipedia learned the same thing from the other side. Its newcomers were turned away by semi-automated tools, while newcomers invited into the Teahouse, a help space run by people, stayed at higher rates.4

      The message was never the point. A notification is a very cheap way of saying “someone here knows you exist,” and it only works if that’s true.

      People stay for people

      Our own alumni survey says it without prompting. Asked what mattered most, almost everyone named the people.5 Of the eleven who joined a team, seven are still in touch months later. The project ended. The people didn’t.

      So maybe the question after a Cycle isn’t “how do we keep your project alive?” Maybe it’s “where do you and your people go next?”

      Heroes are a single point of failure

      Code Club was designed to be volunteer-led; in its trial, 10 of 14 club leaders ran their club alone.6 A third of trained Carpentries instructors hadn’t taught.7 Robotics teams, 4-H clubs and open-source projects all lean on one devoted person, and quietly fold when that person moves on.

      NaNoWriMo, the November novel challenge, closed in 2025. Some of its local groups simply kept going, under new names, on channels they owned.8 The center failed. The neighborhoods didn’t.

      Build for the neighborhoods.

      What we still don’t know

      Everyone who answered our survey is someone who stayed. We haven’t heard from the people who came to one workshop and drifted off, and they’re exactly who this is for.

      So we’re carrying three questions into Saturday:

      • How might we design the day after a Cycle as carefully as the last day?
      • How might we give people something small and real to do before day one?
      • How might we make sure every nudge has a person behind it?

      If you came once and didn’t come back, you’re the expert here. Tell us what happened.
