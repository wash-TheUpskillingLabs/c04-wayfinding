      Five Themes Running Through the Field

      We did Dorst’s fifth step on the 50 organizations around The Labs: what they’re really after, where it meets what alumni and organizers told us, and four frames to test before the Nov 14 vote.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 9, 2026. A ten-minute read.

      What the Field Is Really After

      On Nov 7 the pod wrote its paradox, and each builder left with a frame. This week every frame gets a quick prototype and three real people to try it. On Nov 14 the room votes on proposals.

      Before that, we went back to a step we hurried past. Kees Dorst’s method has nine steps, and the fifth is themes: “investigating the themes that emerge in the broader field.”1

      A theme is the deepest layer of what someone wants. Ask an organization what it wants and you get a solution: a platform, a credential, a job fair. Ask how, and you get a scenario. Ask why, and you get a goal. Keep going, and you reach a theme: something people live through, personal and universal at once, like belonging, trust or being in control. Dorst’s colleagues find themes by looking for what keeps coming back across people who otherwise have little in common.2

      Themes are what make a frame hold. A frame built on a theme the whole field shares has ground to stand on; a frame built on a clever comparison doesn’t. As our README puts it, frames come from the themes, not from staring at the paradox.1

      So we read the 50 organizations on our field map: libraries, public agencies, funders, peer programs, volunteer networks and national standard-setters.3 For each one we asked: under what it says it wants, what is it really after? Then we set three voices from inside The Labs beside them: the alumni survey, the organizers’ account and the problem owner.4–6

      This is Wayfinding’s work because the field is the country around the route. It’s where people come from on the way into The Labs, and where they go on the way out. If The Labs points somewhere the field can’t see, people get lost at the border.

      Five Themes, Drawn as Lines

      Five themes kept coming back. We drew each one as a Metro line running down the page. Each organization is a row, with a station on every line whose theme it carries, judged by what it says it wants, what it has tried and how it sees the problem. A row with stations on two lines is an interchange, and interchanges are where frames tend to come from: Dorst’s next step is “identifying patterns in the themes to create frames.”1

      The bold rows at the top are inside The Labs. Read across them to see which themes they share with the field. Hover a station to see its evidence; the table under the map lists every one, with a link to the file it came from. Each mark is a reading, not a fact. If you’d read a file differently, tell us.

      The Five Themes

      Being Recognized

      To be seen for what you can do, by whoever decides. This is the loudest theme in the field: 27 of the 50 carry it. Workers need employers to see past the degree screen. Libraries want federal law to name them as workforce institutions. Programs need funders to see results, so they count credentials, wages and placements. Recognition always comes from someone upstream, in that someone’s language.

      Inside The Labs, only the problem owner raised it: employers judge us against multi-million-dollar institutions, and writing things down doesn’t count as a contribution.6 Alumni and organizers didn’t mention it at all.

      Knowing Where You Are

      To see the whole, your place in it, and the next step. Fourteen of the 50 carry it, and nearly all of them draw the whole from above: a state inventory of resources, a regional platform with AI agents, a resource guide, a clear path to a good job. Inside, all three voices carry it, and from the ground: what’s next, what’s expected of me right now, a roadmap from my own work into a team.4–6

      Someone Beside You

      A person, or a group, who knows you and goes with you. Seventeen of the 50. In public systems it’s a paid guide: a case manager, a coach, a librarian trained to help. Everyone is trying to stretch that person further, with AI to free up advisors, some of whom carry more than 400 students, staff trained before the public, about 50 paid slots for about 500 people. A few peers make the group the route instead, like Climb Hire’s pods and Civic Tech DC’s recurring room.3 Inside, all three voices carry it. Alumni said the most valuable part was the people. The problem owner said nobody’s job is to guide them.

      Lasting Past the End

      That the work, and the people, are still there after it ends. Eighteen of the 50. The field lives in funding cycles the way our members live in Cycles, and the endings are hard. Byte Back closed after 27 years despite good results. The Clark Foundation spent itself out. Code for America ended its volunteer network. Money for digital navigators was cut in 2025. What lasted had a recurring room and a name, like Civic Tech DC, or a loop for alumni, like Per Scholas.3 Inside, all three voices carry it: leaving the Cycle means leaving The Labs, yet 7 of 11 alumni are still in touch with their team.4

      Becoming Someone New

      Crossing into a new working self, and believing it. The quietest theme: 9 of the 50. The field mostly treats a career change as a conversion, with a certificate, a résumé translated out of federal format or a nudge toward entrepreneurship. Its own evidence says the crossing is about who you are. Byte Back found its participants lacked confidence, not computers. Of the workers Opportunity@Work calls STARs, skilled through alternative routes, only 15 in 100 call themselves that.3 Inside, the problem owner sees it too: people aren’t sure they’re allowed to go off and do it on their own.6

      What the Lines Have in Common

      Dorst’s sixth step looks for patterns across the themes. Five stood out.

      The field counts people one at a time; the inside values what’s shared. Every number the field publishes is per person: a credential, a wage, a placement, a person trained. What alumni valued most was the people they built with, and the problem owner found that asking for individual contributions cut across a team’s sense of itself.6 The Labs makes its value in teams, and the field can only see it in individuals.

      The field draws the map from above; people need it from where they stand. Inventories, platforms and guides show everything that exists. Nobody in the field draws “you are here,” and that is the part our alumni asked for.

      A person’s attention is the scarcest thing on both sides. The field stretches it with tools and training. The evidence keeps saying a crossing works when a person is there: reminder texts helped students get to college when a counselor answered the replies, and statewide campaigns that dropped the advising lost the effect; Wikipedia newcomers invited to its Teahouse stayed, while those met by bots left.9, 10

      Endings are normal, and what survives is a rhythm and a name. Programs and grants end on a schedule. The groups that outlived their hub kept a recurring room and a name, and the relationships outlived the program anyway.

      Every door is where three themes meet. At each threshold someone decides whether you’re ready, someone is or isn’t there to meet you, and you decide whether you belong. Year Up says its own assessment limits who it can serve. UDC asks for documents and a test at the door. Arlington’s lab asks only for a library card and a signed agreement.3 Inside, “not ready” has nowhere to go.6 These doors are the thresholds this pod owns.

      Four Frames to Try Before Nov 14

      The frames the pod wrote on Nov 7 aren’t in the README yet, so we can’t test them here. Instead, here are four frames the themes suggest, each built at an interchange on the map and written in the pod’s form. Take one, or hold yours up against them.

      Vouching

      If we treat this as a problem of vouching, then recognition comes from the people who built beside you, written where the field can read it.

      Being Recognized meets Someone Beside You at seven organizations on the map. Elsewhere there are signs it works. Workers skilled through alternative routes moved up more often inside employer networks committed to reading their evidence, 52 in 100 against 39 in the market at large, though those employers may have joined because they already hired such workers. And employers read contributions to other people’s open-source projects as endorsements.7, 8 It moves the paradox by making team-made work visible without freezing it into a certificate. To prototype it, after a group day each teammate vouches for one specific thing another did, on that person’s record. Show it to three alumni and one person who hires.

      The Station Agent

      If we treat this as a problem of a station with an agent at the gate, then every step on the route shows where you are and names the person to ask about the next one.

      Knowing Where You Are meets Someone Beside You, in all three voices inside. The field’s version is the digital navigator, a trusted person at the first threshold; our alumni asked for moderators to step in when teams got stuck.3, 4 It moves the paradox by pairing a map that holds still with a person who can adapt. To prototype it, put a named alum or moderator on each step of the journey map, and ask three people at three different steps to find their next one.

      Home Port

      If we treat this as a problem of a home port, then a Cycle is one voyage, and a crew keeps its berth between voyages.

      Someone Beside You meets Lasting Past the End at seven organizations, and in all three voices inside. Civic Tech DC kept meeting after its national hub let it go. Recurse Center’s alumni “never graduate.” The Indivisible groups that lasted met monthly and replaced leaders as they left.3, 11, 12 It moves the paradox by giving people something stable to come back to, so the Cycle can stay adaptive. To prototype it, make a standing night and a page where past teams keep their place and newcomers can visit, and ask three alumni whether they’d come.

      Rite of Passage

      If we treat this as a problem of a rite of passage, then each threshold is a crossing that is named, witnessed and remembered.

      Becoming Someone New is the quietest line on the map, and it may be the one that’s been missing. The anthropologist Arnold van Gennep found the same three stages in rites of passage across many societies: leaving an old role, a time in between, and being welcomed into the new one.13 The middle stage is called liminal, from the Latin for threshold. To prototype it, set aside a short, opt-in moment at a group day when a team names a crossing one of its members made, like a first build shipped or a first training taught, and ask three people what it changed. A rite marks a crossing. It never ranks the people who haven’t crossed yet.

      A Test for Any Frame

      • Which theme is it built from? If you can’t name one, it’s a comparison, not a frame.
      • Do unlike organizations share that theme, and does someone inside The Labs? Check the map.
      • Which side of the paradox gives? Say what moves.
      • Does it open many things to build, or only one? Dorst’s colleagues call this fruitfulness.
      • Can three real people try it by Nov 13?

      What This Can’t Tell Us Yet

      This is desk research, and it shows. Dorst finds themes in what people live through. We read what organizations publish. Every mark in the field comes from a website, a report or testimony, not from a person telling us what they’re after.

      That tilts the map. Public pages are written to be recognized by funders and councils, so Being Recognized is probably louder here than it is among the people who work there. Four of the files are thin, and two organizations carry no theme at all in what we found.

      Inside, we have three voices: a survey, a record and a conversation. Twelve alumni answered the survey, and 11 of them had joined a team; the people who came once and left aren’t in it. Nobody inside has yet been asked what they’re really after.

      And one reader made the marks. Someone else would mark some rows differently, which is why every station shows its evidence.

      The fastest way to make this better is the interview our README already asks for: “What would a good year here look like for you? Why does that matter? And why does that matter?” Keep asking why, and you reach a theme.
