      Five Human Needs Running Through the Field

      Under every program, credential and grant around The Labs is a person who needs something. We read the 50 organizations on our field map for what that is, set it beside what alumni and organizers told us, and found four frames to try before the Nov 14 vote.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 9, 2026. A ten-minute read.

      What People Need, Underneath

      On Nov 7 the pod wrote its paradox, and each builder left with a frame. This week every frame gets a quick prototype and three real people to try it. On Nov 14 the room votes on proposals.

      Before that, we went back to a step we hurried past. Kees Dorst’s method has nine steps, and the fifth is themes: “investigating the themes that emerge in the broader field.”1

      A theme is what people need underneath what they ask for. Ask an organization what it wants and you get a solution: a platform, a credential, a job fair. Ask how, and you get a scenario. Ask why, and you get a goal. Keep asking, and you reach something a person lives through, personal and universal at once, like belonging, trust or feeling in control. Dorst’s colleagues find themes by looking for what keeps coming back across people who otherwise have little in common.2

      That’s where a frame gets its strength. A frame built on a real human need has ground to stand on; a frame built on a clever comparison doesn’t. As our README puts it, frames come from the themes, not from staring at the paradox.1

      Behind every organization on our field map are people: a federal worker starting over after the cuts, a librarian asked to help someone find work, an advisor with too many people to see, an adult who walked into a program and didn’t know where to go next. So we read the 50 organizations for the person underneath.3 What is each one trying to give someone, and what does that person need? Then we set three voices from inside The Labs beside them: the alumni survey, the organizers’ account and the problem owner.4–6 Last, we named each need with the term psychologists use for it, so the names mean something precise and the pod can build on what’s already known.

      This is Wayfinding’s work because the field is the country around the route. It’s where people come from on the way into The Labs, and where they go on the way out. If The Labs meets people somewhere the field can’t see them, they get lost at the border.

      Five Needs, Drawn as Lines

      Five needs kept coming back. We named each one the way a person would say it, and drew it as a Metro line running down the page. Each organization is a row, with a station on every line whose need it reaches for, judged by what it says it wants, what it has tried and how it sees the problem. A row with stations on two lines is an interchange, and interchanges are where frames tend to come from: Dorst’s next step is “identifying patterns in the themes to create frames.”1

      The bold rows at the top are inside The Labs. Read across them to see which needs they share with the field. Hover a station to see its evidence; the table under the map lists every one, with a link to the file it came from. Each mark is a reading, not a fact. If you’d read a file differently, tell us.

      The Five Needs

      For each need: how a person might say it, what the research says, what it feels like to go without it, how the field answers it, and how it shows up inside The Labs.

      Mattering

      “I want someone to see what I can do, and for it to count.”

      Psychologists have studied mattering since 1981: the sense that other people notice you, think you’re important and rely on you. In its first studies, with four samples of teenagers, it predicted less depression even after self-esteem was taken into account. It has two halves, feeling valued and adding value, and people need both.14, 15

      Without it, you’re overlooked: screened out by a degree you don’t have, or summed up by a number that can’t show what you added. The field answers this need more than any other; 27 of the 50 organizations reach for it, almost always with something countable: a credential, a wage, a placement. Organizations feel it too. Libraries want federal law to name them as workforce institutions, and every program needs a funder to see its results.3 Inside The Labs, only the problem owner raised it: employers judge us against multi-million-dollar institutions, and writing things down doesn’t count as a contribution.6

      Coherence

      “I need this to make sense: where I am, what’s next, and that I can handle it.”

      The medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky called this a sense of coherence. He built the idea from interviews with people who had come through severe hardship, and found three parts: the world is understandable, you have what you need to manage it, and it’s worth the effort. Across hundreds of studies since, a stronger sense of coherence goes with better mental health and quality of life.16

      Without it, you’re guessing, and the guessing wears you out. Fourteen of the 50 reach for it, nearly all from above: a state inventory of resources, a regional platform with AI agents, a resource guide.3 Inside, all three voices feel it, and from the ground: what’s next, what’s expected of me right now, how my own work becomes part of a team.4–6

      Support

      “I don’t want to face the hard part by myself.”

      Social support is one of the best-studied ideas in psychology. It protects people most when things are hardest, which researchers call buffering, and a review of 148 studies found that people with stronger social relationships had 50% better odds of survival, an effect comparable to well-known risks like smoking. Brain studies make it concrete: under threat, holding a familiar person’s hand quiets the brain’s alarm. James Coan’s social baseline theory reads that the other way around. The brain expects company, and being alone is what adds the extra work.17, 26

      Without it, the hard part is the part you face alone. Seventeen of the 50 reach for it. In public systems the answer is a paid guide, like a case manager, a coach or a librarian trained to help, and everyone is trying to stretch that one person further: AI to free up advisors, some of whom carry more than 400 students; about 50 paid counseling slots for about 500 federal workers. A few make the group the answer instead, like Climb Hire’s pods and Civic Tech DC’s recurring room.3 Inside, all three voices feel it. Alumni said the most valuable part of The Labs was the people.4 The problem owner said nobody’s job is to help them find their way.6

      Belonging

      “I want a place, and people, that are still mine when this ends.”

      In 1995 Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed the evidence and called the need to belong a fundamental human motivation. It takes two things together: frequent, good time with the same people, and a bond that lasts. A changing cast of strangers doesn’t satisfy it, and people form bonds readily and resist letting them end.18

      Without it, the end of a program feels like being dropped. Eighteen of the 50 reach for it, and they know endings well. Byte Back closed after 27 years despite good results. The Clark Foundation spent itself out. Code for America ended its volunteer network. Money for digital navigators was cut in 2025. What lasted kept a recurring room and a name, like Civic Tech DC, or a way back in for alumni, like Per Scholas.3 Inside, all three voices feel it: leaving the Cycle means leaving The Labs, yet 7 of 11 alumni are still in touch with their team, just as Baumeister and Leary would predict.4, 6

      Becoming

      “I want to believe I can become someone new, and that I’m allowed to.”

      Psychologists Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius called the selves we hope to become, or fear becoming, possible selves. A possible self moves people when it feels real and comes with a plan. In randomized trials, Daphna Oyserman’s School-to-Jobs program helped low-income middle schoolers picture a future self and map concrete steps toward it, and their grades and engagement in school improved.27 Herminia Ibarra found career changers work the same way: they don’t think their way into a new self, they try possible selves on, often by finding someone already living the life they want.22

      Belief is what moves a person across. Albert Bandura called it self-efficacy, and it grows most from doing the thing and succeeding, next from watching someone like you do it, and then from being told, by someone you trust, that you can.20 A review of 47 job-search programs found they helped people find work only when they built motivation, self-belief included, as well as skills.21

      Without it, you hold back, even when you have the skills. This is the quietest need on the map: 9 of the 50. The field mostly treats a change of career as paperwork, with a certificate, a résumé translated out of federal format or a nudge toward entrepreneurship. Its own evidence says it’s about belief. Byte Back found its participants lacked confidence, not computers. Of the workers Opportunity@Work calls STARs, skilled through alternative routes, only 15 in 100 call themselves that.3 Inside, the problem owner sees it too: people aren’t sure they’re allowed to go off and do it on their own.6

      Two Older Maps That Agree

      The five needs came from the field, not from a textbook. But two well-tested frameworks land in nearly the same place, which makes us more confident the needs are real.

      Self-determination theory holds that everyone needs autonomy, competence and relatedness, and decades of studies across cultures back it.25 Support and Belonging are relatedness. Becoming rests on competence, with autonomy in the words “allowed to.” Coherence is what these researchers call structure: the clear expectations and guidance that make competence possible.

      Marie Jahoda studied an Austrian village whose only big employer closed in the Great Depression, and asked what work gives people besides pay. Her answer was five things: a structure to the day, regular contact with people, a sense of being useful to others, being valued, and regular activity. A 2023 meta-analysis found that people out of work report less of all five, and that each one predicts mental health on its own.24 Four line up with ours: structure is Coherence, contact is Support, being useful to others is Mattering and Belonging, and being valued is Mattering. Only activity has no line of its own.

      Jahoda’s work matters for Wayfinding. Between jobs, people lose all of these at once. Between Cycles, our members may lose them too: the rhythm, the people, the shared project. That’s a comparison, not a finding; we haven’t asked members what they lose when a Cycle ends. It’s worth asking.

      What the Lines Have in Common

      Dorst’s sixth step looks for patterns across the themes. Five stood out, and each is a gap between how organizations see people and how people feel.

      People are counted one at a time, but what they value is each other. Every number the field publishes is per person: a credential, a wage, a placement, a person trained. What alumni valued most was the people they built with, and the problem owner found that asking for individual contributions cut across a team’s sense of itself.4, 6 The Labs makes its value in teams; the field can only see it person by person.

      Organizations draw the map from above; a person needs it from where they’re standing. Inventories, platforms and guides show everything that exists. Nobody draws “you are here,” and that is what our alumni asked for.

      What everyone is short of is a person’s time. The field tries to stretch it with tools and training. The evidence keeps saying a crossing works when a person is there: reminder texts helped students get to college when a counselor answered the replies, and statewide campaigns that dropped the advising lost the effect; Wikipedia newcomers invited to its Teahouse stayed, while those met by bots left.9, 10

      Places close; people keep each other anyway. Programs and grants end on a schedule. The groups that outlived their hub kept a recurring room and a name, and the relationships outlived the program.

      At every door, a person is asking three questions. Will they see what I can do? Is anyone here for me? Do I belong here? The last question has been tested. In a one-hour exercise during the first year of college, students read that worries about belonging are common at first and fade with time. It raised Black students’ grades through senior year, and years later they reported more satisfaction with their careers.19 Year Up says its own assessment limits who it can serve. UDC asks for documents and a test at the door. Arlington’s lab asks only for a library card and a signed agreement.3 Inside, “not ready” has nowhere to go.6 These doors are the thresholds this pod owns.

      Four Frames to Try Before Nov 14

      The frames the pod wrote on Nov 7 aren’t in the README yet, so we can’t test them here. Instead, here are four frames these needs suggest, each built at an interchange on the map and written in the pod’s form, with what it would feel like to the person on the other end. Take one, or hold yours up against them.

      Vouching

      If we treat this as a problem of vouching, then recognition comes from the people who built beside you, written where the field can read it.

      It’s built where Mattering meets Support, at seven organizations on the map. A vouch gives both halves of mattering at once: someone noticed, and what you added is on the record. Elsewhere there are signs it works. Workers skilled through alternative routes moved up more often inside employer networks committed to reading their evidence, 52 in 100 against 39 in the market at large, though those employers may have joined because they already hired such workers. And employers read contributions to other people’s open-source projects as endorsements.7, 8 It moves the paradox by making team-made work visible without freezing it into a certificate. To prototype it, after a group day each teammate vouches for one specific thing another did, on that person’s record. Show it to three alumni and one person who hires.

      To a member it would feel like this: someone I built with said what I did, somewhere it counts.

      The Station Agent

      If we treat this as a problem of a station with an agent at the gate, then every step on the route shows where you are and names the person to ask about the next one.

      It’s built where Coherence meets Support, which all three voices inside feel. It offers what Antonovsky found people need, a world that makes sense, along with the company Coan found the brain expects. The field’s version is the digital navigator, a trusted person at the first threshold; our alumni asked for moderators to step in when teams got stuck.3, 4 It moves the paradox by pairing a map that holds still with a person who can adapt. To prototype it, put a named alum or moderator on each step of the journey map, and ask three people at three different steps to find their next one.

      To a member it would feel like this: I always know where I am, and who to ask.

      Home Port

      If we treat this as a problem of a home port, then a Cycle is one voyage, and a crew keeps its berth between voyages.

      It’s built where Support meets Belonging, at seven organizations and in all three voices inside. Civic Tech DC kept meeting after its national hub let it go. Recurse Center’s alumni “never graduate.” The Indivisible groups that lasted met monthly and replaced leaders as they left.3, 11, 12 It meets Baumeister and Leary’s two conditions directly: the standing night is the frequent contact, and the berth is the bond that lasts. Attachment research adds a reason it helps people move on, not just stay: adults explore more when they have a secure base that is there when needed, doesn’t interfere and encourages them.23 It moves the paradox by giving people something stable to come back to, so the Cycle can stay adaptive. To prototype it, make a standing night and a page where past teams keep their place and newcomers can visit, and ask three alumni whether they’d come.

      To a member it would feel like this: when the Cycle ends, I still have somewhere to go, and people who expect me.

      Rite of Passage

      If we treat this as a problem of a rite of passage, then each threshold is a crossing that is named, witnessed and remembered.

      It’s built on Becoming, the quietest line on the map, and maybe the need that’s been missing. A rite uses three of Bandura’s sources of self-belief at once: the person’s own success, seen by others like them, and named out loud by people they trust. The anthropologist Arnold van Gennep found the same three stages in rites of passage across many societies: leaving an old role, a time in between, and being welcomed into the new one.13 The middle stage is called liminal, from the Latin for threshold. To prototype it, set aside a short, opt-in moment at a group day when a team names a crossing one of its members made, like a first build shipped or a first training taught, and ask three people what it changed. A rite marks a crossing. It never ranks the people who haven’t crossed yet.

      To a member it would feel like this: someone marked the moment I became a builder.

      A Test for Any Frame

      • Which human need is it built from? Name it, say it the way a person would, and say what research stands behind it. If you can’t, it’s a comparison, not a frame.
      • Do unlike organizations reach for that need, and does someone inside The Labs feel it? Check the map.
      • Which side of the paradox gives? Say what moves.
      • Does it open many things to build, or only one? Dorst’s colleagues call this fruitfulness.
      • Can three real people try it by Nov 13, and tell you how it felt?

      What This Can’t Tell Us Yet

      This is desk research, and it shows. Dorst finds themes in what people live through. We read what organizations publish, and named needs for people we haven’t met. Every mark in the field comes from a website, a report or testimony, not from a person telling us what they need. The words in quotation marks above are ours, written the way we think a person would say them. They’re a guess until someone says them back.

      That tilts the map. Public pages are written to be seen by funders and councils, so Mattering is probably louder here than it is among the people those organizations serve. Four of the files are thin, and two organizations carry no theme at all in what we found.

      Inside, we have three voices: a survey, a record and a conversation. Twelve alumni answered the survey, and 11 of them had joined a team; the people who came once and left aren’t in it. Nobody inside has yet been asked what they need.

      The research we used to name the needs mostly studied individuals, often students or couples. Reading it into what organizations publish is our inference. It tells us what to look for, not what people here feel.

      And one reader made the marks. Someone else would mark some rows differently, which is why every station shows its evidence.

      The fastest way to make this better is the interview our README already asks for: “What would a good year here look like for you? Why does that matter? And why does that matter?” Keep asking why, and you reach the need. Then use their words, not ours, for the names on this map.
