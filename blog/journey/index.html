      One Person’s Way Through The Labs, Left to Right

      The region chart shows the parts. This map follows one participant from first hearing of us to after a Cycle, with what people told us at every step.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 8, 2026. A nine-minute read.

      Why Follow One Person

      The Wayfinding pod asks how people find their way around The Labs, and where they lose it. Our region chart answers from above: the parts of the region and what moves between them. This map answers from the ground. It follows one participant, step by step, and asks what you do at each step, where it happens, who’s with you, and what people who’ve been there told us.

      Read it as your own route. Find the step you’re on, and look at what comes next.

      The Journey on One Map

      Read it like the Metro. The blue line is the journey, left to right, and dashed means planned and not running yet. Red rings are where people leave, the arcs above are people skipping ahead or coming back, and the outlined columns are the Cycle.

      Inside the Cycle, the line works like track that several lines share. When proposals are open, they run side by side as a bundle: problems, then paradoxes, then frames, then projects. At a vote, some proposals end at a terminus bar and the chosen ones branch off. Vote 1 sends pods off on their own lines, each in its own colour, and Vote 2 sends projects off inside each pod in that pod’s colour. As on the Metro, the other pods run green and yellow beside it; blue is yours, and silver is a proposal that wasn’t chosen. The colours were picked so that people with any common kind of colour blindness can still tell every line apart, and each line also carries a letter, a position and a thin dark edge, so colour is never the only clue. Like Metro’s service discs, each pod’s line also carries a letter, so you can follow it without telling colours apart. And like a Metro line, each way out is named for where it ends: the names come from what alumni told us they went on to do. Every project comes back together at the showcase. How many pods and projects form is decided in the Cycle, not in advance, so the map draws a few and the dots say there may be more, or fewer.

      The bottom row quotes the pod’s statements: A is the alumni survey of the first three Cycles, O is organizers. Where it says nothing heard yet, we haven’t asked anyone who was there.

      Flags above the line are landmarks: the moments people remember, like Kickoff, Sensemaking Day and the Showcase, with this Cycle’s dates beside them. Use Where Are You? to pick your step. A red ring marks it, and only your step and the next one stay at full strength, so you can see what’s ahead without reading the whole map.

      What the Map Shows

      Most people start at step 4. Today the Cycle is the front door, so the arc from hearing of us jumps straight to it, past the sessions and the prep. Organizers said it plainly: the Cycle is the point of entry. The plan is for the Cycle to be something you build up to.

      Before the Cycle, there isn’t a path yet. Trainings, workshops, open labs and meetups happen in any order, and nothing helps you see where you’ve got to. So the map draws them as a loop, not a line. You can join anywhere and go round as often as you like, and there’s a suggested way round: learn a tool at a training, which is all learning for you and, ideally, taught by alumni as their way of sharing, work a problem at a workshop, which is about 20 percent learning and 80 percent building, build together at an open lab, then share what you found and meet people at a meetup, which is mostly sharing. That’s the Learn, Build, Share spiral, one small turn at a time, and a Cycle is several turns at once. The planned record under the loop would stamp each session, so even a messy path makes sense looking back.

      The complaints pile up inside the Cycle. Nine of the seventeen quotes sit in steps 4 to 6, and nearly all of them ask the same thing: what am I meant to do now? Joining a pod, then a team, is where people lose the thread. That is the pod’s problem, stated by the people who lived it. Line the quotes up under the map and they sit at the votes, where proposals end and lines branch. People get lost where the group makes a choice, and each choice leaves more lines, not fewer: after Vote 1 you pick a pod, after Vote 2 a project inside it. That is when an alumnus asks for “a clearer roadmap from individual work into team pods.”

      Two steps have no voice at all. Nobody has told us how they first heard of us, or what the showcase felt like. And the survey only reached people who stayed, so the red rings come from what others saw of people leaving, never from anyone who left.

      The arcs on the right are the hopeful part. Alumni come back to teach a training or take a stuck team’s question. That loop runs today without anyone running it.

      Open Questions for the Next Draft

      What makes the sessions add up? Right now each one stands alone. A record of the sessions you’ve been to, and a suggested way round, would turn the calendar into a path. Your profile is the natural place to keep it, and what a profile gets you is still being worked out.

      What is step 3? Short prep before a Cycle is planned and unbuilt. It is also the likeliest fix for the quotes in step 4.

      What happens at step 8 for people who don’t stay? Projects that outlast a Cycle and employers who read the record are both planned, and neither has an owner yet.

      What do we call things? The app calls the start of a Cycle Kickoff and asks people to vote on problem situations. This map says problem proposals. People find their way by matching the names they see to the names on the map, so one name has to win, everywhere.

      Who did we lose? Every red ring needs a voice from someone who left. Talking to them is the next round of interviews.

      Correct It, or Add Your Step

      Like the region chart, this map is a data file. Every step, row and quote lives in one JSON file in the pod’s repo, and every quote comes from the pod’s statements, so a new interview can land here as a pull request.

      If your step looks different from where you stood, tell us. That is the research. We’re also testing the map with eleven short tasks, because liking a map and being able to use it turn out to be different things.
