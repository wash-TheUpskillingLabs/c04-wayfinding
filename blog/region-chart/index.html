      How the region fits together, on one chart

      How The Upskilling Labs of Greater Washington is organised, the dates the Cycle runs on, the route a person takes through both, and what we’re building next: kits, neighborhood workshops, salons, meetups, summits and an open commons.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 7, 2026. A seven-minute read.

      One region, five rows

      It’s hard to hold the whole region in your head at once. There’s the region itself: national, the regional association, a steering committee, maintainers, builders, pods, projects. There’s what the region runs. There’s the Cycle, which runs on dates. And there’s you, moving through all of it. So we put all of it on one chart, a row each, and marked where the rows touch. The top three rows, bracketed, are the region and the two things it runs: a commons of OLOS, modules and kits, and places for people to gather.

      Read each row left to right. Its first lines say what it gives and what it gets back. When the same step appears in two rows, a connector joins them and says what passes across, and which way. Red is where people stop. Dashed is planned: not running yet.

      Open the chart

      The logic, in one pass

      National gives every region the same platform and takes back what the regions prove. The region takes a copy it’s allowed to change. A steering committee decides what the region becomes and, with the regional association, maintains OLOS, the Upskilling Labs app; pod maintainers keep the region’s changes compatible so they can travel back; builders turn frames into changes. Value runs in a loop: platform down, proof up.

      The Cycle gives twelve weeks, a room on group days and a vote, and gets problems picked, finds, prototypes and three releases. Every date on it is a place where something is due.

      The person’s row is the one the others exist for. They give four to six hours a week and what they already know. They get practice, a team and a public record of what they built. What leaves their row is hours, a proposal or evidence; what arrives is a question, a frame or a team.

      Where the value leaks

      Two steps are red. At the vote, a project the room doesn’t pick takes its pod with it: the people who formed around it are left without a team, and nothing on the chart says where they go. At after, the person leaves with a record of what they built, and nothing in the organisation can read it yet. Employers haven’t been asked. There’s no way back in that isn’t another Cycle.

      Those are the places the problem situation map keeps pointing at, too.

      What we’re building next

      The two dashed rows under the region are what it runs, and the plan for them. Neither is running in full yet. Both are aimed at the leaks.

      Where people gather

      Neighborhood workshops. A team of neighbours hosts a workshop in person, at their own library, run from a kit, so a first session can happen anywhere in the region without waiting on us. Salons are smaller: an evening around one question, where people who’ve been through a Cycle meet people who haven’t. Meetups are planned as multi-organization events from the start, co-hosted with civic tech groups, libraries, nonprofits and employers. Summits become real DC-style summits, with panels, partners, agencies and employers in the room, and the Cycle’s showcase on the program. That’s the first answer to after: put the people who could read the record in the room where it’s shown.

      The commons: OLOS, modules and kits

      Kits are modular programmatic kits: a session in a box, with the agenda, materials, facilitator notes and setup, so a stranger can run it. Learning modules are what a training teaches, written once and taught by any trainer. OLOS, the modules and the kits all take an open contribution model, the way open-source projects do: anyone can be a contributor by opening an issue or a pull request; reviewers are trusted to say what’s ready; committers can merge; maintainers keep a part healthy over time and carry it upstream to national. OLOS itself, the Upskilling Labs app, is maintained by the steering committee and the regional association; modules and kits will have maintainers of their own.

      That’s the second answer to after: a role that keeps you in. A pod’s first issue is already a contribution; a project’s merged change is already a commit. The ladder just names what people are doing and gives them the next rung.
