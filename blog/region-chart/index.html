      How the region fits together, on one chart

      How The Upskilling Labs of Greater Washington is organised, the dates the Cycle runs on, the route a person takes through both, and what we’re building next: kits, neighborhood workshops, salons, meetups, summits and an open commons.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 7, 2026. A six-minute read.

      What moves between the parts

      It’s hard to hold the whole region in your head at once, so we drew it as parts and what passes between them. The boxes are the parts: national; the regional association, which for now sits inside The Upskilling Labs, and the two things it runs; the Cycle, partners, funders, employers, and people at each stage. The lines are what moves. Four kinds of things move, and each has its own line, so you can follow one at a time: people, work and code, knowledge, and money and support.

      Red is where people leave. Dashed is planned: not running yet. Pick one kind of line to see it on its own.

      Four flows, read one at a time

      The outer outline is The Upskilling Labs. For now the regional association is part of it, not a separate organization: national runs the region, and a steering committee has begun the region’s self-organization toward a chartered regional organization of its own.

      People

      Someone hears about us and comes to a first session. If it’s worth it, they come back, become a member, and join a Cycle. They finish with a team and a record of what they built. Today, that’s where the line ends. The plan is for two lines to leave from there: one to employers who can read that record, and one back into the region as a host, trainer or contributor, who then runs the next person’s first session.

      Work and code

      OLOS, the Upskilling Labs app, is built and maintained by national. The region runs its own fork of it, kept current by the regional association and the steering committee, so it can change things to fit how the region works. Prototypes and merged changes from the Cycle go into the region’s fork; whatever holds up goes back to national as pull requests, so the next region starts from it. Kits and modules are planned to work the same way: written once in the commons, run in many rooms.

      Knowledge

      National gives the method and the Cycle’s design. The region gives the Cycle problems worth twelve weeks; the Cycle gives back finds, paradoxes and what each team learned. Sessions give members a tool or a way of working. Alumni tell us what happened next.

      Money and support

      Grants and partnerships move through The Upskilling Labs: funders and partners agree terms with national, not with the region. Most of what keeps the region going day to day is time and rooms: four to six volunteer hours a week from each person in a Cycle, and libraries that open their doors. The region plans to raise money of its own from sustaining regional members, for and through the Greater Washington brand. That money would still sit inside the national organization, in the region’s own sub-account. Employers in the room at the showcase are planned, not agreed.

      Where people leave

      Three red lines leave the chart. Some people come once and don’t come back; we don’t yet know why. At the vote, a project the room doesn’t pick takes its pod with it, and the people who formed around it are left without a team. And when the program ends, a person leaves with a record of what they built and nothing in the region that can read it: employers haven’t been asked, and there’s no way back in that isn’t another Cycle.

      Those are the places the problem situation map keeps pointing at, too. Every dashed line on the chart is aimed at one of them.

      What we’re building next

      The dashed lines are the plan. Most of it is in the two things the region runs: places to gather, and a commons of OLOS, modules and kits. Neither is running in full yet. Both are aimed at the places people leave.

      Where people gather

      Neighborhood workshops. A team of neighbours hosts a workshop in person, at their own library, run from a kit, so a first session can happen anywhere in the region without waiting on us. Salons are smaller: an evening around one question, where people who’ve been through a Cycle meet people who haven’t. Meetups are planned as multi-organization events from the start, co-hosted with civic tech groups, libraries, nonprofits and employers. Summits become real DC-style summits, with panels, partners, agencies and employers in the room, and the Cycle’s showcase on the program. That’s the first answer to after: put the people who could read the record in the room where it’s shown.

      The commons: the region’s OLOS, modules and kits

      Kits are modular programmatic kits: a session in a box, with the agenda, materials, facilitator notes and setup, so a stranger can run it. Learning modules are what a training teaches, written once and taught by any trainer. OLOS, the modules and the kits all take an open contribution model, the way open-source projects do: anyone can be a contributor by opening an issue or a pull request; reviewers are trusted to say what’s ready; committers can merge; maintainers keep a part healthy over time and carry it upstream to national. OLOS itself, the Upskilling Labs app, is maintained by national; the region runs a fork of it, which the steering committee and the regional association keep current and send changes back from. Modules and kits will have maintainers of their own.

      That’s the second answer to after: a role that keeps you in. A pod’s first issue is already a contribution; a project’s merged change is already a commit. The ladder just names what people are doing and gives them the next rung.
