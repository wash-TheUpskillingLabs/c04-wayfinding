      Two Frames for Five Human Needs: The Quilting Bee and the Trail Club

      Where else do people get the things they need at once, without being paid to be there? We listed forty places, developed the ten with the best evidence, and found two that hold all five needs and all four paradoxes: the quilting bee, and the trail club that keeps a long trail open. Each frame also says where it differs from our work.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 9, 2026. A ten-minute read.

      From Needs to Frames

      Our last post found five human needs running through the field around The Labs: Mattering, Coherence, Support, Belonging and Becoming.1 Dorst’s sixth step turns themes like these into frames: “identifying patterns in the themes to create frames.”2 This week each builder prototypes a frame with three real people, and on Nov 14 the room votes. So we went looking for frames worth trying.

      A frame is an analogy. It says: treat this problem as if it were that other thing, a thing everyone already knows how to run, and see what follows. In Dorst’s words, new frames come from “linking to practices from other professional fields.” His best-known case is Kings Cross, Sydney’s nightlife district, long labelled a problem of alcohol-related violence. Treated instead as a music festival, it opened a whole list of things to build: late-night transport out, more toilets, chill-out spaces, varied entertainment, better taxi queues, safe places to sober up, and guides to welcome visitors.3

      Kings Cross also shows how a frame fails. The City of Sydney took the festival frame on. Later, after two young men died in separate attacks, the state government cut opening hours, and the City was pushed back to treating the district as a crime scene. Dorst and his colleagues call this a frame the client can no longer accept.4 A frame only works if the people who’d live with it can adopt it.

      So here is how we looked. We wrote the pod’s hunch, and three more candidates, as paradoxes: two needs that pull apart. For each one we asked where else in the world people get both at once, without being paid to be there. Every answer is a place you could walk into, from a pilgrimage to a potluck, and we listed forty. We developed a place only if there was evidence its way of working does what we need where it comes from, and only if the likeness ran through a need, not just the surface. Every frame is written in the pod’s form, If we treat this as a problem of…, then…, and tested with seven questions near the end. Then we looked for places that hold all of it, and found two: the quilting bee and the trail club. Every frame also says where it differs from our real situation, because that’s where a prototype will surprise you.

      Four Paradoxes, Starting With Our Hunch

      The pod wrote its paradox on Nov 7, but it isn’t in the README yet, so these are candidates. The first is the hunch the pod wrote on Oct 24, in the README’s because form. The other three come from what alumni, organizers and the problem owner have told us.5, 6, 7, 8 Each sets two needs against each other.

      Seeing the Route, Making Your Own

      The hunch: “Building for stability and adaptability. Is the self-managed, adaptive nature of the learning experience making it harder to understand the value, the learning and the belonging? Adaptive vs. static and visible.”2

      Upskillers need to see the route, because people commit to what makes sense: where they are, what’s next, and that they can handle it. Today how it all fits together isn’t written down anywhere, and organizers call cognitive load their biggest problem.5, 6 Upskillers need the route to be their own, because The Labs is self-managed by design, and the self a person is building is one nobody else could plan. People already aren’t sure they’re allowed to go off on their own.5 Both are true. Every fixed path makes The Labs easier to see and harder to own, and every open choice does the reverse.

      The evidence for structure is strong. In a randomized trial, CUNY’s ASAP program, which bundles a clear structure, an advisor seen twice a month and a cohort, nearly doubled the share of community college students graduating within three years, from 22% to 40%, and three Ohio colleges repeated it.9 Researchers who redesign community colleges sum up the old model as too many choices and too little guidance.10 But ASAP students study full time, with financial help, and Upskillers come in the evenings. Scouting has lived with this paradox for a century: a catalog of merit badges each Scout chooses from, beside a fixed ladder of ranks. Its failure mode is the badge “mill” that inflates the count.11

      Seen as Yourself, Part of a Team

      An Upskiller needs what they did to be seen as theirs, because employers read people one at a time and judge The Labs against multi-million-dollar institutions. A team needs its work to stay the team’s, because what alumni valued most was each other, and asking for individual contributions cut across a team’s sense of itself.5, 7 Both are true.

      A Person at Every Crossing

      A newcomer needs a person at each crossing, because readiness without someone to cross with doesn’t carry anyone. Reminder texts got students to college when a counselor answered the replies; campaigns that dropped the advising lost the effect.12 Organizers need the work to fit around their lives, because they’re volunteers too. Nobody’s job is to help people find their way, and organizer delays become participant emergencies.5, 6 Both are true. It’s one need, Support, felt by two people who can’t both have it on today’s terms.

      When the Cycle Ends

      A Cycle needs to end, because a deadline is what makes a team ship together.8 People need something that doesn’t end, because belonging takes frequent contact over time, and today leaving the Cycle means leaving The Labs.5, 13 Seven of 11 alumni are still in touch with their team anyway.7 Both are true.

      Forty Places We Considered

      Dorst’s advice is to make many frames before choosing any. So for each paradox we listed every place we could think of where people get both needs at once, from pilgrimages to potlucks. A frame has to be somewhere people already know, so you can say “it’s like…” and they picture it.

      Then we chose ten to develop. A place made the cut when there was evidence its way of working does what we need where it comes from, when the likeness ran through a need and not just the surface, and when it opened more than one thing to build. Several that didn’t make it are worth a builder’s week: summer camp is the closest likeness to a Cycle we found, a climbing gym fixes the ski resort’s weak spot, and hockey assists, the dive buddy and the AA sponsor each answer a paradox in a way the ten don’t. Take one, and find its evidence. The ten in bold are developed below.

      Seeing the Route, Making Your Own

      Three places for the pod’s own hunch. Each keeps something fixed and visible, and leaves the rest to the person.

      The Camino

      If we treat this as a problem of the Camino, a waymarked pilgrimage, then the way is marked and holds still, each person walks it at their own pace and for their own reasons, and a passport they carry is stamped by the people who host them.

      Pilgrims to Santiago de Compostela carry a credencial and fill it with stamps along the way. They show it to stay at pilgrim hostels, and in Santiago it confirms they walked at least the last 100 km.14 There are many routes, from many starting points, all ending in the same place. Many of the hosts are volunteers who walked it themselves: the training run by American Pilgrims on the Camino asks that you’ve walked at least 100 km, and people who’ve gone from walking to hosting call it a Camino of its own.15

      It moves the hunch along a new line. The marks and the stamps are static and visible; the walk is adaptive. What gives is the idea of one path. It opens a lot to build: a Labs passport stamped by hosts at each threshold, so the record of how far you’ve come is written by others and not self-reported, which is exactly the record this pod owns; signs in OLOS at every junction, saying where you are and the ways on from here; alumni who host after a short training; a first stamp for each way in, from a library, an agency or a friend; and an arrival that someone witnesses. To try it by Nov 13, give three people at different steps a passport with the journey map’s thresholds as stamp boxes, have a host stamp it at an open lab, and ask what they’d want it to show, and to whom. Watch for one thing: a pilgrimage has one destination and The Labs doesn’t, so a stamp must never read as a finish line, and the passport must stay a record, never a checklist or a gate.

      Where it differs: a pilgrimage is usually walked once, mostly alone, toward one place. The Labs is returned to, worked in teams, and has no single destination.

      To a member it would feel like this: I can see how far I’ve come and the way on is marked, but the walk is mine.

      The Ski Resort

      If we treat this as a problem of a ski resort, then every junction has a sign, every run is rated against this mountain, you choose your run by skill and mood, and a ski school and a patrol are there when you need them.

      The steering committee parked this one on Oct 28 as an example of a frame.16 It holds up better than an example. North American resorts have used the green circle, blue square and black diamond since the ski areas’ association adopted them in 1968.17 Each mountain rates its own runs, so a symbol says how hard a run is compared with the other runs on that mountain, not everywhere.18

      It moves the hunch by turning the route into a choice among rated runs, with a map of the whole mountain at once. What gives is one path. It opens a trail map of The Labs drawn from the journey map; ratings for each threshold that belong to this region and get revised each season from where people actually turned back; a ski school for people who want a lesson first; a patrol that sweeps for people stuck partway down; and a lift that brings you back to the top. To try it by Nov 13, rate the journey map’s thresholds green, blue and black with three alumni, then ask three newcomers to pick a first run and say why. Watch for two things. The mountain mostly holds still, and The Labs changes every Cycle. And a rating rates the run, but people read it as rating the skier, so a rating goes on a run and never on a person.

      Where it differs: skiers buy a day on a mountain that mostly holds still. Upskillers give their evenings to a place that changes every Cycle.

      To a member it would feel like this: I can see the whole mountain, and I pick the run that fits me today.

      The CrossFit Box

      If we treat this as a problem of a CrossFit box, then everyone does the same workout, written on the whiteboard, scaled to where each person is today, with a coach in the room.

      A CrossFit gym, which members call a box, posts one workout of the day for everyone. CrossFit’s own guidance is that scaling meets each athlete where they are while keeping what the workout is for, and that most people scale most days; scaled is still CrossFit.19, 20 When researchers compared members of a CrossFit gym with members of a traditional gym, the CrossFit members reported more social connection and a stronger sense of belonging, though neither predicted how often people came.21

      It moves the hunch inside a single night. The session is static and visible: one workout, on the board. How hard it is for you is adaptive. What gives is the idea that a visible structure means everyone at the same level. It opens one build prompt for each open lab, written up in three scales; a coach in the room; scaling said out loud as normal; and the same kind of night for every level, like shipping something small with other people. Much of this lives inside a session, which is the Scaffolding pod’s ground, so it’s one to build with them. To try it by Nov 13, run one open lab with a single prompt on the board in three scales and a coach in the room, and ask three people at different levels how it felt. Watch the whiteboard. On one CrossFit podcast, a member describes hearing someone call out a full-weight score and feeling they hadn’t done enough.22 Post the workout, never the scores.

      Where it differs: a workout is over in an hour and leaves nothing behind. A Labs night is meant to leave something built that someone keeps.

      To a member it would feel like this: we’re all doing the same thing tonight, and mine fits me.

      Seen as Yourself, Part of a Team

      Two places where people are seen for their own part of something that belongs to the group.

      The Film Credits

      If we treat this as a problem of a film’s credits, then everyone who made the work is named for what they did, on one list that belongs to the work.

      The credits at the end of a film name everyone from the director to the caterers, each by role. Science borrowed the idea. A standard called CRediT, the Contributor Roles Taxonomy, names 14 roles, from conceptualization to writing. One person can hold several roles and several people can share one, marked lead, equal or supporting if it helps.23 It exists because the order of names on a paper had become an unreliable sign of who did what.24 And recognition from peers moves people: when researchers gave a peer award at random to 100 of Wikipedia’s most productive editors who had never had one, those editors were 60% more productive over the next 90 days than 100 who weren’t given it.25

      It meets Mattering by role, not by rank, and Belonging holds because the list belongs to the team’s work. What gives is the ranking: there’s no order of names. It opens a credits block on every project page in OLOS, with roles for the work Labs teams actually do, like research, building, design, testing, facilitating, hosting, writing and keeping the team together; credits that flow to a person’s profile and export for a résumé; credits for hosts and organizers, whose work is otherwise invisible; and a line from a teammate on any credit, a vouch. Employers already trust a vouch: in nine large firms, people hired through an employee’s referral were 10 to 30% less likely to quit than others with similar skills.26 To try it by Nov 13, write credits for one finished project with its team, every role agreed, and show it to three alumni and one person who hires. Ask what they read first. The Wikipedia study holds the warning: award winners went on to get more awards from other editors, 12 to 2, which the authors put down to prestige rather than merit. Give everyone a line, so credit doesn’t pile up on whoever is most visible.

      Where it differs: a film is finished and its credits are fixed. Labs work keeps changing after it ships, so its credits have to keep being written.

      To a member it would feel like this: my name is on what we made, next to what I did.

      The Relay

      If we treat this as a problem of a relay, then each person’s leg is their own, timed and seen, and the result belongs to the team.

      At the 2008 Olympics, freestyle swimmers in the second, third and last legs of a relay swam faster than they did in their own individual races. The first swimmers didn’t.27 A larger study of 199 swimmers found the gain only when a swimmer’s leg mattered to the team and the team had a real chance at a medal.28 In medley relays, where it’s harder to tell how teammates swam, the gain disappeared. Across more than 300,000 races, later legs were faster only when the team result meant something; otherwise every leg was slower.29, 30

      The relay doesn’t ask people to choose between being seen and belonging: being seen, in a leg the team needs, is what makes people give more to the team. What gives is the idea that individual credit and team spirit pull against each other. It opens each person’s part named on the team’s work, a handoff moment between parts, and a team result everyone shares. To try it by Nov 13, show each person’s part on one project page with a handoff note from the person before, and ask three alumni whether it made them feel more part of the team or less. Watch the first leg, where people gave less. Whoever starts may feel least needed, so make the first leg’s handoff count too. And show the legs, never compare the times.

      Where it differs: a race has one clock and a clear finish. Labs work is judged by the people who use it, long after the handoff.

      To a member it would feel like this: my part was mine, and we did it together.

      A Person at Every Crossing

      Three places for the newcomer who needs a person and the organizer who doesn’t have the hours. They differ in who that person is.

      The Train Station

      If we treat this as a problem of a train station, then the board shows every way on from here, the signs tell you where you are, and a staffed desk at every station tells you which train is yours.

      Signs and boards let most travelers find their own way, and the desk is for whoever can’t. Medicine has built the desk on purpose since 1990, when Harold Freeman started patient navigation in Harlem.31 A navigator, often a community health worker or a trained layperson, finds out what stands in a person’s way and guides them through the system. The US Community Preventive Services Task Force reviewed 27 studies and found it increased colorectal cancer screening of every kind.32 Kings Cross’s festival frame produced the same role: guides to welcome visitors.3 Airports add one thing worth borrowing, a transfer desk, so a missed connection means rebooking, not being stranded.

      One person per station serves everyone passing through, so the hours have a limit: a person per threshold, not per traveler, and the signs do the rest. It opens a departures board for every threshold, a named host at each station of the journey map, a first question at the desk of “what’s in the way?”, a transfer desk for anyone who missed a Cycle, and a name to ask in every notification. To try it by Nov 13, put a named alum or moderator on each station of the journey map and ask three people at three different stations to find their next train. Watch for the desk becoming a bottleneck. If every desk is staffed by an organizer, the organizers’ side of the paradox is the one that gives.

      Where it differs: trains run on a timetable whether or not anyone boards. At The Labs, the next train often doesn’t exist until someone organizes it.

      To a member it would feel like this: I always know where I am, and who to ask.

      The Doula

      If we treat this as a problem of having a doula, then someone who knows the way stays beside you the whole way across, not to do it for you, but so you never face it alone.

      A Cochrane review pooled 27 trials with nearly 16,000 women and found that continuous one-to-one support during labour improved outcomes, with no harms found. Support from someone in a doula’s role, trained but neither hospital staff nor family, seemed to help most.33 When researchers asked women what doulas did, they said doulas helped them feel in control, boosted their confidence and explained what was happening in plain words.34

      Continuous support costs hours, so this frame moves the paradox only if the companion is someone who just crossed, for one crossing, and if companioning is their own next step. Then organizers don’t carry it. It opens an alum companion for a newcomer’s first Cycle, companionship for the first few weeks only, companions drawn from the Cycle just finished, and a companion’s role in the credits. To try it by Nov 13, ask three alumni whether they’d companion someone next Cycle and what they’d need, and ask one recent joiner what they’d have wanted. Watch for dependence, uneven companions and burnout. A doula stands outside the hospital, and a companion mustn’t become unpaid staff.

      Where it differs: a birth lasts hours, and a doula is trained for it. A Labs crossing takes weeks, and the companion is a volunteer.

      To a member it would feel like this: someone who’s done this is walking it with me.

      The Tailor’s Shop

      If we treat this as a problem of a tailor’s shop, then newcomers start on small, real pieces of real garments, beside people a step ahead, and helping the next apprentice is how you move toward the centre of the shop.

      Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger studied how tailors’ apprentices, midwives, quartermasters, butchers and recovering alcoholics learn their practice, and found the same pattern in all of them. They called it legitimate peripheral participation: newcomers start at the edge, with tasks that are small but real, and move toward full participation in the community’s work.35 The same pattern runs through the other places here. The Camino’s hosts were pilgrims, and parkrun’s volunteers are its runners and walkers. The region already says contributing is how an Upskiller becomes a trainer, facilitator or mentor.36

      In the shop, help isn’t extra hours on top of the work. It’s the edge of the work, and giving it is the helper’s own next step, so of all twelve frames this one asks least of organizers. It opens a short list of real starter tasks on every project; roles that step inward, from visitor to contributor to maintainer to host, each with a credit; learning inside project nights instead of in separate sessions; and a newcomer’s first piece checked by someone one step ahead. To try it by Nov 13, write three real starter tasks for one live project, give them to three people at the edge, and see who comes back. Watch for the edge becoming a place people get stuck, or a source of free labor; Lave and Wenger tie a newcomer’s legitimacy to real access to the community and its resources. And watch each step inward. When the Carpentries put a test between their instructor training and the badge, only about 57 in 100 trainees made it through, and in 2023 they made the test smaller.37

      Where it differs: an apprentice tailor works full time for years. An Upskiller gives a few evenings, so each step inward has to be much smaller.

      To a member it would feel like this: I’m doing real work from the first night, and I can see how people got further in.

      When the Cycle Ends

      Two places that let the Cycle keep its deadline while belonging keeps going.

      The Harbor

      If we treat this as a problem of a harbor, then a Cycle is one voyage, and a crew keeps its berth between voyages.

      Civic Tech DC kept meeting after its national hub let it go. Recurse Center’s alumni “never graduate.” The Indivisible groups that lasted met monthly and replaced leaders as they left.1 And attachment research explains why a harbor helps people sail out, not just stay in: in a study of 167 married couples, a partner who was available, didn’t interfere and encouraged them strongly predicted how much people explored.38

      The Cycle keeps its deadline; the berth doesn’t end. What gives is the Cycle’s claim to be the whole of The Labs. It opens team pages that last after the Cycle, a standing night in port, visits between crews, and crews that re-form for the next voyage. To try it by Nov 13, make a standing night and a page where past teams keep their place and newcomers can visit, and ask three alumni whether they’d come. Watch for two things: a berth for teams leaves out people who weren’t on one, and an empty berth is worse than none.

      Where it differs: a ship has a captain and a crew that sails together for years. Labs teams form fresh each Cycle, and nobody commands them.

      To a member it would feel like this: when the Cycle ends, I still have somewhere to go, and people who expect me.

      parkrun

      If we treat this as a problem of parkrun, then the same thing happens at the same time and place every week, anyone can come without signing up for a season, everyone who helps is counted, and nobody finishes last.

      parkrun is a free, timed 5 km every Saturday morning, run by local volunteers. In a UK study of over 7,000 parkrunners, most weren’t regular runners before they joined, and in a survey of 865 Australian adults, men’s wellbeing went with the sense of community they found there.39 Volunteering is logged on your profile and counts toward milestone shirts, just as running does.40 And every event has a tail walker, a volunteer who stays at the back so that nobody finishes last.41

      The Cycle can stay a sprint, because what people belong to happens every week and doesn’t end. What gives is the Cycle as the only way in. It moves another paradox too: the sprint that burns out the volunteers who run it and assumes a season of free evenings.8 Here you come any week you can. It opens open labs as the standing weekly thing, a tail walker who stays with whoever is newest or furthest behind, helping that counts the same as taking part, and milestones for showing up and for helping, kept on the person’s own record. To try it by Nov 13, run one open lab in parkrun’s shape, with next week’s date announced, a named tail walker and every helper credited, and ask three people who came whether they’d come back, and why. Watch for two things. A weekly thing needs volunteers every week. And counting attendance edges toward the activity tracking The Labs has ruled out, so any count belongs to the person, is opt-in, and is never compared.

      Where it differs: a run asks nothing of you but showing up. A Labs night asks for work, which is harder to drop into.

      To a member it would feel like this: it’s on every week, I come when I can, and nobody gets left at the back.

      What the Frames Have in Common

      Ten places with little else in common, and the same few ideas keep turning up.

      The people who crossed host the crossing. The Camino’s hosts were pilgrims. parkrun’s volunteers run and walk it. In the tailor’s shop, today’s apprentice helps tomorrow’s. This answers both sides of the paradox about people at crossings: the newcomer gets a person, and helping is that person’s own next step.

      Fix the marks, free the walk. The Camino’s waymarks, the ski resort’s signs, the CrossFit whiteboard. In each, what holds still is the signs and the structure, and what moves is the person: their pace, their run, their weight on the bar. That’s an answer to the hunch: static and visible where it helps you choose, adaptive everywhere else.

      Show the parts, never the ranking. Credits list roles with no order of names. A relay shows each leg, and it’s the team’s time that counts. parkrun’s tail walker means nobody finishes last, and a stamp records that you were there, not how fast. The warnings point the same way: the CrossFit whiteboard of scores, the swimmers who gave less in the first leg. People give more when their part is seen and needed, and less when they’re placed behind.

      A record the person carries, written by others. The credencial, a film’s credits, a parkrun profile. Each belongs to one person and is written by the people around them. That’s this pod’s ground: we own the thresholds, and the record and notifications that make the routes legible.2

      Something standing outlasts a season. parkrun every Saturday, a crew’s berth between voyages, the same class at the same hour at the box. The season can end because something else doesn’t.

      The frames lean away from what the field counts. The field reaches most for Mattering, and almost always with something it can count: a credential, a wage, a placement. These frames reach for it least, five of twelve. Support carries the most, ten, and Becoming, the need the field reaches for least of all, carries eight. Perhaps that’s because these places run without paying anyone to be there, and people come back for who they’re with and who they’re becoming. It’s a pattern in the places we chose, not a finding about the world. But it suggests the field’s loudest answer may not be the one that keeps people coming.

      Two Frames That Hold Everything: The Quilting Bee

      The patterns kept pointing at one kind of place: one pattern with room for each person, signed parts in a shared whole, help that happens where people already sit, a record people keep, and a gathering that outlasts each project. So we went back through the forty looking for places that have all of it at once, and found two. Each holds all five needs and all four paradoxes. Each also differs from our real situation in ways worth naming. The first was already on the list, filed under one paradox when it answers all four.

      The Quilting Bee

      If we treat this as a problem of a quilting bee, then everyone works from one pattern in their own fabric, each block is signed by the person who made it, newcomers learn at the frame beside people who have quilted for years, the finished quilt is given to someone at a threshold in their life, and the bee meets again for the next one.

      In the 1840s and 1850s, groups made friendship quilts: every block followed the same pattern, each was signed by its maker, and the quilt was given to a couple marrying or a family moving west.42, 43 Some of the best known, the Baltimore album quilts, were made 40 miles up the road from Washington, many with nearly every block signed by a different person.44 Quilters still pass work between them. In a round robin, each member adds a border to a shared center; one bee of ten quilters worked in pairs on each border of one quilt, and draws names so that every member ends up with a quilt the group made.45 When researchers interviewed 29 members of a quilting group, the quilters described learning new skills, getting lost in the work, close friendships, encouragement from others that pushed them to get better, and the purpose of quilts given away.46

      It holds all four paradoxes. Seeing the route, making your own: the pattern is static and visible, and the fabric and the hand are yours. The README’s worry was that an adaptive experience makes “the value, the learning and the belonging” hard to see.2 A quilt shows all three: the gift shows the value, each block shows what its maker learned, and the quilt shows who belonged. Seen as yourself, part of a team: every block is signed, and the quilt belongs to everyone. A person at every crossing: help happens at the frame, from whoever sits beside you, so it isn’t extra hours, and an experienced quilter can help the neighbors on either side. When the Cycle ends: each quilt is finished and given away, and the bee meets again.

      It holds the other ten, too. The Camino’s passport becomes each member’s album, the blocks they’ve made, signed by the people they made them with. The film credits are the signatures. The relay is the round robin. The tailor’s shop and the doula are the frame, where a newcomer sits beside someone who has done it. The train station and the ski resort are the pattern on the wall. The CrossFit box is one pattern made in many fabrics. The harbor and parkrun are the bee that meets again.

      What it opens. A pattern library: each kind of Labs night or project as a named pattern with a set block size, so any lab can run it and every block fits. National keeps the patterns, each region’s bee makes its own quilts, and a pattern that works here goes back to everyone, which is already how this region works with national OLOS.2 Signed blocks on every project page, and a label sewn on when it ships, saying who made it and for whom. The frame: open labs where people work side by side on one team’s project, with a newcomer beside someone who has done it. Round robins: a project that passes from one Cycle’s team to the next, each adding a border. A member’s album. And the sashing, the strips that join blocks into a quilt, which is this pod’s own part: the thresholds between the parts of The Labs.

      To try it by Nov 13, give everyone at an open lab the same small block, one prompt of a fixed size, to make their own way and sign. Join the blocks into one thing and give it to someone who asked for it, then ask three people whether they could see their part, the whole, and who it was for. Separately, lay out one project page as a quilt, with signed blocks, the handoffs as sashing and a label, and show it to three alumni and one person who hires.

      Who would have to adopt it: organizers, who keep the patterns; teams, who make the blocks; experienced members, who sit at the frame; and national, who keeps the library.

      Watch for the expert who unpicks a newcomer’s stitches. One quilter remembers her mother quietly removing guests’ stitches that didn’t measure up.47 At the frame, review has to be help, never a redo in front of everyone. Watch for a pattern so tight there’s no room for anyone’s fabric. A quilt takes months, and a block has to fit in an evening. And the quilt has to go to someone who asked for it.

      Where it differs: a quilt is finished, it’s one object, its blocks are made apart, and it’s made in one room. Our work is none of those things; the table below says what that changes.

      To a member it would feel like this: I made my block my way, and my name is on it. It’s part of something bigger that we gave away, and next week we start another.

      Where the Quilt Differs From Our Work

      Much of what The Labs builds is open-source software: changes to OLOS, made in this region’s copy and sent back to the national project, and each proposal has to say who keeps it in January.2 A quilt and an open-source project differ in ways that matter for exactly that work, and every frame should say where its likeness ends.

      Two Frames That Hold Everything: The Trail Club

      The second frame holds the people and also the open-source work, because it starts from where the quilt differs.

      The Trail Club

      If we treat this as a problem of a trail club that keeps a long trail open, then one standard marks the whole trail so anyone can walk it their own way, each local club keeps its own section with a named overseer for every stretch, newcomers learn on work trips beside people who have done it for years, crews form for a season’s big project and then disband, and the club and the trail go on.

      The Appalachian Trail runs about 2,200 miles and is kept open mostly by volunteers: in 2023, 4,730 of them gave 170,147 hours.52 About 30 local clubs each maintain an assigned section, and the Appalachian Trail Conservancy, whose small paid staff works mainly as their support, holds them together.53, 54 This region has its own. The Potomac Appalachian Trail Club formed in 1927 to build a key section, and today it cares for about 1,200 miles of trail.55 Its overseers each take ownership of a stretch, and district managers recruit, train and support them. Crews take on the larger projects.56, 57

      One small standard holds the whole trail together: a white blaze two inches wide and six inches tall, about 165,000 of them, kept up by the clubs. Clubs may choose between two ways of marking a turn and no others, and an individual maintainer can’t change the marking alone.58, 59 Crews like the Konnarock Crew run sessions of three to eight days. No experience is needed, experienced leaders teach, and crew work has led some volunteers to jobs with the Conservancy.60, 61 Problems come back through the logbook in every shelter, where hikers write about the state of the trail, and through trail-condition reports the Conservancy gathers from the ground and shares.62, 63 One club’s newsletter logs who did what work, where and when, and clubs report each year’s volunteers, work and hours to the Conservancy.64, 65

      It answers where the quilt differs. A trail is never finished: blazes fade, storms bring trees down, and the overseer is the person who keeps it after the season. Most hikers never meet the people who keep their trail open, which is open source’s problem too, and the club’s answer is a work log and a yearly count. Sections depend on each other, so one closure reroutes everyone. The work is spread out, each overseer on their own schedule, while work trips bring people together. Changes to the standard go through the club. A small core keeps the trail, and a long tail comes for a day. And the first threshold is designed: “no experience needed.” And it’s held the way this region holds OLOS. Local clubs keep sections of one trail to one standard, and a national body keeps the standard and carries the whole.

      It holds all four paradoxes. Seeing the route, making your own: the blazes are the same everywhere, and every hiker walks their own hike. Seen as yourself, part of a team: the stretch is yours and the trail is everyone’s. A person at every crossing: district managers and crew leaders bring newcomers in, so helping is part of the work and not extra hours. When the Cycle ends: crews disband, and the club and the trail go on.

      It holds the other frames, too. The Camino and the ski resort are the marked way. The CrossFit box is a crew’s single project, worked at every level. The film credits and the relay are the work log and the sections handed on. The train station is the shelter, with its register. The doula and the tailor’s shop are the crew leader beside a newcomer. The harbor is the club, and parkrun is the regular work trip. From the quilt we’d keep two things the trail lacks. One is the signature: put each keeper’s name where users can see it, not only in the club newsletter. The other is the gift: a trail is always being walked by someone in the middle of a crossing.

      Where it differs from our work. A trail can’t be copied or adapted the way code can, so the copy-and-send-back part of OLOS has no likeness here. You have to be there to work on a trail; much of OLOS can be worked on from anywhere. A trail changes slowly, and a relocation can take years; software changes weekly. And the trail is rooted in one place, while The Labs moves between libraries.

      What it opens. One blaze: a small shared mark for the way on at every threshold in OLOS, the same in every region, with a short list of allowed local variations. Sections and overseers: every stretch of the route, whether a threshold, a page or a notice, has a named keeper who walks it a few times a season, which answers “who keeps it in January?” by design. Work trips: open labs as Saturday work trips, with small, real fixes and newcomers beside overseers. Crews: each Cycle as a crew that takes on one big project and disbands, while the club stays. A register at every threshold, where users leave notes and keepers read them. And a work log that credits the unseen work.

      To try it by Nov 13, give three thresholds on the journey map each a named overseer, put a simple register at each, and run one evening’s work trip to clear what the registers show. Then ask three people whether they could tell who keeps the way, and whether they’d take a stretch themselves.

      Who would have to adopt it: regional organizers, as district managers; alumni, as overseers; national OLOS, as the keeper of the standard; and the people using it, who write in the register.

      Watch for overseers burning out alone on a stretch nobody visits, an orphaned section with no keeper, a standard that hardens until nothing can change, and unseen work staying unseen. And it can read as outdoorsy: the frame is the club, not the hike.

      To a member it would feel like this: I can find my way because someone marked it, I know who keeps my stretch, and one day that someone might be me.

      A Test for Any Frame

      The five questions from the themes post, plus three more: one to keep each frame an analogy, one to say where the likeness ends, and one Kings Cross taught us.

      • Is it a place or system people already know? If you can’t walk into it, it’s an idea, not a frame.
      • Where does the likeness end? Name how the place differs from The Labs, because that’s where a prototype will surprise you.
      • Which human need does the likeness run through, and what research stands behind it?
      • Do unlike organizations reach for that need, and does someone inside The Labs feel it?
      • Which side of the paradox gives? Say what moves.
      • Does it open many things to build, or only one?
      • Can three real people try it by Nov 13, and tell you how it felt?
      • Who would have to live with it, and would they adopt it? Name them: hosts, teams, organizers, employers, libraries.

      What This Can’t Tell Us Yet

      Every frame here is borrowed from somewhere else. The evidence says each place’s way of working meets a need where it comes from, not that it will work at The Labs. That’s what this week’s prototypes are for.

      The evidence is uneven. Doulas, patient navigation, ASAP, hiring by referral and relay swimming rest on trials or large datasets. The CrossFit finding is one comparison of two gyms. The Camino, the ski resort and parkrun mostly rest on how they run, and parkrun’s wellbeing studies are surveys of people who already come. Lave and Wenger’s work is close study of a few communities, not a trial. The quilting bee rests on museum records and one interview study, and the trail club on the Conservancy’s and the clubs’ own accounts.

      Every frame is an analogy, so every frame breaks somewhere. Each section says where its likeness ends. Those are the places a prototype is most likely to surprise us, so test there first.

      The paradoxes are candidates. The first is the pod’s hunch, rewritten; the other three are ours. The frames the builders wrote on Nov 7 aren’t written down yet, so we can’t say how these compare.

      And one reader chose the places. Someone else would have looked elsewhere. If you know a place where people get two of these needs at once, without being paid to be there, tell us.
