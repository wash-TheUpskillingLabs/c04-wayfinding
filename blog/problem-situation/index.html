      Every way we’ve heard of getting lost, on one map

      We wrote down everything people have told us about trying to find their way around The Labs. Then we drew it. Here’s what we saw, and what we still can’t see.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 7, 2026. A ten-minute read.

      You’re not the only one

      “Onboarding was tricky.” “Joining a project was not obvious.” “At times I did not really understand what to do next.”

      Three different people said those things, about three different Cycles. If you’ve ever felt the same, you’re in good company. None of them was unhappy with a workshop or with the person leading it. They liked those parts. What tripped them up was the space in between: one thing ends, and the next thing hasn’t shown up yet, and nobody’s quite sure if they’re supposed to wait or go.

      You know the feeling if you’ve stood on a platform watching the sign blink while your train doesn’t come. Nothing is broken, exactly. You just can’t tell what to do.

      That gap is the thing our pod studies. So we started by listening.

      Everything people told us, on one page

      Over a few weeks we collected 277 things people have said about this. Twelve past participants answered a survey. The folks who run The Labs told us their side. So did the steering committee and the problem owner. And we read up on 54 other programs that have wrestled with the same gap, which is why a few of the marks on the map are hollow: those are borrowed from somewhere else.

      Then we sorted every statement by asking five ordinary questions. What gets in the way? Who’s involved? Where does it happen? Why? And how would you fix it? Each question gets a circle. Inside, statements that say similar things huddle together, and the darker the ground, the more people said it. It’s a big picture. On a phone, scroll sideways; it really wants a large screen.

      What jumped out at us

      The first thing you notice is the biggest cluster in the “what” circle. We called it lost between the parts, and it holds more statements than anything else on the map. So no, it isn’t just you. It’s the most common thing people told us.

      Around it sit a few that look different but rhyme. Many people are looking for the next step to be spelled out. There’s too much to keep track of. And on a team of five, the building often falls to one person while the others help in other ways.

      Then look at the “how solved” circle. Most of the hollow marks live there now. When one of the 54 programs we read found a way across the same gap, we wrote down what it did, in one line, with the program’s name on it: a person at the first contact, a kit a stranger can run, a clock everyone can see. None of it is a plan for us. It’s a shelf of things that worked somewhere, with the label still on.

      The second thing you notice is what’s missing. Almost nothing on the map comes from someone who came once and didn’t come back. Nothing comes from employers. Those are the people we most need to hear from, and we’d rather show that gap than paper over it.

      One thing to keep in mind as you look: a cluster is big because more people said it, not because it matters more. The map helps us see. It doesn’t decide for us.

      Why we drew it instead of fixing it

      It’s really tempting to read a list like this and start fixing. More signs. More reminders. Another meeting. We feel the pull too.

      But look at what the map is holding at the same time. A lot of people asked us to treat them like capable adults. A lot of people also said they weren’t sure what to do next. Both are true, and both are fair. Hand out more instructions and you let down the first group. Hand out fewer and you lose the second.

      A design researcher named Kees Dorst has a word for a knot like that: a paradox. Two things that are both true and seem to rule out every easy answer. His advice is to find the paradox before you reach for a fix, because the fix that ignores it usually makes one side worse. A map like this is the step before that. You can’t see the knot until everything people believe is laid out side by side.

      That pair is just an example to show how it works. The pod is writing its real paradox today, with this map open on the table.

      Tell us what we missed

      The map is only as good as what’s on it, and we know it’s missing voices. If you’ve been through a Cycle, helped run one, or came once and moved on, you probably know something we don’t.

      It doesn’t need to be polished. A sentence is plenty, and your name stays off it. Add it, and the map redraws itself.
