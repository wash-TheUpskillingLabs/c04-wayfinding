      240 ways of getting lost, on one map

      We collected everything people have told us about finding their way around The Labs, and drew it. Here’s what the picture shows, and what it can’t.

      The Wayfinding pod, Nov 7, 2026. A ten-minute read.

      Nobody said the same thing twice

      “Onboarding was tricky.” “Joining a project was not obvious.” “At times I did not really understand what to do next.”

      Those are three people, from three different Cycles, describing the same feeling in three different ways. None of them is complaining about a workshop or a teacher. They liked those. What they’re describing is the gap in between: the moment a program ends and the next thing hasn’t shown up yet.

      If you’ve ever stood in a train station watching a sign flicker while your train didn’t come, you know the feeling. Nothing is broken. You just can’t tell what to do.

      So we put all of it on one page

      Over a few weeks we gathered 240 things people have said about this problem. Some came from a survey of twelve past participants. Some came from the people who run The Labs, some from the steering committee, and some from the problem owner. And some came from the 54 other programs we studied, which is why a few marks on the map are hollow.

      Then we sorted every one of them by asking five plain questions: what gets in the way, who is involved, where does it happen, why does it happen, and how would each person fix it? Each question is a circle. Inside, similar statements sit together in a cluster, and the darker the ground, the more people said it. Scroll sideways on a small screen; the picture is meant to be read large.

      What the picture shows

      Start with the biggest cluster in the “what” circle. It’s called lost between the parts, and it holds more statements than any other. The feeling isn’t rare. It’s the most common thing on the map.

      Next to it sit a few that look different but rhyme. People are waiting to be told what to do. There is too much to keep track of. And in a team of five, often one builder is doing the work while four consult.

      Now look at what’s thin. Almost nothing on the map comes from someone who came once and didn’t return. Nothing comes from employers. Those are the voices we’re missing, and the map shows that gap too, instead of hiding it.

      One honest warning: a cluster is big because more statements landed there, not because it matters more. Counting is a way to look, not a way to decide.

      Why draw it before fixing it

      It’s tempting to read a list like this and start fixing. Put up more signs. Send more reminders. Add another meeting.

      But look at how the map holds two things at once. Many people ask us to treat them like capable adults. And many say they’re waiting to be told what to do next. Both are true. Handing out more instructions makes the first worse, and handing out less makes the second worse.

      The design researcher Kees Dorst has a name for that kind of knot: a paradox. It’s two things, both true, that seem to block every easy answer. His method says that you find the paradox first, and only then look for a way through it. A map like this one is the step before: you can’t spot a paradox until you’ve seen everything people believe laid out side by side.

      That pair is just an example, not our finding. The pod is writing its real paradox today, with this map open on the table.

      Tell us what we missed

      The map is only as good as what’s on it. If you’ve been through a Cycle, helped run one, or came once and drifted away, there’s probably something here we haven’t heard.

      You don’t need to write anything polished. A sentence is enough, and we’ll leave your name off. Add it to the map and the map redraws itself.
